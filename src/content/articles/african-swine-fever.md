African swine fever (ASF) is one of the most serious diseases affecting pigs worldwide. This highly contagious viral infection can spread quickly through domestic pig herds and wild pig populations, causing severe illness, sudden deaths, and mortality rates that may approach 100% in some outbreaks.  

Although African swine fever is devastating for pigs, it is important to understand one reassuring fact: **ASF does not infect people and is not a human food-safety threat**. People can eat properly prepared pork safely, but they can unknowingly carry the virus to pigs on contaminated clothing, footwear, vehicles, equipment, or pork products.  

For pig farmers, backyard producers, veterinarians, animal owners, hunters, and travelers, prevention is the most effective defense. There is no universally available treatment, and vaccine availability varies by country. Strong biosecurity, early detection, and immediate reporting remain essential.

## What Is African Swine Fever?

African swine fever is caused by the African swine fever virus, or ASFV. It affects domestic pigs, feral pigs, wild boar, and other members of the pig family. Pigs of all ages and breeds can become infected.

The disease is not the same as classical swine fever, also called hog cholera. The two diseases can produce similar symptoms, including fever, weakness, bleeding, and death, so laboratory testing is necessary to tell them apart accurately.  

ASF can appear in several forms:

- **Peracute ASF:** Pigs may die suddenly with few visible signs.
- **Acute ASF:** High fever, severe weakness, bleeding, and rapid death are common.
- **Subacute ASF:** Signs may be less intense, but illness can last longer.
- **Chronic ASF:** Weight loss, skin problems, respiratory disease, and swollen joints may develop over weeks or months.

The severity depends on the virus strain, the amount of virus involved, the route of infection, and the health and immune status of the pig.

## Why Is African Swine Fever So Dangerous?

African swine fever is dangerous because it can spread rapidly and cause extensive losses before producers recognize what is happening. Highly virulent strains can kill most or all infected pigs, sometimes within days. Less virulent strains may produce vague signs that are easy to confuse with other pig diseases.  

An outbreak can also create major economic consequences, including:

- Loss of pigs and breeding stock
- Mandatory quarantine and movement restrictions
- Costs associated with testing, cleaning, and disinfection
- Depopulation of infected or exposed herds
- Disruption to pork processing and transportation
- Export restrictions and trade losses
- Higher pork prices or reduced supply
- Loss of income for small-scale and commercial farmers

The disease has spread across regions of Africa, Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Its continued movement is linked to infected pigs, wild boar, contaminated pork products, human travel, farm equipment, feed, vehicles, and poor waste-management practices. 

## Can African Swine Fever Affect Humans?

No. **African swine fever does not infect humans.** It is not a zoonotic disease, meaning it does not spread from pigs to people and does not cause illness in humans.  

However, people play an important role in spreading ASF between pig populations. The virus can remain on:

- Boots and shoes
- Clothing and gloves
- Vehicle tires and trailers
- Farm tools and equipment
- Livestock transport vehicles
- Hunting equipment
- Contaminated feed containers
- Raw or improperly processed pork products

For example, a person who visits an infected pig farm may carry the virus on their boots. If those boots are not cleaned and disinfected, the person could introduce ASF to another farm.

## African Swine Fever Symptoms

The signs of ASF can vary significantly. Some pigs may die suddenly, while others show illness for several days or develop chronic problems.

Common African swine fever symptoms include:

- High fever
- Loss of appetite
- Depression and weakness
- Reluctance to move
- Huddling or lying down
- Red, purple, or blotchy skin
- Skin hemorrhages or darkened areas
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea, which may be bloody
- Coughing
- Difficulty breathing
- Nasal or eye discharge
- Poor coordination
- Abortions, stillbirths, or weak piglets
- Sudden death

Red or purple discoloration may be more noticeable on the ears, snout, tail, legs, abdomen, and underside of the body. Internal bleeding may occur even when skin changes are not obvious.

A pig with these symptoms does not automatically have ASF. Other diseases, including classical swine fever, erysipelas, salmonellosis, septicemia, and respiratory infections, can cause similar signs. Do not attempt to diagnose ASF based only on appearance.

## How Does African Swine Fever Spread?

ASF spreads through direct contact, contaminated materials, infected pork, and certain tick species.

### Direct pig-to-pig transmission

Infected pigs can spread the virus through blood, saliva, nasal discharge, urine, feces, and other body fluids. Close contact in pens, transport vehicles, markets, and poorly managed housing can accelerate transmission.

Wild boar and feral pigs can also carry ASF and pass it to domestic pigs through direct contact or contaminated environments.

### Contaminated pork products

Raw, undercooked, dried, cured, smoked, or frozen pork may contain infectious virus. Feeding pigs food waste or kitchen scraps containing contaminated pork is a major risk. ASF virus can survive for long periods in meat and some processed pork products.  

Never feed pigs:

- Raw pork
- Leftover sausage or ham
- Meat scraps
- Unprocessed kitchen waste
- Carcass material
- Food waste collected from restaurants or aircraft
- Pork products of unknown origin

If a local authority allows swill feeding, follow official heat-treatment requirements exactly. When in doubt, do not feed food waste to pigs.

### People and farm equipment

People may spread ASF without becoming infected. Contaminated clothing, footwear, knives, buckets, trailers, tractors, and other equipment can move the virus between farms.

Visitors, livestock traders, slaughter workers, veterinarians, transporters, hunters, and farm employees should follow strict entry and exit procedures.

### Ticks

Soft ticks in the genus *Ornithodoros* can act as biological vectors and reservoirs in some regions. Their importance varies by location and production system. Where these ticks are present, controlling access to infested buildings and habitats is an important part of disease prevention. 

## What Should You Do If You Suspect ASF?

Treat sudden deaths or multiple sick pigs as an emergency. Early reporting can prevent the disease from spreading to neighboring farms.

If you suspect African swine fever:

1. **Stop moving pigs immediately.** Do not sell, transport, lend, or slaughter suspected pigs.
2. **Restrict access to the area.** Keep visitors, vehicles, pets, and other animals away from the pigs.
3. **Separate sick pigs only if it can be done safely** and without spreading contamination.
4. **Do not open or move carcasses** unless instructed by a veterinarian or animal health official.
5. **Contact your veterinarian immediately.**
6. **Notify the appropriate government animal health authority.**
7. **Record recent movements**, including new pigs, visitors, vehicles, feed deliveries, and animal sales.
8. **Follow official instructions for sampling, quarantine, cleaning, and disposal.**

Do not wait for more pigs to become sick. ASF can spread before the full pattern of clinical signs is obvious.

## How Is African Swine Fever Diagnosed?

Veterinarians may suspect ASF based on fever, hemorrhages, high mortality, sudden death, and epidemiological risk. However, clinical signs alone are not enough for confirmation.

Laboratory testing may include:

- Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR
- Virus detection tests
- Antigen detection
- Virus isolation
- Antibody testing
- Additional tests to distinguish ASF from classical swine fever

Samples may include blood, spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, kidney, and other tissues. Samples should be collected and transported only according to veterinary authority instructions because improper handling can expose people, animals, and the environment to contamination. 

## Is There a Treatment or Vaccine for ASF?

There is no effective treatment that cures African swine fever. Antibiotics do not kill ASFV because it is a virus. Antibiotics may be used for secondary bacterial infections under veterinary direction, but they will not stop ASF.

Vaccine availability is not uniform worldwide. Some countries have approved or evaluated specific vaccines, while international veterinary authorities continue to assess their safety, effectiveness, and appropriate use. Producers should never purchase or use unauthorized vaccines because unlicensed products may be ineffective or create additional disease-control problems.  

Where ASF is confirmed, authorities may require quarantine, movement restrictions, humane depopulation, safe carcass disposal, cleaning, disinfection, and epidemiological tracing. These measures are difficult, but rapid action reduces the risk of a larger outbreak.

## Practical African Swine Fever Prevention

The best way to protect your pigs is to build biosecurity into every daily routine.

### 1. Keep pigs away from wild and feral pigs

Use secure fencing to prevent contact with wild boar and feral swine. Outdoor pigs require extra protection because they may encounter infected animals, carcasses, soil, feces, or contaminated feed.

### 2. Control farm visitors

Maintain a visitor log and allow only essential people onto the property. Require visitors to use clean, farm-specific clothing and footwear.

Ask visitors whether they have recently been around pigs, wild boar, slaughter facilities, livestock markets, or farms in regions affected by ASF.

### 3. Clean and disinfect properly

Disinfectants work best after organic material has been removed. A reliable cleaning process includes:

- Remove manure, mud, blood, and feed debris.
- Wash surfaces with detergent.
- Rinse thoroughly.
- Apply an approved disinfectant at the correct concentration.
- Allow the required contact time.
- Let surfaces dry before reuse.

Clean and disinfect boots, loading areas, tools, trailers, feeders, and vehicles regularly.

### 4. Use dedicated clothing and footwear

Keep farm boots and coveralls on the property. Do not wear them to markets, hunting sites, other farms, or livestock auctions.

Provide handwashing stations or hand sanitizer at entry and exit points.

### 5. Quarantine new pigs

Keep new or returning pigs separate from the main herd for the period recommended by your veterinarian or animal health authority. Monitor them daily and do not share equipment between quarantine and resident pigs.

### 6. Purchase feed carefully

Buy feed from reputable suppliers and protect it from contamination by wild animals, rodents, birds, vehicles, and visitors. Store feed in closed containers or secure buildings.

### 7. Manage dead pigs safely

Do not leave carcasses where domestic pigs, wild animals, dogs, scavengers, or people can access them. Follow local regulations for removal, burial, composting, rendering, or other approved disposal methods.

### 8. Keep accurate records

Record pig purchases, sales, births, deaths, visitors, deliveries, veterinary treatments, and movements. These records can help authorities identify the source and spread of disease during an investigation.

## Is It Safe to Eat Pork?

ASF does not infect people, and properly prepared pork is safe to eat. However, pork from sick or illegally slaughtered animals should never be used as pig feed. Moving raw or improperly processed pork can spread the virus to pigs and farms.  

Always follow local food-safety regulations and purchase pork through approved channels. Never bring pork products across borders in violation of import rules.

## African Swine Fever FAQs

### Can dogs, cats, cattle, or chickens get African swine fever?

ASF naturally affects pigs and other members of the pig family. It does not infect people or typical farm animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, chickens, or dogs. However, pets and other animals may mechanically carry contaminated material on their feet or bodies.

### Can pigs recover from African swine fever?

Some pigs infected with less virulent strains may survive. Recovered pigs may remain infected or contribute to continued spread, so survival does not mean the herd is safe. Veterinary authorities must direct testing and control decisions.

### Does cooking pork kill African swine fever virus?

Adequate heat can inactivate the virus, but home preparation is not a substitute for official disease-control rules. Never feed pigs raw or undercooked pork, and do not use uncertain pork waste as feed.

### How quickly does ASF kill pigs?

Highly virulent infections may cause death within several days. Some pigs may die suddenly before obvious signs develop, while subacute and chronic infections can last much longer. The speed depends on the virus strain and the individual herd.

### Can ASF spread through the air?

Long-distance airborne spread is generally limited compared with direct contact, contaminated materials, infected pork, and animal movement. Good ventilation does not replace biosecurity.

### Should I vaccinate pigs against African swine fever?

Ask your veterinarian or animal health authority. Vaccine approval and recommendations vary by country and region. Never use an unlicensed or illegally obtained vaccine.

### Is African swine fever the same as swine flu?

No. African swine fever is caused by ASF virus and is unrelated to influenza viruses. ASF does not infect humans.

## Conclusion

African swine fever is a severe pig disease with the potential to cause sudden deaths, herd losses, trade disruptions, and long-term economic damage. While it does not threaten human health, people are often responsible for moving the virus between farms through contaminated pork, clothing, footwear, vehicles, equipment, and careless animal movements.

Your best protection is consistent biosecurity: keep pigs away from wild swine, do not feed raw pork or untreated food waste, quarantine new animals, clean and disinfect equipment, control visitors, monitor pigs daily, and report suspicious illness immediately.

When every pig owner treats prevention as a daily responsibility, early detection becomes more likely and the risk of a devastating African swine fever outbreak becomes much easier to control.
