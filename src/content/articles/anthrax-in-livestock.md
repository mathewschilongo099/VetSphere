Anthrax in livestock is a serious disease that can kill cattle, sheep, goats, horses, and other animals quickly—sometimes before you notice any warning signs. It is also a zoonotic disease, meaning infected animals can expose people to potentially life-threatening infection.

The most important rule is simple: **if an animal dies suddenly, do not open, butcher, move, or eat the carcass until a veterinarian has ruled out anthrax.** Contact your veterinarian and local animal-health authority immediately.

With rapid reporting, livestock vaccination, movement controls, and proper carcass disposal, you can greatly reduce losses and protect your family, employees, neighbors, and customers.

## What Is Anthrax in Livestock?

Anthrax is caused by the spore-forming bacterium *Bacillus anthracis*. It primarily affects grazing herbivores, including cattle, sheep, goats, buffalo, deer, antelope, and other ruminants. Horses can also become seriously ill, while pigs are generally more resistant but may develop throat, intestinal, or systemic disease.  

The bacteria produce toxins that damage blood vessels, tissues, and organs. In susceptible livestock, the illness may progress so rapidly that an animal is found dead without having appeared sick.

Anthrax spores are especially concerning because they can survive in soil for many years, and in some conditions for decades. Animals become infected most often by eating spores while grazing or consuming contaminated feed or water. Spores may be brought to the soil surface by flooding, heavy rain, erosion, drought-related soil disturbance, or deep cultivation.  

Anthrax is not usually spread by ordinary animal-to-animal contact. The main concern is environmental contamination from infected blood, fluids, carcasses, or animal products such as hides, wool, hair, bones, and meat. 

## Why Anthrax Is Dangerous on Farms and Ranches

Anthrax can cause sudden, severe losses in a herd. One animal may die without warning, followed by additional deaths among animals grazing in the same contaminated area.

The disease also creates a public-health risk. People may become infected by handling a sick or dead animal, contacting contaminated blood or tissues, or working with infected hides, hair, wool, bones, and other products. Eating meat from an infected animal can cause gastrointestinal anthrax.  

People at increased risk include:

- Livestock owners and ranch employees
- Farmers and herders
- Veterinarians and veterinary technicians
- Slaughterhouse and meat-processing workers
- Butchers and animal-product handlers
- Wildlife workers
- Anyone involved in carcass disposal

Human anthrax can occur through the skin, digestive tract, or respiratory system. Skin exposure is the most common form, usually occurring when spores enter a cut, scratch, or other break in the skin. Seek medical advice immediately after possible exposure, even if you feel well.

## Which Livestock Are Most Susceptible?

Cattle, sheep, and goats are highly susceptible to anthrax. In these animals, the disease commonly causes acute or peracute septicemia, which means the bacteria and toxins spread rapidly through the bloodstream.

Horses may develop a somewhat different illness, including fever, colic, depression, breathing difficulty, diarrhea, and swelling. Pigs are more resistant, but they can develop localized throat swelling, difficulty swallowing, enlarged lymph nodes, intestinal illness, or sudden death.  

Dogs and cats are generally more resistant, but they can become infected if they eat raw or undercooked meat from an infected animal or contact infected tissues and body fluids.

## Signs of Anthrax in Livestock

The most common sign of anthrax in cattle, sheep, and goats is **sudden death**. An animal may be grazing normally and then be found dead later the same day.

Possible signs before death include:

- High fever
- Weakness or depression
- Staggering or uncoordinated movement
- Trembling or muscle tremors
- Difficulty breathing
- Excitement followed by collapse
- Seizures or terminal convulsions
- Reduced rumination
- Reduced milk production
- Abortion in pregnant animals
- Swelling under the skin, especially around the neck, chest, shoulders, or abdomen

After death, you may notice:

- Dark, unclotted blood from the nose, mouth, anus, or other openings
- Rapid bloating and decomposition
- Little or no rigor mortis
- Blood that appears thick, dark, or tar-like

These signs are strongly suggestive but are not enough to confirm anthrax. Other causes of sudden livestock death include lightning strike, bloat, poisoning, clostridial disease, acute poisoning, hemorrhagic diseases, and other severe infections. Laboratory testing and veterinary investigation are necessary. 

## What to Do if You Suspect Anthrax

Treat a suspected anthrax death as an emergency. Take the following steps:

1. **Keep people away from the animal and the surrounding area.**
2. **Prevent livestock, dogs, wildlife, and scavengers from contacting the carcass.**
3. **Do not open, skin, cut, drag, or butcher the carcass.**
4. **Do not take hides, horns, hair, wool, bones, blood, or other tissues.**
5. **Do not sell or consume meat from the animal.**
6. **Do not move the carcass unless directed by animal-health officials.**
7. **Call your veterinarian immediately.**
8. **Report the suspected case to the appropriate animal-health authority.**

Do not wait for laboratory confirmation before making the initial call. Anthrax is a reportable disease in many jurisdictions, and rapid reporting helps authorities protect nearby herds and manage human exposure.  

If the carcass has already been opened, tell the veterinarian exactly what happened and who had contact with it. Do not attempt to clean up the area without professional guidance.

## Why You Should Never Open a Suspected Anthrax Carcass

Opening a carcass exposes the bacteria to oxygen. This can encourage the formation of highly resistant spores, which may contaminate soil, vegetation, equipment, clothing, and nearby surfaces. It can also release infected blood and fluids.

For this reason, a postmortem examination should not be performed on a carcass suspected of anthrax. Veterinarians and diagnostic laboratories use carefully selected samples and specialized procedures to reduce exposure and environmental contamination.  

If you find a bloated carcass with blood around the natural openings, do not assume it is safe to investigate. Keep the carcass intact and wait for veterinary instructions.

## How Anthrax Is Diagnosed

Anthrax diagnosis can be difficult because several diseases cause sudden death. A veterinarian will consider:

- The animal’s species and age
- Recent weather and soil conditions
- Previous anthrax cases in the area
- Vaccination history
- Grazing location
- Number of sudden deaths
- Clinical signs before death
- Appearance of the carcass

Laboratory testing may include culture, polymerase chain reaction testing, microscopic examination, or other specialized tests. The veterinarian or regulatory authority will decide which sample is safest and most appropriate. Commonly, diagnostic testing is performed using blood, tissue, or other carefully collected material.  

Do not collect samples yourself unless instructed and equipped to do so. Improper sampling can expose you to the organism and spread contamination around the farm.

## Treatment for Anthrax in Livestock

Treatment is most likely to help animals identified early, before severe bloodstream infection develops. Antibiotics such as penicillin or certain tetracyclines may be used under veterinary direction, depending on the species, condition of the animal, local regulations, and drug-label requirements.  

However, anthrax often progresses too quickly for treatment to save animals that are already severely ill. Never give leftover antibiotics without veterinary advice. Incorrect drug selection, dose, or withdrawal management can harm animals, delay proper control, and create food-safety problems.

During an outbreak, a veterinarian or animal-health authority may recommend:

- Immediate treatment of selected exposed animals
- Isolation of sick animals
- Preventive treatment for animals that may be incubating infection
- Vaccination of exposed or at-risk livestock
- Temporary movement restrictions
- Removal of animals from contaminated pasture
- Disposal of contaminated feed, bedding, and materials
- Environmental cleaning and disinfection

Antibiotics and live anthrax vaccines may not be used at the same time because antibiotics can interfere with vaccine organisms. The timing must be determined by a veterinarian. 

## Anthrax Vaccination for Livestock

Vaccination is the most effective way to prevent anthrax in livestock areas where the disease is known to occur or has occurred previously. Veterinary anthrax vaccines are available for several livestock species, but product approvals, schedules, age restrictions, and withdrawal periods vary by country and product label.

Your veterinarian can help you create a vaccination plan based on:

- Your livestock species
- Local disease history
- Pasture location
- Seasonal risk
- Wildlife activity
- Weather patterns
- Previous vaccination records
- Regulatory requirements

In endemic regions, vaccination is often scheduled before the season when anthrax outbreaks are most likely. Some authorities recommend annual vaccination of grazing livestock in affected areas.  

Keep accurate records of:

- Animal identification
- Vaccine name and lot number
- Date administered
- Dose and route
- Person administering the vaccine
- Booster due date
- Meat or milk withdrawal information

Do not assume that one vaccination permanently protects an animal. Follow your veterinarian’s schedule and the product label.

## Carcass Disposal: The Most Important Outbreak-Control Step

Improper carcass disposal can contaminate soil and create a long-term source of infection. The preferred disposal method is usually controlled incineration or burning under the direction of veterinary and environmental authorities. Deep burial may be used when burning is not possible, but local regulations and groundwater conditions must be considered.  

Never dispose of a suspected anthrax carcass through ordinary deadstock collection unless authorities specifically approve the method. Transporting a contaminated carcass can spread infected fluids and spores to roads, equipment, vehicles, and new premises.

During disposal:

- Use trained personnel.
- Wear appropriate protective clothing, gloves, boots, and other recommended personal protective equipment.
- Keep scavengers and insects away.
- Avoid dragging or opening the carcass.
- Prevent blood and body fluids from entering soil or waterways.
- Decontaminate equipment and footwear according to official instructions.
- Record the disposal location and follow-up requirements.

The exact disposal method should always be coordinated with your veterinarian and animal-health authority because regulations differ by location.

## Quarantine and Biosecurity During an Outbreak

Once anthrax is suspected or confirmed, authorities may quarantine the affected property. Quarantine may restrict movement of livestock, carcasses, hides, wool, meat, equipment, feed, and other potentially contaminated materials.

Do not move animals to market, slaughter, another pasture, or a neighboring farm unless officials authorize the movement. Movement can spread exposure beyond the original property and make disease control more difficult.

Good outbreak biosecurity includes:

- Restricting access to affected pastures
- Separating sick animals from healthy animals when safe to do so
- Moving healthy animals away from contaminated sites only with veterinary guidance
- Disinfecting boots, tools, trailers, and equipment
- Controlling flies and scavengers
- Removing potentially contaminated feed
- Keeping detailed animal-movement records
- Monitoring all exposed livestock several times daily
- Reporting new illness or deaths immediately

Quarantine is generally not lifted until required carcass disposal, vaccination, monitoring, and decontamination steps are completed. 

## Protecting Yourself and Your Workers

Do not handle a suspected anthrax carcass with bare hands. At a minimum, people involved in approved response activities may need gloves, coveralls, long sleeves, pants, washable boots, eye protection, and respiratory protection based on the task and official risk assessment.

Cover cuts and abrasions before working around livestock. Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water, change contaminated clothing, and keep work clothing separate from household laundry.

Contact a healthcare professional immediately if you:

- Handled a suspected carcass
- Had blood or body fluid contact with your skin or eyes
- Had contact through a cut or scratch
- Inhaled dust or contaminated material
- Ate meat from an animal that died suddenly
- Developed a skin sore, fever, swelling, vomiting, abdominal pain, or breathing problems after exposure

Public-health officials may recommend monitoring, antibiotics, or other preventive treatment after a significant exposure. 

## Can You Eat Meat From an Animal That Died Suddenly?

No. Never eat, sell, donate, or process meat from livestock that died suddenly or from an unexplained illness.

Cooking does not make an unknown carcass safe. In documented outbreaks, handling a carcass was associated with skin anthrax, while eating contaminated meat was associated with gastrointestinal anthrax.  

Only meat from animals inspected and approved through the legally required slaughter process should enter the food supply.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Anthrax in Livestock

### Is anthrax contagious from one animal to another?

Anthrax is not usually spread through ordinary contact between live animals. Most livestock become infected by ingesting spores from contaminated soil, feed, or water. However, infected blood, body fluids, carcasses, and animal products can contaminate the environment and expose other animals or people. 

### What is the first sign of anthrax in cattle?

The first sign is often sudden death with little or no observed illness. Before death, cattle may have fever, weakness, staggering, breathing difficulty, trembling, or collapse.

### Should I open a dead animal to look for anthrax?

No. Opening a suspected carcass can expose bacteria to oxygen, encourage spore formation, and spread contamination. Call your veterinarian and leave the carcass undisturbed.

### Can anthrax be treated in livestock?

Early antibiotic treatment may help animals that are identified before severe illness develops. Treatment decisions must be made by a veterinarian because anthrax can progress rapidly and medication timing may affect vaccination and food-animal withdrawal requirements.

### Is there an anthrax vaccine for cattle, sheep, and goats?

Vaccines are available for several livestock species and are widely used in areas where anthrax occurs. Your veterinarian should select the appropriate product and schedule for your animals and location.

### Can humans get anthrax from livestock?

Yes. People can become infected through contact with contaminated animals, carcasses, blood, tissues, hides, wool, hair, bones, or meat. Human exposure should be reported to a healthcare provider or public-health authority promptly.

### How long can anthrax spores survive in soil?

Anthrax spores are highly resistant and can survive in suitable soil for many years, potentially decades. This is why vaccination, careful carcass disposal, and pasture-management planning are essential after an outbreak. 

## Final Takeaway

Anthrax in livestock requires immediate action. Sudden death—especially when accompanied by blood from natural openings, bloating, or a lack of rigor mortis—should be treated as a warning sign.

Protect your operation by following three priorities: **report suspicious deaths quickly, never open or consume a suspect carcass, and maintain veterinarian-guided vaccination and biosecurity plans.** Proper response can prevent additional livestock deaths, reduce long-term soil contamination, and protect the people who work with your animals.
