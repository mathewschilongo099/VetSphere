Avian influenza in poultry, commonly called bird flu, is one of the most serious infectious disease threats facing commercial farms, backyard flocks, and wild birds. Some forms cause little or no illness, while highly pathogenic avian influenza can spread rapidly, cause sudden deaths, and result in severe losses across an entire flock.  

Whether you raise chickens, turkeys, ducks, geese, quail, pheasants, or other domestic birds, understanding avian influenza is essential. Early recognition, strict biosecurity, and prompt reporting can help protect your birds, neighboring flocks, farm workers, and the wider poultry industry.

This guide explains what avian influenza is, how it spreads, the signs to watch for, how veterinarians diagnose it, and the practical steps you can take to reduce the risk.

## What Is Avian Influenza in Poultry?

Avian influenza is caused by influenza A viruses that infect birds. The viruses can affect domestic poultry, wild birds, captive birds, and, in some circumstances, mammals and people. Wild waterfowl and shorebirds are important natural hosts and may carry or shed the virus without appearing sick.  

Avian influenza viruses are classified in several ways, including by their hemagglutinin and neuraminidase surface proteins. You may hear terms such as H5N1, H5N2, H7N9, or H9N2 when discussing specific virus strains.

For poultry health, the most important classification is pathogenicity:

- **Low pathogenicity avian influenza**, or LPAI
- **Highly pathogenic avian influenza**, or HPAI

“Pathogenicity” describes the ability of a virus strain to cause disease in poultry. It does not necessarily predict how severe the virus will be in every bird species or in people.

## LPAI vs. HPAI: What Is the Difference?

### Low Pathogenicity Avian Influenza

LPAI infections may cause no obvious signs, particularly in wild birds. In domestic poultry, LPAI can cause mild respiratory disease, reduced appetite, decreased egg production, or diarrhea. Some H5 and H7 LPAI viruses are closely monitored because they can, under certain circumstances, mutate into highly pathogenic forms in poultry.  

Even when LPAI causes mild disease, it should not be ignored. Infected birds can spread the virus to other birds, and an outbreak may lead to movement restrictions, trade consequences, testing requirements, and production losses.

### Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza

HPAI can cause severe, systemic disease involving multiple organs. Chickens, turkeys, and other gallinaceous poultry may become seriously ill or die quickly. In some peracute cases, birds may die with few or no visible warning signs.  

HPAI is highly contagious. Once introduced into a flock, the virus may spread through direct bird-to-bird contact and through contaminated equipment, clothing, footwear, vehicles, litter, manure, crates, egg flats, feed, and water.

## How Avian Influenza Spreads Through Poultry Flocks

Avian influenza can enter a farm or backyard flock in several ways. Understanding these transmission routes helps you identify and close biosecurity gaps.

### Contact With Wild Birds

Wild ducks, geese, gulls, shorebirds, and other birds may carry avian influenza viruses. They can contaminate poultry areas through droppings, saliva, respiratory secretions, feathers, or contaminated water.

Outdoor poultry are at increased risk when they share space with wild birds or have access to ponds, wetlands, streams, puddles, or untreated surface water.

### Contaminated Equipment and Materials

The virus can move between premises on:

- Boots and clothing
- Vehicle tires and wheel wells
- Poultry crates and transport cages
- Egg trays and flats
- Shovels, buckets, and tools
- Feed and water containers
- Manure, litter, and feathers
- Shared equipment
- Hands and gloves

A person does not need to touch an infected bird to spread the virus. Walking through contaminated soil or litter can be enough to carry infectious material to another poultry house.

### Movement of Birds

Introducing new birds, returning birds from shows, buying birds from live markets, or accepting unwanted poultry can introduce infection. A bird may appear healthy while incubating disease or shedding virus.

Do not assume that a healthy appearance means a bird is free of avian influenza.

### People and Farm Traffic

Visitors, farm workers, service providers, feed deliveries, egg collection teams, veterinarians, hunters, and poultry traders can all create pathways for disease spread if appropriate precautions are not followed.

## Clinical Signs of Avian Influenza in Poultry

Signs vary according to the virus strain, bird species, age, immune status, and whether other infections are present. LPAI may cause subtle problems, while HPAI may result in sudden flock mortality.

Watch for:

- Sudden death without previous illness
- An unusual increase in flock deaths
- Loss of appetite
- Decreased feed or water consumption
- Depression, weakness, or lethargy
- Ruffled feathers
- Coughing, sneezing, or gasping
- Nasal or oral discharge
- Swollen eyelids, head, comb, wattles, or hocks
- Purple or bluish discoloration of the comb, wattles, or legs
- Soft-shelled, misshapen, or fewer eggs
- Diarrhea, sometimes greenish
- Poor coordination or inability to stand
- Tremors, twisted necks, paralysis, or drooping wings

USDA APHIS lists sudden death, reduced appetite, egg-production changes, swelling, purple discoloration, respiratory signs, incoordination, and diarrhea among the signs associated with avian influenza.  

In some flocks, a decline in water or feed consumption may be noticed before obvious clinical signs. Monitor daily production records and compare them with normal flock performance.

## Why Sudden Death Requires Immediate Action

Sudden deaths are not always caused by avian influenza. Other possibilities include heat stress, poisoning, Newcastle disease, fowl cholera, infectious bronchitis, trauma, or other serious conditions.

However, sudden unexplained mortality should always be treated as an emergency disease concern. Do not wait several days to see whether the problem resolves, and do not move birds, eggs, manure, equipment, or carcasses off the property until you receive veterinary or regulatory advice.

Clinical signs alone cannot confirm avian influenza. Laboratory testing is required. 

## What to Do if You Suspect Avian Influenza

If one or more birds show suspicious signs, take immediate precautions:

1. **Stop moving birds.** Do not sell, give away, transport, or take birds to markets, shows, auctions, or exhibitions.
2. **Restrict access.** Keep visitors, pets, vehicles, and unnecessary workers away from the flock.
3. **Separate sick birds if advised.** Isolation may help limit contact, but use dedicated equipment and clothing.
4. **Do not handle dead or sick birds unnecessarily.**
5. **Contact a veterinarian or animal health authority immediately.**
6. **Report unusual illness or deaths.** In the United States, USDA APHIS advises owners to report suspected disease at **1-866-536-7593** or through their state veterinarian.  
7. **Follow official instructions.** Authorities may recommend testing, quarantine, depopulation, cleaning, disinfection, and restrictions on movement.

Do not send samples on your own unless directed by a veterinarian or official animal health laboratory. Improper handling can expose people and spread contaminated material.

## How Veterinarians Diagnose Avian Influenza

A veterinarian may evaluate the flock’s history, clinical signs, mortality pattern, production records, housing, recent bird movements, wild bird exposure, and biosecurity procedures.

Laboratory diagnosis may involve:

- Reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction, or RT-PCR
- Detection of influenza A viral RNA
- Virus isolation
- Antibody testing
- Additional testing to identify the virus subtype and pathogenicity

Samples may be collected from oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs, respiratory material, or internal organs during postmortem examination.  

Because avian influenza can resemble other poultry diseases, testing is essential. A respiratory outbreak or reduced egg production does not automatically mean that a flock has AI.

## Is There a Treatment for Avian Influenza?

There is no routine flock treatment that eliminates HPAI. Antiviral drugs are not approved or generally recommended for treating poultry flocks. Antibiotics do not treat viruses, although a veterinarian may use them in limited circumstances to manage secondary bacterial infections associated with some LPAI cases.  

When HPAI is confirmed, government authorities may require humane depopulation of infected and exposed birds. This difficult measure is used to reduce the amount of virus on the premises and prevent spread to other flocks.

Response procedures may include:

- Quarantine
- Testing and surveillance
- Movement restrictions
- Humane depopulation
- Carcass disposal
- Cleaning and disinfection
- Environmental sampling
- A waiting period before restocking
- Follow-up testing and biosecurity evaluation

Rules vary by country, state, and disease classification. Always follow the instructions of your local animal health authority.

## Biosecurity: The Best Protection for Your Poultry

Biosecurity means the measures you use to prevent disease from entering or leaving your flock. It is the most important day-to-day defense against avian influenza.

### 1. Keep Poultry Away From Wild Birds

Whenever possible, house poultry in covered enclosures that prevent contact with wild birds and their droppings. Repair holes in roofs, walls, and fencing.

Keep poultry away from ponds and other shared water sources. Provide clean drinking water in containers that wild birds and other animals cannot access.

### 2. Protect Feed and Water

Store feed in sealed containers. Clean spills promptly, and prevent wild birds, rodents, cats, and other animals from accessing feed or water.

Contaminated feed and water can become important exposure routes, particularly when domestic poultry and wild birds use the same areas. 

### 3. Use Dedicated Clothing and Footwear

Wear boots and clothing reserved for poultry work. Keep them on the property when possible.

Wash hands before and after handling birds. Clean and disinfect equipment regularly, but remember that disinfectants work best after visible manure, dirt, and organic material have been removed.

### 4. Limit Visitors

Post restricted-access signs and maintain a visitor log for commercial facilities. Do not allow unnecessary visitors into poultry housing areas.

Anyone who must enter should use clean or disposable protective clothing and footwear. Avoid visiting other poultry premises on the same day.

### 5. Quarantine New Birds

Purchase birds from reputable sources with appropriate health documentation. Keep new or returning birds separate from the established flock and monitor them carefully before introduction.

Quarantine is not a substitute for testing or veterinary advice, but it reduces the chance of immediately exposing the entire flock.

### 6. Avoid Mixing Species

Keeping chickens, ducks, geese, turkeys, and other species together can complicate disease control. Ducks and geese may carry avian influenza with few signs, potentially exposing more susceptible poultry.

Separate species and age groups whenever practical. Commercial farms may also use all-in/all-out production, allowing facilities to be cleaned and disinfected between flocks. 

### 7. Control Rodents and Pests

Rodents and other animals can move contaminated material around a property. Seal openings, remove spilled feed, manage vegetation, and maintain clean storage areas.

### 8. Keep Records

Record daily mortality, feed intake, water intake, egg production, and unusual clinical signs. Good records help you identify changes early and give your veterinarian useful information.

## Protecting Poultry Workers and Families

People can rarely become infected with avian influenza viruses after close, prolonged, and unprotected contact with infected animals or contaminated environments. The risk is higher for poultry workers, veterinarians, responders, and people handling sick or dead birds.  

If contact with potentially infected birds is unavoidable:

- Wear appropriate personal protective equipment.
- Use respiratory and eye protection as directed.
- Wear gloves, coveralls, and dedicated boots.
- Avoid touching your eyes, nose, or mouth.
- Wash hands thoroughly after removing protective equipment.
- Change clothing and footwear before entering your home.
- Monitor for symptoms after exposure and contact a healthcare provider if illness develops.

Avoid handling sick or dead wild birds with bare hands. Contact local wildlife or animal health authorities for instructions.

The current public health risk may be low for the general public, but risk assessments can change as viruses evolve and new animal or human cases are identified. 

## Avian Influenza and Food Safety

Poultry and eggs from regulated commercial supply chains are monitored through official animal health and food safety systems. Birds from confirmed infected flocks should not enter the food chain.

As a general food safety precaution:

- Cook poultry and eggs thoroughly.
- Use a food thermometer.
- Cook poultry and egg dishes to an internal temperature of **165°F (74°C)**.
- Wash hands after handling raw poultry.
- Clean cutting boards, knives, counters, and utensils.
- Keep raw poultry separate from ready-to-eat foods.

Cooking poultry and eggs properly kills viruses, including avian influenza viruses, as well as many common foodborne bacteria. 

## Are Avian Influenza Vaccines Available for Poultry?

Vaccination may be used in some countries or under specific control programs, but it is not a universal replacement for biosecurity, surveillance, movement controls, and official outbreak response.

Vaccines must be appropriately matched to circulating viruses and used under veterinary or regulatory supervision. Vaccination policies can affect surveillance, trade, movement, and reporting requirements. WOAH and FAO emphasize that vaccination, where appropriate, should be part of a broader prevention and control strategy.  

Never vaccinate a flock for avian influenza without consulting your veterinarian and animal health authority.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can healthy-looking poultry carry avian influenza?

Yes. Some LPAI infections cause few or no obvious signs, and certain wild birds can shed virus while appearing healthy. This is why biosecurity and surveillance are important even when your flock looks normal. 

### Can backyard chickens get avian influenza?

Yes. Backyard flocks can become infected through contact with wild birds, contaminated equipment, people, vehicles, feed, water, or newly introduced poultry. Small flock owners should use the same basic biosecurity principles as commercial producers.

### Should I treat sick poultry with antibiotics?

Do not self-medicate a flock. Antibiotics do not kill avian influenza viruses and may delay proper diagnosis. Contact a veterinarian or animal health authority first.

### Should I isolate a sick bird?

If safe and practical, separating a sick bird may reduce direct contact with healthy birds. Use dedicated equipment and clothing, and seek veterinary advice promptly. Isolation does not replace official testing or reporting.

### Can humans catch avian influenza from poultry?

Human infections are uncommon but can occur, especially after close, prolonged, unprotected contact with infected birds or contaminated environments. Use appropriate PPE and avoid handling sick or dead birds whenever possible. 

### Are cooked chicken and eggs safe to eat?

Properly handled and thoroughly cooked poultry and eggs are considered safe from avian influenza viruses. Cook poultry and egg dishes to 165°F (74°C), and prevent cross-contamination in the kitchen. 

### What is the first sign of HPAI in a flock?

Sudden unexplained death may be the first and sometimes only obvious sign. Other early indicators include reduced feed or water intake, a sharp drop in egg production, respiratory disease, weakness, swelling, purple discoloration, or neurological signs.

## Conclusion

Avian influenza in poultry is a serious, fast-moving disease, but consistent prevention can significantly reduce your risk. Keep domestic birds away from wild birds, control farm traffic, protect feed and water, use dedicated clothing and equipment, quarantine new birds, and monitor your flock every day.

If you notice sudden deaths, unusual illness, respiratory signs, or a sudden decline in egg production, act immediately. Stop bird movement, protect people from exposure, contact a veterinarian or animal health authority, and report the suspected disease. Early action is one of the most effective tools for protecting your flock and limiting the spread of avian influenza.
