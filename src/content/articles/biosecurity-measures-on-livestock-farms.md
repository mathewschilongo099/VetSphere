Livestock disease outbreaks can spread quickly—and the consequences may be serious. Illness can reduce growth, fertility, milk production, and egg production while increasing veterinary costs, mortality, labor demands, and trade restrictions.

The good news is that effective farm biosecurity does not always require expensive technology or complicated systems. It depends on consistent daily actions that prevent disease from entering your farm, reduce spread between animals, and limit movement between farms. These actions include controlling visitors, quarantining new livestock, cleaning equipment, protecting feed and water, managing pests, and monitoring animal health.

Whether you operate a dairy farm, beef ranch, pig farm, sheep or goat operation, poultry farm, or mixed livestock property, a written biosecurity plan can help protect your animals and your livelihood.

## What Is Biosecurity on a Livestock Farm?

Biosecurity is the collection of practices used to prevent infectious diseases from entering, spreading within, or leaving a livestock operation. It includes animal management, sanitation, disease surveillance, traffic control, vaccination, pest control, and record keeping.

A complete livestock biosecurity program has three main goals:

1. **Keep disease away from your farm.**
2. **Stop disease from spreading between animals and groups.**
3. **Prevent infected animals, equipment, and materials from spreading disease elsewhere.**

Disease-causing organisms can move through direct animal contact, manure, saliva, urine, milk, blood, respiratory droplets, contaminated feed, water, clothing, footwear, tools, vehicles, insects, rodents, birds, and wildlife.

Your biosecurity plan should therefore consider every person, animal, vehicle, product, and piece of equipment that crosses onto or around your farm.

## Why Farm Biosecurity Matters

A disease outbreak can affect more than animal health. It can disrupt your entire operation.

Potential consequences include:

- Animal deaths and euthanasia
- Reduced milk, meat, egg, or wool production
- Slow growth and poor feed conversion
- Reproductive losses
- Increased medication and veterinary expenses
- Quarantine and animal movement restrictions
- Loss of market access
- Increased risk of zoonotic disease
- Long cleaning and recovery periods
- Damage to customer and community confidence

Strong biosecurity also supports animal welfare. Healthy animals are more likely to eat, grow, reproduce, and perform normally. Preventing disease is generally more effective and less costly than trying to control an outbreak after it begins.

## 1. Create a Written Farm Biosecurity Plan

The first step is to create a written, farm-specific biosecurity plan. A plan should reflect your species, farm layout, production system, local disease risks, and normal animal and vehicle movements.

Your plan should identify:

- All livestock species and age groups on the property
- Barns, pens, pastures, isolation areas, and loading zones
- The main entrance and approved visitor parking area
- Clean and dirty areas
- High-risk traffic routes
- Cleaning and disinfection stations
- Feed and water sources
- Manure and carcass disposal locations
- Pest control procedures
- Emergency contacts
- Disease reporting procedures
- Staff responsibilities and training requirements

Walk through your farm and identify where contamination is most likely to occur. Pay special attention to shared gates, handling facilities, feed alleys, loading areas, water points, equipment sheds, and locations where visitors or delivery vehicles enter.

Your veterinarian can help you identify disease risks and develop vaccination, testing, isolation, and treatment protocols appropriate for your operation.

## 2. Control Visitors and Farm Traffic

People and vehicles can carry infectious organisms on boots, clothing, hands, tires, wheel wells, tools, and equipment. Limiting unnecessary traffic is one of the simplest and most effective biosecurity measures.

Use a single controlled entrance when possible. Post signs instructing visitors to stop, call, and register before entering animal areas. Keep a visitor log that records names, contact details, date of entry, reason for the visit, and recent contact with other livestock farms.

Practical visitor controls include:

- Allow only essential visitors into animal areas.
- Provide clean farm-specific coveralls and boots.
- Require visitors to wash their hands before and after animal contact.
- Keep personal vehicles away from livestock buildings.
- Use designated visitor parking.
- Require service providers to follow your written farm procedures.
- Ask visitors whether they recently visited another livestock facility.
- Delay visits when a person has been exposed to a disease outbreak.
- Accompany unfamiliar visitors while they are on the farm.

During a suspected or confirmed outbreak, stop all nonessential visits. Essential visitors, such as veterinarians and animal health officials, should follow enhanced protective clothing, movement, and cleaning procedures.

## 3. Establish Clean and Dirty Zones

A clean-and-dirty system helps prevent contamination from moving into animal areas.

The **dirty zone** may include public roads, visitor parking, delivery areas, manure storage, and vehicle access points. The **clean zone** includes animal housing, feed storage, milking areas, hatcheries, and other controlled production spaces.

Mark the boundary clearly with signs, gates, fencing, ropes, or physical barriers. Everyone should understand where they must change footwear, wash hands, put on protective clothing, or clean equipment before crossing.

Avoid moving from dirty areas into clean areas without completing the required steps. Keep delivery drivers, livestock haulers, garbage trucks, and rendering vehicles outside the clean zone whenever possible.

## 4. Quarantine New and Returning Animals

New animals are a common source of disease introduction. Even animals that look healthy may be incubating an infection or carrying organisms without obvious signs.

Purchase livestock only from reputable sources with reliable health records. Ask for information about:

- Vaccination history
- Disease testing
- Previous illnesses
- Treatment records
- Herd or flock health status
- Recent movement history
- Transport conditions

Keep new, borrowed, returning, or show animals separate from the resident herd or flock. Many farm biosecurity programs recommend an isolation period of approximately 21 to 30 days, although the appropriate period depends on the species, disease risk, testing program, and veterinarian’s advice. Some operations may require longer periods.

The quarantine area should have separate:

- Feed and water equipment
- Boots and coveralls
- Handling tools
- Treatment supplies
- Cleaning equipment
- Caretakers, where possible

Care for healthy and highly susceptible animals first, quarantined animals afterward, and sick animals last. If separate staff are not available, clean and change clothing and footwear before returning to the main herd.

Isolation and quarantine are not the same. Quarantine is generally used for animals of unknown or potentially different health status. Isolation is used for animals showing signs of illness or animals known to have been exposed.

## 5. Separate Sick Animals Immediately

Observe your livestock every day. Early recognition of illness allows you to act before disease spreads through the herd or flock.

Possible warning signs include:

- Coughing, sneezing, or nasal discharge
- Diarrhea or abnormal manure
- Loss of appetite
- Depression or unusual behavior
- Lameness
- Fever
- Sudden drop in milk or egg production
- Poor growth
- Reproductive problems
- Unexplained deaths
- Neurological signs
- Abnormal breathing
- Increased mortality in young animals

Move sick animals to a designated isolation area as soon as practical. Use dedicated equipment and protective clothing. Do not carry shared buckets, halters, needles, grooming tools, or cleaning equipment from the sick pen to healthy animals.

Contact your veterinarian promptly when illness is severe, unusual, rapidly spreading, or associated with sudden death. Do not sell, move, or lend potentially infected animals without professional advice.

## 6. Use Effective Cleaning and Disinfection Procedures

Disinfection cannot replace cleaning. Manure, mud, bedding, feed, and other organic material can protect pathogens and reduce the effectiveness of disinfectants.

Use this basic cleaning and disinfection sequence:

1. Remove animals from the area.
2. Remove manure, bedding, feed, and visible debris.
3. Dry-clean surfaces using scrapers, brushes, or other suitable tools.
4. Wash with water and an appropriate detergent.
5. Rinse away dirt and detergent.
6. Allow surfaces to dry when possible.
7. Apply a disinfectant approved for the intended use.
8. Follow the product label for concentration, temperature, and contact time.
9. Rinse animal-contact surfaces when required by the label.
10. Allow the area and equipment to dry before animals return.

Clean from the least contaminated area toward the most contaminated area. Avoid creating spray or runoff that could contaminate nearby pens, feed, water, or animals.

Footbaths are useful only when maintained correctly. Remove visible manure from boots before dipping them, use the correct disinfectant concentration, provide adequate contact time, and replace the solution when dirty or according to the product label. A dirty footbath can spread contamination rather than remove it.

## 7. Clean Vehicles, Trailers, and Equipment

Vehicles and equipment can move pathogens between farms and between groups of animals on the same farm.

Before equipment enters an animal area:

- Remove visible dirt and manure.
- Wash with detergent and water.
- Rinse thoroughly.
- Apply a suitable disinfectant.
- Allow the required contact time.
- Let the equipment dry.

Clean tires, wheel wells, loading ramps, trailer floors, gates, and vehicle interiors where contamination may occur. Livestock trailers should be cleaned and disinfected before hauling another load.

Avoid borrowing equipment from other farms. If sharing is unavoidable, clean and disinfect items before they arrive and after they leave. Use separate equipment for feed handling, manure handling, sick animals, and healthy animals whenever possible.

Animal-contact equipment—including needles, syringes, dehorners, castration tools, halters, ropes, hoof trimmers, and treatment instruments—requires special attention. Never reuse disposable needles, and follow veterinary instructions for cleaning reusable instruments.

## 8. Protect Feed and Water

Contaminated feed and water can expose large numbers of animals at once. Buy feed and feed ingredients from reputable suppliers with quality control programs.

Store feed:

- In sealed bins or secure buildings
- Away from manure and deadstock areas
- Off the ground when appropriate
- Where birds, rodents, pets, and wildlife cannot reach it
- Protected from moisture, flooding, and spoilage

Clean up spilled feed immediately. Do not allow feed delivery equipment, manure equipment, or dirty boots to contaminate feed storage and feeding areas.

Provide clean, reliable drinking water. Clean troughs, bowls, drinkers, and water lines regularly. Avoid untreated surface water when it may be contaminated by manure, wildlife, sewage, or agricultural runoff.

Do not feed livestock unsafe food waste, untreated swill, raw animal products, or materials prohibited by local regulations. Always follow species-specific feed safety requirements.

## 9. Control Rodents, Insects, Birds, and Wildlife

Pests and wildlife can carry disease organisms on their bodies or spread them through feces, urine, bites, feed contamination, and direct contact.

An integrated pest management program may include:

- Sealing holes, cracks, and gaps in buildings
- Repairing broken doors, roofs, fences, and screens
- Keeping feed in rodent-proof containers
- Removing spilled feed daily
- Cutting weeds and overgrown vegetation
- Eliminating standing water
- Managing manure and waste
- Using traps or approved bait stations
- Preventing bird nesting in animal buildings
- Keeping dogs and cats away from feed and livestock areas
- Monitoring for droppings, tracks, nests, gnaw marks, and carcasses

Work with a qualified pest control professional when necessary. Use products according to their labels and keep pesticides away from animals, feed, water, and children.

## 10. Follow Vaccination and Preventive Health Programs

Vaccination is not a substitute for biosecurity, but it can reduce disease severity, shedding, and production losses.

Work with your veterinarian to create a preventive health program that may include:

- Core and risk-based vaccines
- Parasite control
- Reproductive health management
- Routine testing
- New-animal screening
- Nutritional support
- Calf, lamb, kid, piglet, or chick management
- Colostrum protocols where relevant

Store vaccines at the temperature recommended by the manufacturer. Record product names, lot numbers, dates, animal identification, dose, route, and person administering the vaccine.

Do not use antibiotics routinely as a replacement for hygiene or disease prevention. Use medications under veterinary direction and follow label instructions, withdrawal periods, and local regulations.

## 11. Manage Manure, Carcasses, and Waste Safely

Manure, bedding, afterbirth, milk, tissues, and carcasses can contain infectious organisms. Handle them in a way that prevents contact with healthy animals, feed, water, equipment, and neighboring farms.

Your waste management plan should cover:

- Daily manure removal where appropriate
- Safe manure storage
- Separation of manure tools from feed tools
- Cleaning and disinfection of contaminated equipment
- Prompt removal of dead animals
- Approved carcass disposal
- Protection from scavengers and wildlife
- Safe handling of abortion materials and afterbirth

Follow local requirements for composting, rendering, burial, incineration, or collection. Contact your veterinarian or animal health authority if an animal dies unexpectedly or if multiple animals die over a short period.

## 12. Train Employees and Keep Accurate Records

Biosecurity works only when everyone follows the same procedures. Train employees, family members, contractors, and seasonal workers before they handle animals.

Training should cover:

- How diseases spread
- Visitor entry procedures
- Handwashing and clothing changes
- Footwear cleaning
- Animal movement
- Sick-animal reporting
- Cleaning and disinfection
- Safe medication use
- Waste handling
- Emergency response

Keep records of:

- Animal purchases and sales
- New-animal quarantine
- Animal movements
- Visitor and vehicle entry
- Vaccinations
- Treatments
- Disease testing
- Mortality
- Cleaning and disinfection
- Pest control
- Feed and water concerns

Good records help you identify patterns, trace possible exposure, demonstrate health status, and respond quickly during an outbreak.

## 13. Use All-In, All-Out Management Where Practical

All-in, all-out production means keeping groups of animals together according to age or production stage, moving the entire group at the same time, and cleaning the facility before restocking.

This approach reduces mixing between age groups and provides an opportunity to:

- Remove organic material
- Wash and disinfect housing
- Repair damaged surfaces
- Allow downtime
- Check ventilation and drainage
- Review records before the next group arrives

If all-in, all-out management is not possible, manage each group as a separate health unit. Avoid unnecessary mixing and use dedicated staff, equipment, and clothing whenever practical.

## 14. Prepare for a Suspected Disease Outbreak

Every farm should have an emergency response plan. The plan should explain exactly what to do if a serious or reportable disease is suspected.

Include:

- Your veterinarian’s phone number
- Local animal health authority contacts
- Instructions for stopping animal movement
- A list of essential visitors
- Isolation locations
- Protective equipment supplies
- Disinfection procedures
- Carcass and waste protocols
- Employee communication procedures
- Records that must be preserved

If you suspect a serious infectious disease, isolate affected animals, restrict movement, stop unnecessary visitors, and contact your veterinarian or animal health authority immediately. Do not transport potentially infected animals or contaminated equipment without instructions.

## Livestock Farm Biosecurity Checklist

Use this quick checklist to review your operation:

- Is there a written biosecurity plan?
- Is the farm entrance clearly marked?
- Are visitors required to sign in?
- Is visitor parking away from animal areas?
- Do new and returning animals undergo quarantine?
- Is there a separate sick-animal area?
- Are healthy animals handled before sick animals?
- Are boots, coveralls, and equipment dedicated to the farm?
- Are vehicles and trailers cleaned before entry?
- Are feed and water protected from pests and manure?
- Are rodents, flies, birds, and wildlife controlled?
- Are vaccines and treatments recorded?
- Are animal movements documented?
- Are dead animals removed safely?
- Do employees know how to report illness?
- Is the plan reviewed and updated regularly?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most important biosecurity measure on a livestock farm?

There is no single measure that prevents every disease. The strongest protection comes from combining access control, animal quarantine, routine cleaning and disinfection, pest control, health monitoring, vaccination, and accurate records.

### How long should new livestock be quarantined?

A common recommendation is approximately 21 to 30 days, but the correct period depends on the species, disease risk, testing plan, and veterinarian’s advice. Some diseases require longer isolation.

### Can a footbath replace cleaning boots?

No. Manure and organic material should be removed from boots before disinfection. The footbath must contain the correct disinfectant concentration, remain clean, and provide the required contact time.

### Should sick animals be treated before healthy animals?

Generally, healthy and more vulnerable animals should be handled first. Sick or quarantined animals should be handled last, followed by cleaning and disinfection of clothing, footwear, and equipment.

### Can different livestock species share the same facilities?

They can, but mixing species may increase disease risk. Keep species separated when possible, especially when they share feed, water, housing, equipment, or handling areas. Discuss species-specific risks with your veterinarian.

### How often should a biosecurity plan be reviewed?

Review the plan at least annually and whenever you add animals, buildings, employees, species, suppliers, or production activities. Review it immediately after a disease event or a major change in regional disease risk.

### What should I do if I suspect a serious disease outbreak?

Immediately separate affected animals, restrict visitors and animal movement, preserve relevant records, increase protective measures, and contact your veterinarian or animal health authority. Do not move potentially infected animals without professional direction.

## Conclusion

Effective livestock farm biosecurity is built through consistent everyday habits. Restricting visitors, quarantining new animals, separating sick livestock, cleaning equipment, protecting feed and water, controlling pests, monitoring health, and keeping accurate records can significantly reduce disease risk.

Your biosecurity plan should be practical enough for employees to follow every day and specific enough to guide the farm during an emergency. Start by identifying your highest-risk pathways, correct the most important weaknesses, train your team, and review the plan regularly.

Healthy livestock support productivity, profitability, welfare, and food safety. By making biosecurity part of normal farm management, you protect your animals today and strengthen the future of your operation.
