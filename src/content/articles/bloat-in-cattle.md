Bloat in cattle is a serious digestive emergency that can become fatal in a short time. It occurs when gas produced during normal rumen fermentation becomes trapped inside the rumen and cannot be released through belching, also called eructation.

As gas accumulates, the rumen expands—usually causing visible swelling on the upper left side of the abdomen. The enlarged rumen can press against the diaphragm, lungs, heart, and major blood vessels. Without rapid treatment, a severely bloated cow may collapse and die from respiratory or circulatory failure.  

The good news is that thoughtful pasture management, careful feeding practices, daily observation, and a veterinary emergency plan can significantly reduce the risk. This guide explains the types of cattle bloat, common causes, warning signs, treatment options, and practical prevention strategies.

## What Is Bloat in Cattle?

Bloat, also known as ruminal tympany, is an abnormal buildup of fermentation gas in the rumen and reticulum. Healthy cattle regularly produce gas as microbes break down feed. That gas normally separates from the rumen contents and leaves the body when the animal belches.

Bloat develops when the gas cannot escape. There are two primary forms:

- **Frothy bloat**, also called primary ruminal tympany
- **Free-gas bloat**, also called secondary ruminal tympany

The two types may look similar from the outside, but they have different causes and may require different treatments.

## Frothy Bloat in Cattle

Frothy bloat occurs when fermentation gases become trapped in a stable foam within the rumen. Instead of forming larger gas pockets that can be belched out, thousands of tiny bubbles remain mixed with rumen contents.

This type of bloat is commonly associated with rapidly growing, highly digestible forage. Risky plants may contain high levels of soluble protein, fine particles, saponins, or other compounds that encourage foam formation.

Common pasture-related triggers include:

- Alfalfa
- White clover
- Red clover
- Ladino clover
- Young, lush legume-dominant pasture
- Succulent wheat, rye, or other small-grain forage
- Wet forage after rain, dew, or irrigation
- Sudden access to abundant, high-quality pasture

Frothy bloat may also occur in feedlot cattle consuming high-concentrate diets, particularly when the ration contains finely ground grain. Rapid fermentation and fine feed particles can promote foam and interfere with normal gas release.  

Pasture bloat can affect several animals in the same group. It may develop soon after turnout, but cattle can also bloat during the second or third day—or later—after entering a high-risk pasture.

## Free-Gas Bloat in Cattle

Free-gas bloat develops when gas collects as a separate pocket above the rumen contents but cannot escape. It is often caused by a physical or functional problem that prevents normal eructation.

Possible causes include:

- Choke or esophageal obstruction
- A potato, apple, turnip, or other object lodged in the esophagus
- Vagal indigestion
- Damage or pressure involving the vagus nerve
- Rumen atony after grain overload or acidosis
- Diaphragmatic hernia
- Hypocalcemia
- Abnormal posture or recumbency
- Esophageal narrowing or outside pressure on the esophagus

Free-gas bloat is often seen in an individual animal rather than throughout an entire group. If an obstruction is involved, saliva, water, or feed may come from the nose or mouth. An animal with choke and bloat requires immediate veterinary attention because the obstruction prevents gas from escaping. 

## What Causes Bloat in Cattle?

Bloat is usually caused by several factors working together rather than one isolated problem. Diet, pasture maturity, weather, animal behavior, rumen health, and individual susceptibility can all influence risk.

### Sudden pasture changes

Cattle are more likely to bloat when moved quickly from dry forage or mature grass onto lush legumes. Hungry cattle may consume large quantities rapidly, increasing the amount of high-risk forage entering the rumen.

### High-legume pastures

Alfalfa and clover are valuable feed sources, but immature plants may be highly digestible and rich in soluble protein. If the pasture contains little grass or other structural fiber, cattle may be at greater risk of frothy bloat.

### Finely ground grain

Finely ground grain ferments quickly and may contribute to frothy or free-gas bloat. It can also trigger acidosis, which damages the rumen and reduces normal rumen movement.

### Inconsistent feed intake

Long periods without feed followed by rapid consumption can increase the risk of digestive upset. Feedlot cattle should have consistent access to a properly balanced ration and adequate roughage.

### Wet weather and plant stress

Dew, rain, irrigation, frost, drought recovery, and sudden weather changes may increase the risk of pasture bloat. Wet plants can be consumed quickly and may have a different fermentation pattern in the rumen. 

### Individual susceptibility

Some cattle appear more prone to bloat than others. Genetic factors, grazing behavior, rumen function, and previous digestive problems may contribute. Animals that repeatedly develop bloat should be evaluated with your veterinarian and may need to be removed from high-risk grazing situations.

## Signs and Symptoms of Bloat in Cattle

Early recognition is essential. A cow can progress from mild swelling to severe respiratory distress quickly, so do not wait for the animal to collapse before taking action.

Common early signs include:

- Swelling of the upper left abdomen
- A left flank that changes from concave to rounded or protruding
- Stopping grazing or eating
- Reluctance to move
- Restlessness
- Kicking or stomping at the belly
- Looking back at the flank
- Frequent urination or defecation
- Stretching the neck
- Increased breathing rate

As bloat worsens, you may notice:

- Severe abdominal distension
- Labored or rapid breathing
- Open-mouth breathing
- Tongue protrusion
- Grunting
- Bulging eyes
- Staggering
- Weakness
- Repeatedly lying down and getting up
- Collapse

A cow that is down, gasping, or unable to breathe normally is in critical danger. Severe bloat may cause death within an hour in some cases, although progression varies. 

## Is Bloat in Cattle an Emergency?

Yes. Treat suspected bloat as an emergency.

The expanding rumen can compress the diaphragm and prevent the lungs from expanding. It can also interfere with blood flow, causing weakness, collapse, and death. A bloated animal should never be left for observation without intervention.

Contact your veterinarian immediately, especially if the animal has:

- Marked left-side swelling
- Breathing difficulty
- Open-mouth breathing
- A protruding tongue
- Staggering or weakness
- Choke symptoms
- Collapse
- No rapid improvement after initial treatment

If several cattle are affected, move the group away from the suspected pasture or feed source while obtaining veterinary advice. Inspect the entire group for early signs.

## How Is Bloat Diagnosed?

Diagnosis is often based on the animal’s history and visible signs. The left side of the abdomen is examined for distension, and the veterinarian may listen to rumen movements and tap the abdominal wall to assess the type of gas accumulation.

A stomach tube may help distinguish the two major types:

- **Free gas bloat:** Gas may escape rapidly through the tube, and the abdomen may begin to shrink.
- **Frothy bloat:** Little gas escapes, and foam or frothy rumen contents may be present in the tube.

A stomach tube does not always resolve frothy bloat because the foam prevents gas from forming a pocket and leaving the rumen. The veterinarian will also investigate underlying causes such as choke, grain overload, acidosis, vagal disease, or hypocalcemia. 

## Treatment for Bloat in Cattle

Treatment depends on the severity and type of bloat. Your veterinarian should help you create a farm-specific emergency protocol before cattle enter high-risk pasture.

### Stomach tube

A large-bore stomach tube may relieve free-gas bloat by allowing trapped gas to escape. It may also provide a route for delivering an antifoaming product in frothy bloat.

Only trained personnel should pass a stomach tube. Incorrect placement can injure the animal or allow fluids to enter the lungs.

### Antifoaming products

Frothy bloat requires an agent that breaks down the foam. Products may contain poloxalene, mineral oil, vegetable oil, or another approved surfactant.

Merck Veterinary Manual lists poloxalene as an effective treatment for legume bloat and notes that certain vegetable or mineral oils may also reduce foam stability. Always follow the product label, local regulations, withdrawal periods, and veterinary instructions. 

### Do not force a drench into a distressed animal

Do not force liquids into a severely bloated cow that cannot swallow normally. A distressed or recumbent animal may inhale the fluid, leading to aspiration pneumonia or sudden death.

In many cases, an antifoaming product is safer and more effective when delivered through a correctly placed stomach tube or under veterinary supervision. 

### Trocarization or emergency rumenotomy

If the animal is in immediate danger and a stomach tube does not provide relief, a veterinarian may use a trocar and cannula to decompress the rumen. In life-threatening cases, emergency rumenotomy may be necessary.

These procedures can save a life, but they carry risks such as infection, leakage of rumen contents, peritonitis, and wound complications. They should be performed only by a veterinarian or a person specifically trained and authorized to do so under an established emergency plan. Follow-up cleaning, treatment, and monitoring are essential. 

## How to Prevent Bloat in Cattle

No single prevention method works in every herd. The most effective programs combine pasture management, gradual diet changes, adequate fiber, observation, and approved preventive products.

### 1. Provide hay before turnout

Offer good-quality grass hay or another suitable dry forage before turning cattle onto lush legume pasture. This helps reduce hunger, slow pasture intake, and increase the amount of structural fiber in the rumen.

Merck Veterinary Manual recommends that hay make up at least one-third of the diet to meaningfully reduce pasture-bloat risk. 

### 2. Introduce pasture gradually

Do not turn hungry cattle directly onto a lush alfalfa or clover field. Introduce the pasture in stages and continue providing lower-risk forage during the transition.

Gradual adaptation gives the rumen microbial population time to adjust and reduces the chance of rapid overconsumption.

### 3. Manage the grass-to-legume ratio

Mixed grass-legume pastures generally pose less risk than pastures dominated by immature legumes. Maintain adequate grass in the stand and avoid allowing cattle to selectively consume only the highest-risk plants.

Strip grazing or smaller paddocks can help control intake, but cattle must still be observed carefully.

### 4. Avoid grazing wet, high-risk forage

Use caution when pasture is covered with heavy dew, rain, or irrigation water. Delay turnout when conditions are known to increase intake or bloat risk, particularly during rapid spring growth or after weather-related plant changes.

### 5. Use approved anti-bloat products

Poloxalene may be provided through feed, mineral, liquid supplements, blocks, or pasture applications. It must be consumed in an adequate amount every day to work reliably.

Self-fed products may not protect every animal equally because intake varies. Monitor access and use the product according to its label and your veterinarian’s advice. 

### 6. Consider ionophores when appropriate

Ionophores such as monensin or lasalocid may help reduce bloat risk and improve feed efficiency when used in approved cattle feeds. They are not a replacement for sound pasture and ration management.

Never use an ionophore outside the product label. Accidental overdosing can be toxic, so accurate mixing and even distribution are essential.

### 7. Add roughage to feedlot rations

Finishing rations should contain adequate chopped or cut roughage. Avoid sudden increases in grain, finely ground feed, or abrupt ration changes.

Merck Veterinary Manual recommends at least 10% to 15% cut or chopped roughage in feedlot rations and advises using rolled or cracked grain instead of finely ground grain. 

### 8. Monitor cattle frequently

Observe cattle closely during the first week after turnout, after weather changes, and whenever feed or pasture changes. Early morning and other high-risk grazing periods may require additional checks.

Keep emergency supplies accessible, including:

- A suitable cattle handling facility
- A properly sized stomach tube
- Approved antifoaming products
- Contact information for your veterinarian
- Emergency instructions for farm employees

## Frequently Asked Questions About Bloat in Cattle

### 1. What does a bloated cow look like?

The upper left side of the abdomen becomes swollen and may bulge outward behind the ribs. The cow may stop grazing, appear restless, kick at the belly, breathe rapidly, or extend its neck.

### 2. Can cattle die from bloat?

Yes. Severe bloat can compress the lungs and interfere with blood circulation. Death may occur rapidly, especially when the animal develops open-mouth breathing, staggering, or collapse. 

### 3. What is the difference between frothy bloat and free-gas bloat?

Frothy bloat traps fermentation gas in a stable foam and is commonly linked to lush legumes or finely ground grain. Free-gas bloat involves a separate gas pocket that cannot escape because of choke, impaired rumen movement, or another obstruction.

### 4. Will a stomach tube cure all cases of bloat?

No. A stomach tube may relieve free-gas bloat, but it may not release gas trapped in stable foam. Frothy bloat often requires an antifoaming agent delivered through a properly placed tube.

### 5. Can I give mineral oil to a bloated cow?

Some mineral and vegetable oils may be used as antifoaming agents, but the correct product, amount, and delivery method matter. Do not force a drench into an animal that cannot swallow safely. Contact your veterinarian for instructions.

### 6. How can I prevent pasture bloat?

Provide hay before turnout, introduce cattle gradually, maintain mixed grass-legume pasture, avoid sudden access to lush wet forage, use approved anti-bloat products when appropriate, and inspect cattle frequently.

### 7. Should I remove a cow that repeatedly bloats?

Repeated bloat is a management and welfare concern. Have the animal examined by a veterinarian, investigate the diet and pasture, and consider removing chronically affected cattle from high-risk grazing areas.

## Conclusion

Bloat in cattle is preventable in many situations, but it must always be treated as a medical emergency when signs appear. The most recognizable warning sign is swelling high on the left side, followed by discomfort, reduced grazing, rapid breathing, and progressive respiratory distress.

Successful bloat management starts before cattle enter a risky pasture or receive a new ration. Provide adequate fiber, make diet changes gradually, control access to lush legumes, use approved preventive products correctly, and train everyone on your farm to recognize the early signs.

Most importantly, have a veterinary treatment plan ready. When a cow is severely bloated, every minute matters.
