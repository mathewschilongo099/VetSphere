# Bovine Respiratory Disease: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Bovine Respiratory Disease (BRD) is one of the most common and costly health problems in cattle worldwide, affecting beef and dairy animals of all ages, especially young calves and feedlot cattle.  This complex respiratory syndrome leads to pneumonia, reduced growth, higher treatment costs and, in severe cases, death. BRD is sometimes called “shipping fever” because it often occurs after transport and other stressful events.  Understanding the causes, symptoms of Bovine Respiratory Disease, diagnosis, treatment and prevention is essential for farmers, students and anyone caring for cattle.

## What is Bovine Respiratory Disease?

Bovine Respiratory Disease is not a single disease but a complex (often called the BRD complex) caused by a combination of stress, viral infections and bacterial infections that damage the lungs and airways of cattle.  Under normal conditions, many of the viruses and bacteria associated with BRD live quietly in the respiratory tract without causing illness. When cattle are stressed or their immunity is weakened, these organisms can invade the lungs and cause serious pneumonia.  

BRD is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in beef feedlots and a major cause of death in beef calves from three weeks of age to weaning.  It is also a significant problem in dairy calves and can affect adult cattle, especially in intensive systems. Economic losses come not only from deaths but also from reduced average daily gain, poor feed conversion and lower carcass value.  

From a practical standpoint, Bovine Respiratory Disease usually presents as fever, depression, coughing, nasal discharge and breathing difficulty in calves or feedlot animals, often within the first 30–45 days after arrival or after a stressful event such as weaning or long-distance transport.  Because BRD develops quickly and early treatment greatly improves outcomes, regular observation and rapid response are critical on any cattle operation.

## Causes of Bovine Respiratory Disease

The causes of Bovine Respiratory Disease are multifactorial. The disease results from a complex interaction between pathogens (viruses and bacteria), the animal’s immune system, and environmental and management stressors.  This is why outbreaks can vary greatly between farms and why “one size fits all” prevention rarely works.

Key viruses commonly associated with BRD include infectious bovine rhinotracheitis virus (IBR), bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), bovine respiratory syncytial virus (BRSV), and parainfluenza-3 virus (PI3).  Important bacterial pathogens include Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni and Mycoplasma bovis.  Often, a viral infection damages the respiratory tract first, lowering local defenses and allowing opportunistic bacteria to invade the lungs and cause fibrinous or necrotic bronchopneumonia.  

Stress is a major driver of BRD. Critical stressors include weaning, long-distance transport, commingling cattle from multiple sources, auction market handling, sudden diet changes, high-energy rations, dust, temperature extremes, humidity, overcrowding, and painful procedures such as castration or dehorning done around the same time as shipping.  Poor colostrum intake and underfeeding in young calves, as well as inadequate ventilation and poor air quality in barns or feedlots, also increase risk.  

Because the causes of Bovine Respiratory Disease involve both infectious agents and management risk factors, control programs must address all three: the pathogens (through vaccination and appropriate antimicrobial use), the host (through good nutrition and immunity) and the environment (through low-stress handling, biosecurity and housing). 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Bovine Respiratory Disease

The clinical signs and symptoms of Bovine Respiratory Disease range from mild to severe and can vary with age, pathogen mix and stage of disease. Early detection is essential, because cattle are prey animals and tend to hide illness until they are quite sick.  Daily observation of pens and groups is therefore a cornerstone of BRD management.

Common early symptoms of Bovine Respiratory Disease include reduced feed intake, depression, drooping ears, lowered head carriage and separation from the group.  As disease progresses, visible respiratory signs appear: coughing, increased respiratory rate, labored breathing, and serous to mucopurulent nasal discharge. Fever (rectal temperature above about 103.5°F or 39.7°C) is a typical finding, especially in acute cases.  Eye discharge, rough hair coat and dehydration may also be observed.

Severely affected cattle may show marked respiratory distress with open-mouth breathing, extended neck, grunting, and sometimes subcutaneous emphysema over the neck and shoulders.  In advanced cases, animals may become recumbent and die if not treated promptly. Some cattle develop chronic pneumonia with poor growth, weight loss and a “poor doer” appearance after surviving an acute episode.  Subclinical BRD, where lung lesions are present at slaughter without obvious clinical signs during life, can also significantly reduce growth and carcass performance.  

Because many of these signs are non-specific and can resemble other conditions, careful observation, temperature checking and veterinary input are important when assessing suspect animals. Visual scoring systems that combine posture, behavior, nasal and eye discharge, cough, and respiratory effort are increasingly used to standardize BRD detection in feedlots and calf units. 

## How to Diagnose Bovine Respiratory Disease

Diagnosis of Bovine Respiratory Disease on-farm usually starts with clinical assessment. Pen riders, stockpeople or farmers identify animals with depression, reduced feed intake, abnormal posture, nasal discharge, cough and fever, and classify them as BRD suspects.  In many commercial settings, treatment decisions must be made quickly, so initial diagnosis is often based on clinical signs plus rectal temperature rather than laboratory tests.  

More advanced diagnostic approaches are available and are especially useful in outbreaks, high-value animals, or when current control measures are failing. Veterinarians may collect nasal swabs, deep nasopharyngeal swabs, tracheal washes or bronchoalveolar lavage samples to identify the viruses and bacteria involved using culture, PCR or other laboratory methods.  Serology can help determine exposure patterns at the herd level. Necropsy of recently dead animals, with detailed examination of lungs and airways, remains the gold standard for confirming BRD and characterizing lung lesions.  

Recent research has explored new diagnostic tools such as blood metabolomics, automated behavior monitoring, remote temperature sensing and clinical scoring apps to detect BRD earlier and more accurately.  These technologies aim to improve sensitivity for subclinical or early disease that might be missed by visual inspection alone. Regardless of the methods used, working closely with a veterinarian to design a herd-specific diagnostic and monitoring plan is vital.

Because BRD has multiple causes and risk factors, diagnosis should also include review of management practices: source and history of purchased cattle, timing of weaning and transport, vaccination status, nutrition, pen design, ventilation, stocking density and handling procedures.  Identifying and correcting these underlying contributors is as important as identifying the pathogens.

## Treatment of Bovine Respiratory Disease

How to treat Bovine Respiratory Disease depends on disease severity, type of animals affected, previous treatments and farm protocols, but timely antimicrobial therapy is central in most clinical cases. Broad-spectrum antibiotics labeled for BRD, particularly macrolides and phenicols, are commonly used as first-line treatments in feedlot and calf operations.  Other classes such as fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines and cephalosporins are also used under veterinary guidance, depending on regulations and resistance concerns.  

Early treatment of sick animals, ideally at the first signs of depression, fever and mild respiratory signs, greatly improves prognosis and reduces lung damage.  Many feedlots use rectal temperature thresholds and clinical scores to decide when to treat. Some high-risk groups may receive metaphylaxis (mass medication of a group with a BRD-labeled antibiotic) at arrival to reduce disease incidence when there is strong evidence of high risk.  Selective metaphylaxis based on risk or temperature can reduce antimicrobial use while still controlling BRD.  

Supportive therapy is also important. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as meloxicam or flunixin, can reduce fever and inflammation, improve comfort and help cattle maintain appetite.  Good nursing care—easy access to fresh water and palatable feed, protection from extreme weather, minimal handling stress and appropriate bedding—supports recovery. Severely affected animals may require individual care in hospital pens, with close monitoring of response to treatment and consideration of treatment failure criteria for re-treatment or culling.  

Judicious use of antibiotics is critical to slow antimicrobial resistance. Veterinary guidelines recommend using products labeled for BRD, following dose and duration instructions, keeping accurate treatment records, and avoiding unnecessary repeat treatments or off-label combinations unless clearly justified.  Work with your veterinarian to develop a written BRD treatment protocol that fits your operation and complies with local regulations.

## Prevention and Control of Bovine Respiratory Disease

The prevention of Bovine Respiratory Disease relies on an integrated approach that reduces stress, enhances immunity and controls pathogen exposure. No single measure is sufficient on its own; instead, a coordinated program tailored to the farm is needed.  

Stress reduction is a major pillar. Key strategies include weaning calves at least 2–3 weeks before shipment (“preconditioning”), performing castration and dehorning well before transport, minimizing time spent in auction markets, reducing transport duration and providing rest with feed and water on long journeys.  Cattle should be assembled quickly into stable groups, and mixing animals from multiple sources should be minimized where possible.  In feedlots and barns, good ventilation, reduced dust, appropriate stocking density, and protection from temperature extremes help maintain respiratory health.  

Vaccination is another core tool in prevention of Bovine Respiratory Disease. Evidence supports the use of vaccines against key viruses (IBR, BVDV, BRSV, PI3) and bacteria such as Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida and Histophilus somni in reducing BRD incidence and severity when used appropriately.  Vaccination programs should be designed with veterinary guidance, considering local pathogen patterns, timing (preconditioning vs arrival), and type of vaccine (modified-live vs killed). Vaccines are not a “silver bullet” but are powerful when combined with good management and stress control.  

Additional control measures include good colostrum management and nutrition in young calves, biosecurity to prevent introduction of new pathogens, and careful sourcing of replacement or feeder cattle from farms with known health status and preconditioning practices.  Regular monitoring of morbidity, mortality, treatment response and slaughter lung lesions provides feedback on how well the BRD control program is working and where it can be improved. 

## Frequently Asked Questions About Bovine Respiratory Disease

### Q: What is Bovine Respiratory Disease and why is it important?

**A:** Bovine Respiratory Disease is a multifactorial respiratory illness in cattle caused by a combination of stress, viruses and bacteria that damage the lungs and airways.  It is the leading cause of illness and death in feedlot cattle and a major health issue in calves, resulting in large economic losses through death, reduced growth, poor feed efficiency and increased treatment costs. 

### Q: What are the main causes of Bovine Respiratory Disease?

**A:** The causes of Bovine Respiratory Disease include viral infections (such as IBR, BVDV, BRSV and PI3), bacterial infections (including Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni and Mycoplasma bovis), and management stressors like transport, weaning, commingling, poor ventilation and sudden diet changes.  These factors interact, with stress and viruses often weakening defenses so bacteria can invade the lungs.

### Q: What are common symptoms of Bovine Respiratory Disease?

**A:** Typical symptoms of Bovine Respiratory Disease include reduced feed intake, depression, drooping ears, nasal discharge, coughing, elevated temperature, and increased respiratory rate or effort.  Severely affected animals may show open-mouth breathing, extended neck, severe respiratory distress and sometimes subcutaneous emphysema over the neck and shoulders.  Chronic cases may appear as poor doers with reduced growth and rough hair coats. 

### Q: How do you treat Bovine Respiratory Disease?

**A:** How to treat Bovine Respiratory Disease typically involves early administration of an appropriate broad-spectrum antibiotic labeled for BRD, such as macrolides or phenicols, under veterinary guidance.  Supportive care, including NSAIDs for pain and fever relief, good hydration, palatable feed and stress reduction, improves recovery.  In high-risk groups, metaphylactic treatment at arrival may be used to reduce disease incidence when justified. 

### Q: How can Bovine Respiratory Disease be prevented on my farm?

**A:** Prevention of Bovine Respiratory Disease focuses on stress reduction (preconditioning, low-stress handling, good transport practices), vaccination against key respiratory viruses and bacteria, good colostrum and nutrition, and improved housing and ventilation.  Working with a veterinarian to design a tailored vaccination and management program is the most effective approach.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Cattle owners should call a veterinarian promptly when they notice clusters of animals with fever, cough, nasal discharge, depression or increased respiratory effort, especially in newly arrived or recently weaned groups.  Early involvement of a vet is critical when death losses begin to rise, when animals fail to respond to initial treatments, or when recurring outbreaks occur despite existing control measures.  

Veterinarians can help confirm the diagnosis of Bovine Respiratory Disease, collect appropriate samples for laboratory testing, and perform necropsies on dead animals to identify the pathogens involved and the severity of lung lesions.  They can also review management practices, vaccination protocols, and treatment records to identify risk factors and design a comprehensive prevention and control plan specific to the farm or feedlot.  

In addition, veterinarians play a key role in ensuring judicious antimicrobial use. They can recommend appropriate antibiotic choices, dosages, durations and treatment thresholds, helping to maintain drug effectiveness while still protecting animal health and farm profitability.  Regular veterinary review of BRD performance indicators (morbidity, mortality, relapse rates, lung lesions at slaughter) allows for continuous improvement in herd health programs.

## Conclusion

Bovine Respiratory Disease is a complex, multi-causal respiratory syndrome and remains one of the most significant health and economic challenges in cattle production worldwide.  It arises from the interaction of stress, infectious agents and management practices, and expresses itself through a spectrum of clinical signs ranging from mild depression and cough to severe, fatal pneumonia.  Understanding the causes of Bovine Respiratory Disease, recognizing early symptoms, and knowing how to treat Bovine Respiratory Disease quickly are essential skills for farmers, students and all who work with cattle.

Effective control of BRD depends on a balanced program that combines good husbandry and low-stress handling, appropriate vaccination, timely and judicious antimicrobial treatment, and continuous monitoring of health outcomes.  Because each herd and production system is different, working closely with a veterinarian to design and regularly update a BRD prevention and treatment plan is the best way to protect animal welfare and maintain productivity.

By focusing on prevention of Bovine Respiratory Disease through better management, enhanced immunity and environmental improvements, cattle producers can markedly reduce disease incidence, improve growth and carcass performance, and lower the overall costs associated with this major respiratory threat.
