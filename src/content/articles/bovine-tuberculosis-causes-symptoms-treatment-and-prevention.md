# Bovine Tuberculosis: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Bovine Tuberculosis is a serious chronic disease of cattle that also threatens other animals, farm workers, and consumers of unpasteurized milk. It is caused mainly by the bacterium Mycobacterium bovis and can spread slowly but relentlessly through a herd if not carefully controlled.  Because Bovine Tuberculosis affects animal welfare, farm income, and public health, every cattle owner should understand its causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment options, and prevention strategies.

This article explains Bovine Tuberculosis in clear, practical language for farmers, students, and animal owners. You will learn the common causes of Bovine Tuberculosis, the key symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle, how veterinarians diagnose it, what “treatment” really means in the context of a regulated disease, and the most effective prevention and control measures used worldwide. 

---

## What is Bovine Tuberculosis?

Bovine Tuberculosis (often shortened to bTB) is a contagious, slowly progressive bacterial disease that mainly affects cattle but can infect many other mammals, including humans, deer, badgers, wild boar, goats, pigs, dogs, and cats.  The primary cause is Mycobacterium bovis, a close relative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes human tuberculosis.  

The infection usually develops over months to years. Many cattle show no obvious signs for a long time, even though they may already be infected and capable of spreading the bacteria through their breath, milk, feces, or other secretions.  This long, silent phase makes the disease difficult to detect based only on clinical signs and is a major reason why regular testing programs and slaughterhouse surveillance are so important.  

Bovine Tuberculosis is considered a zoonotic disease, which means it can pass from animals to humans. Historically, people often became infected by drinking raw, unpasteurized milk from infected cows. Modern milk pasteurization in many countries has greatly reduced this risk, but in regions where pasteurization is not universal, Mycobacterium bovis still causes a noticeable share of human tuberculosis cases.

## Causes of Bovine Tuberculosis

The fundamental cause of Bovine Tuberculosis is infection with Mycobacterium bovis, a slow-growing bacterium that can survive inside body cells and in the environment for long periods.  It belongs to the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, a group of closely related species that cause tuberculosis in different animal hosts.  

Transmission usually occurs in one of three main ways:

1. **Inhalation of infected droplets**  
   Infected cattle excrete Mycobacterium bovis in tiny droplets from the lungs when they cough or breathe heavily. Close contact in sheds, during transport, or at feeding and watering areas allows nearby animals to inhale the bacteria and become infected.  

2. **Ingestion of contaminated feed, water, or milk**  
   Cattle can pick up the bacteria when they eat or drink material contaminated with saliva, nasal discharge, feces, urine, or pus from infected animals.  Calves may be infected by drinking milk from a cow with Bovine Tuberculosis affecting the udder.  People can also be infected by drinking raw milk or eating products made from unpasteurized milk.  

3. **Contact with infected wildlife or other species**  
   In some countries, wildlife reservoirs (such as badgers, deer, wild boar, possums, and certain other wild mammals) maintain Mycobacterium bovis in the environment and can pass it back to cattle.  Many other domestic and exotic species, including goats, pigs, cats, dogs, buffalo, and zoo animals, can also become infected and act as spillover hosts.  

Risk factors for Bovine Tuberculosis include:

- High stocking density and overcrowded housing  
- Poor ventilation in barns and sheds  
- Mixed grazing or nose-to-nose contact with infected wildlife or neighboring herds  
- Sharing water sources or feed with infected animals  
- Feeding unpasteurized waste milk to calves  
- Lack of, or poor compliance with, regular testing and culling programs

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis

The symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle are often subtle and develop slowly. Many infected animals appear healthy for months or years and are only detected at routine testing or slaughter inspection.  However, as the disease progresses, especially when the lungs or other major organs are heavily affected, clinical signs may appear.

Common clinical signs and symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis include:

1. **Chronic, persistent cough**  
   A soft, frequent cough, especially during exercise or in cold air, is a typical sign when the lungs are involved.  The cough may be mild at first and easily overlooked in a herd.

2. **Progressive weight loss and poor body condition**  
   Infected cattle may gradually lose weight despite normal or even increased appetite. They can appear “poor doers,” with rough coats and declining milk yield.  This weight loss is often one of the earliest noticeable signs for farmers.

3. **Reduced appetite, lethargy, and decreased production**  
   As the disease advances, animals may become dull, weak, and show decreased feed intake and reduced milk production, which directly affects farm profitability.  

4. **Enlarged lymph nodes**  
   Swollen lymph nodes, especially in the head and neck (for example, those behind the jaw) or in front of the shoulder, may be felt or seen. In some cases, the lymph nodes may drain pus or form visible lumps.  

5. **Respiratory distress in advanced cases**  
   Severe, long-standing lung infection can cause rapid breathing, labored breathing (dyspnea), nasal discharge, and more obvious coughing fits.  

6. **Digestive or udder involvement**  
   If Bovine Tuberculosis affects the intestines, liver, or other abdominal organs, cattle may develop chronic diarrhea or show signs of digestive upset. Infection in the udder can lead to abnormal milk and a risk of spreading the bacteria to calves and humans through raw milk.  

It is important to remember that many infected animals show no outward symptoms at all. This is why relying on clinical signs alone is unsafe; regular herd-level testing is essential for early detection.

## How to Diagnose Bovine Tuberculosis

Because symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis are often vague or absent, laboratory and field tests are crucial. Veterinarians and animal health authorities use several approaches to diagnose Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle and monitor herds.

### 1. Tuberculin Skin Tests

The most widely used field test is the tuberculin skin test, which measures the animal’s immune response to purified protein derivatives (PPD) from Mycobacterium bovis (and sometimes Mycobacterium avium).  

Key versions include:

- **Single intradermal tuberculin test (SITT)** or **single intradermal test (SIT)** – A small volume of bovine tuberculin is injected into the skin (commonly in the neck or near the base of the tail). The skin thickness is measured at injection and again about 72 hours later. A significant swelling indicates a positive reaction. 

- **Single intradermal comparative cervical test (SICCT) / SCITT** – Both bovine and avian PPDs are injected at separate sites in the neck. Comparing the reactions helps distinguish true Mycobacterium bovis infections from cross-reactions caused by other mycobacteria, such as environmental species or Mycobacterium avium paratuberculosis.  

These tests are central to national eradication and control programs and are often mandatory in many countries. 

### 2. Blood Tests (Interferon-Gamma Assays and Serology)

Blood-based tests are increasingly used to complement skin testing:

- **Interferon-gamma release assays (e.g., BOVIGAM)** measure the release of interferon-gamma from blood cells exposed to tuberculin antigens. They can sometimes detect infections earlier than skin tests and are useful in high-risk or heavily infected herds. 

- **Antibody tests (serology)**, including tests on serum or bulk tank milk, can help identify infected animals or herd exposure, especially in later stages when antibody levels are higher. 

### 3. Post-Mortem Examination and Laboratory Confirmation

Many cases of Bovine Tuberculosis are discovered during slaughter inspection. Meat inspectors or veterinary surgeons look for characteristic lesions (tubercles) in lymph nodes, lungs, and other organs.  

Suspicious tissues are then tested in the laboratory by:

- **Microscopic examination and special staining** to look for acid-fast bacteria  
- **Culture of Mycobacterium bovis**, which is slow but definitive  
- **PCR and other molecular methods** to detect and identify the organism quickly  

These methods confirm infection, help trace outbreaks, and contribute to monitoring the success of control programs.

## Treatment of Bovine Tuberculosis

When farmers ask how to treat Bovine Tuberculosis, it is important to understand that managing this disease is very different from treating many other bacterial infections. In most countries with formal control programs, individual treatment of cattle with tuberculosis drugs is either legally prohibited or strongly discouraged for several reasons.

### Why Individual Treatment is Not Practiced

- **Public health risk**: Treated animals may still harbor bacteria and continue to pose a zoonotic risk through milk or meat, especially if treatment is incomplete or not fully effective. 
- **Drug resistance**: Long courses of human anti-tuberculosis drugs (such as isoniazid, rifampicin, etc.) would be required. Incomplete or improper treatment in cattle could encourage the development of drug-resistant bacteria that threaten both animal and human health. 
- **Practical and economic limitations**: Effective TB treatment is lengthy, costly, and difficult to manage in large animals and herds. Withdrawal times for meat and milk would be extremely long or impossible to define safely.

Because of these concerns, the standard “treatment” strategy for Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle is:

- **Test-and-slaughter (test-and-cull)**: Regular testing of the herd (skin tests and/or blood tests) and prompt removal (slaughter under official control) of animals that test positive or are classified as reactors. 
- **Movement controls and quarantine**: Restricting movement of animals from infected herds until they are cleared by repeated tests, to prevent spread to other farms. 

### Supportive Care in Non-Control Settings

In countries without strict TB control regulations, some animals might be kept and managed rather than culled, especially in smallholder systems. In those circumstances, supportive management focuses on:

- Improving nutrition and housing to support general health  
- Separating suspicious or sick animals from the rest of the herd  
- Avoiding consumption of raw milk or undercooked meat from potentially infected animals  

However, from a veterinary public health standpoint, this approach is far less safe than test-and-slaughter and does not eliminate the infection.

## Prevention and Control of Bovine Tuberculosis

Prevention of Bovine Tuberculosis is more effective and practical than trying to treat infected cattle. Most successful strategies combine herd-level biosecurity, regular testing, strict culling of reactor animals, careful slaughterhouse surveillance, and in some regions, management of wildlife reservoirs.

### Herd-Level Prevention

Important farm-level measures include:

1. **Regular testing and rapid removal of reactors**  
   Participating in mandatory or voluntary testing programs (skin tests and, if recommended, blood tests) allows early detection and removal of infected animals before they spread infection widely.  

2. **Good biosecurity and herd management**  
   - Avoid introducing animals from unknown or high-risk herds; buy only from herds with known TB status.  
   - Quarantine newly purchased animals and test them where regulations or risk justify it.  
   - Improve ventilation in barns and avoid overcrowding.  
   - Prevent contact between your herd and neighboring cattle at boundaries, shared water, or communal grazing.  

3. **Safe milk and meat practices**  
   - Pasteurize all milk intended for human consumption; this step almost completely eliminates the risk of transmitting Mycobacterium bovis through dairy products. 
   - Do not feed unpasteurized waste milk from suspect cows to calves.  
   - Follow meat inspection regulations, which protect the public from carcasses with TB lesions. 

### Wildlife and Environmental Control

In regions where wildlife species such as badgers, deer, wild boar, or possums act as reservoirs, control of Bovine Tuberculosis is more difficult. Measures may include:

- Reducing direct and indirect contact between cattle and wildlife (fencing, secure feed stores, covered troughs)  
- Targeted management or vaccination strategies for wildlife populations where policies and science support them 

### Vaccination

Bovine TB vaccines, such as BCG (Bacillus Calmette–Guérin) used in humans, can protect cattle to some extent. However, their widespread use in cattle is limited, mainly because vaccinated animals often test positive on traditional tuberculin tests, complicating control programs that rely on test-and-slaughter.  Research is ongoing into DIVA (Differentiate Infected from Vaccinated Animals) tests, which could allow vaccination while still identifying naturally infected animals. 

### National and Regional Control Programs

Many developed countries have dramatically reduced or even eradicated Bovine Tuberculosis through long-term control programs that combine:

- Routine herd testing  
- Compulsory slaughter of reactors  
- Movement restrictions  
- Slaughterhouse surveillance  
- Public health measures like compulsory milk pasteurization  

These programs are costly and require strong cooperation between farmers, veterinarians, and government authorities, but they significantly reduce economic losses and protect public health.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Bovine Tuberculosis

### Q: What causes Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle?

**A:** Bovine Tuberculosis is caused primarily by the bacterium Mycobacterium bovis, a member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. Cattle usually become infected by inhaling bacteria from the breath of infected animals or by ingesting contaminated feed, water, or milk. Wildlife reservoirs and other domestic species can also spread the bacteria to cattle. 

### Q: What are the main symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis?

**A:** Many infected cattle show no obvious signs for a long time. When symptoms do appear, they often include chronic coughing, progressive weight loss and poor body condition, reduced appetite and milk yield, swollen lymph nodes, and in advanced cases, difficulty breathing. Some animals may also develop digestive problems or udder infection if those organs are affected. 

### Q: How do veterinarians diagnose Bovine Tuberculosis?

**A:** Diagnosis relies mainly on tuberculin skin tests (single intradermal or comparative tests) and, in some cases, blood tests such as interferon-gamma release assays. Suspected cases found at slaughter are confirmed by laboratory testing of tissues using microscopy, culture, and molecular methods. These diagnostic tools are used together with herd history and movement records to monitor and control the disease. 

### Q: How to treat Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle?

**A:** In most countries, there is no routine medical treatment for Bovine Tuberculosis in cattle. Instead, control programs rely on test-and-slaughter: infected animals (reactors) are identified through testing and then culled under official supervision. Treating cattle with human TB drugs is generally discouraged or prohibited because it is costly, difficult, may not fully clear infection, and can contribute to drug resistance and public health risks. 

### Q: Can humans get Bovine Tuberculosis from cows?

**A:** Yes. Bovine Tuberculosis is a zoonotic disease. People can be infected with Mycobacterium bovis, mainly by consuming raw, unpasteurized milk or dairy products from infected cows, or more rarely by inhaling aerosols from close contact with infected animals. Modern pasteurization and meat inspection have greatly reduced human cases in many countries, but the risk remains where unpasteurized milk is consumed or where control programs are weak. 

### Q: How can I prevent Bovine Tuberculosis from entering my herd?

**A:** The best prevention includes buying cattle only from herds with known TB status, quarantining and testing new additions, participating in regular official testing programs, promptly removing positive reactors, improving housing and ventilation, avoiding contact with wildlife reservoirs, and ensuring that all milk for humans is pasteurized. Good record-keeping and close cooperation with your veterinarian and animal health authorities are also essential.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

You should contact a veterinarian promptly if:

- You notice suspicious signs, such as chronic coughing, unexplained weight loss, or swollen lymph nodes in your cattle, especially if more than one animal is affected.  
- You receive information from slaughterhouses or animal health officials that a cow from your herd has shown lesions suspicious for Bovine Tuberculosis at post-mortem inspection. 
- Your farm is in an area where Bovine Tuberculosis has been detected in cattle or wildlife, and you need advice on testing schedules, movement controls, and biosecurity measures.  

A veterinarian can:

- Arrange the appropriate skin or blood tests for your herd  
- Help interpret test results and explain what they mean for your animals and for movement of cattle on and off your farm  
- Advise on isolation, culling decisions, cleaning and disinfection, and how to work with regulatory authorities  
- Provide guidance on safe milk and meat handling, and the protection of farm workers and family members  

Early veterinary involvement reduces the risk of widespread infection, lowers economic losses, and protects public health.

## Conclusion

Bovine Tuberculosis is a chronic, infectious, and zoonotic disease with far‑reaching consequences for cattle health, farm economics, and human safety. It is caused mainly by Mycobacterium bovis, which spreads between animals through respiratory droplets, contaminated environments, and, in some regions, through wildlife reservoirs.  Because infected cattle often appear healthy, relying only on visible symptoms is not enough.

Modern control of Bovine Tuberculosis is based on regular herd testing, rapid culling of reactors, strict movement controls, slaughterhouse surveillance, and public health measures such as universal milk pasteurization.  For most farmers, the most important practical steps are to understand the causes and symptoms of Bovine Tuberculosis, cooperate fully with testing and surveillance programs, and apply strong biosecurity and good management on their farms.

By working closely with veterinarians and following national guidelines, farmers can reduce the risk of Bovine Tuberculosis in their herds, protect their livelihoods, and help safeguard the health of people and animals alike.
