Bovine tuberculosis is a serious disease that can spread quietly through cattle herds before any obvious signs appear. Caused mainly by the bacterium *Mycobacterium bovis*, bovine TB affects cattle most often but can also infect bison, deer, elk, goats, wildlife, and people.

The disease matters for more than animal health. Bovine tuberculosis can reduce productivity, restrict livestock movement, damage farm income, affect international trade, and create a public health risk through infected animals and unpasteurized dairy products. Because infected cattle may look healthy for months or years, routine testing and strong biosecurity are essential.  

This guide explains what bovine tuberculosis is, how it spreads, the signs to watch for, how veterinarians diagnose it, and what you can do to protect your herd.

## What Is Bovine Tuberculosis?

Bovine tuberculosis, often called bovine TB or bTB, is a chronic bacterial infection caused primarily by *Mycobacterium bovis*. It belongs to the *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex, a group of closely related bacteria that includes the main cause of human tuberculosis.

Cattle are the principal livestock host, although the infection can affect many warm-blooded animals. Wildlife species may also carry and spread *M. bovis*, making eradication difficult in some regions. Known wildlife reservoirs include badgers in the United Kingdom, white-tailed deer in parts of the United States, and brushtail possums in New Zealand.  

Bovine TB usually develops slowly. Some cattle remain infected without showing visible illness, while others eventually develop lesions in the lungs, lymph nodes, intestines, liver, spleen, or other organs. These lesions are called tubercles or granulomas.

Because clinical signs are often absent or nonspecific, you should never rely on visual observation alone to confirm that cattle are free from bovine tuberculosis.

## Why Bovine TB Is a Serious Herd Health Concern

Bovine tuberculosis can affect your operation in several ways:

- Infected cattle may lose weight and produce less milk or meat.
- Movement restrictions may be placed on affected herds.
- Positive or exposed cattle may need to be removed under official disease-control rules.
- Testing, quarantine, tracing, cleaning, and veterinary investigations can be expensive.
- Wildlife reservoirs may reintroduce infection after a herd has been cleared.
- Confirmed cases can affect consumer confidence and livestock trade.
- People may be exposed through infected animals or unpasteurized dairy products.

International animal-health programs classify bovine TB as a reportable disease because controlling it protects livestock, wildlife, food safety, and public health. 

## How Bovine Tuberculosis Spreads

The most common route of transmission between cattle is inhalation. An infected animal may release bacteria in respiratory droplets when it coughs, breathes heavily, or is housed close to other animals.

Close contact increases the risk, particularly in enclosed barns, crowded yards, transport vehicles, feedlots, and poorly ventilated housing. An infected animal may spread *M. bovis* before showing obvious signs of disease. This silent period is one reason cattle movement is an important factor in herd-to-herd transmission.  

Bovine TB can also spread through ingestion. Calves may become infected by drinking milk or colostrum from an infected cow. Contaminated feed, water, saliva, feces, urine, and other body fluids may also contribute to transmission, depending on the circumstances.

Potential transmission routes include:

- Breathing contaminated respiratory droplets
- Drinking infected milk or colostrum
- Eating contaminated feed
- Drinking from contaminated water sources
- Contact with infected manure or body fluids
- Mixing with infected cattle during transport, sales, shows, or grazing
- Contact with infected wildlife
- Introducing cattle from herds with an unknown disease status

Most cattle-to-cattle transmission occurs through direct contact, but contaminated equipment, vehicles, housing, and handling facilities can increase the risk when hygiene is poor.

## Wildlife and Bovine Tuberculosis

Wildlife can complicate bovine TB control. In areas where *M. bovis* is established in wild animals, cattle may become infected despite regular herd testing.

Risk is greater when wildlife can access:

- Feed stores
- Mineral stations
- Water troughs
- Calving areas
- Pastures
- Silage or hay
- Cattle housing
- Salt licks and bait sites

Wildlife control must follow local laws and veterinary guidance. Practical measures may include wildlife-resistant fencing, covered feed storage, raised feed bunks, protected water sources, and reduced opportunities for nose-to-nose contact.

National and regional programs may also use wildlife surveillance, vaccination, population management, buffer zones, or other measures. The best approach depends on the wildlife species involved, local ecology, disease prevalence, and legal requirements. 

## Signs of Bovine Tuberculosis in Cattle

Many cattle with bovine TB appear healthy. The infection may remain hidden for months or years, and some animals are detected only through routine testing or meat inspection.

When signs do occur, they are usually vague. Possible bovine tuberculosis symptoms include:

- Progressive weight loss
- Reduced appetite
- Weakness or depression
- Poor body condition
- Reduced milk production
- Rough or dull hair coat
- Low-grade, fluctuating fever
- Chronic or intermittent moist cough
- Rapid or difficult breathing
- Enlarged lymph nodes, especially around the head and neck
- Intermittent diarrhea or constipation
- Respiratory distress in advanced disease
- Draining lymph-node lesions in severe cases

A cough may become more noticeable during exercise, cold weather, or when the trachea is pressed. Enlarged internal lymph nodes can obstruct the airways or digestive tract.

However, these signs are not unique to bovine TB. Pneumonia, parasitic disease, cancer, Johne’s disease, chronic malnutrition, and other infections can cause similar problems. A veterinarian must investigate the cause.

Contact your veterinarian promptly if cattle show unexplained chronic weight loss, persistent coughing, enlarged lymph nodes, or declining production—especially if the herd has recently introduced animals or experienced contact with wildlife.

## How Bovine TB Is Diagnosed

### Tuberculin skin testing

The tuberculin skin test is the primary screening method used in many cattle health programs. A small amount of purified protein derivative, or PPD, is injected into the skin. The veterinarian measures the animal’s immune response after the required waiting period.

In the United States, cattle and bison are commonly examined approximately 72 hours after injection. The U.S. National Veterinary Accreditation Program lists the caudal-fold test using bovine PPD as an official testing method for cattle and bison.  

Some programs use a comparative test involving bovine and avian tuberculin. Comparing the two reactions can help distinguish exposure to *M. bovis* from reactions caused by other environmental mycobacteria.

A positive or inconclusive result does not always provide a final diagnosis by itself. Interpretation depends on the test used, the herd’s history, local regulations, previous results, and the animal’s risk of exposure.

### Interferon-gamma blood testing

The interferon-gamma assay is a blood test used in some regions as a supplementary test. It measures immune activity associated with exposure to tuberculosis bacteria.

Blood testing can help identify infected cattle that may not react clearly to a skin test. It is often used in higher-risk herds, confirmed breakdowns, or situations where veterinary authorities need additional information. The exact availability and legal use vary by country and region. 

### Post-mortem examination

Cattle may also be identified through slaughter surveillance or necropsy. Inspectors examine the lungs, lymph nodes, liver, spleen, intestines, pleura, peritoneum, and other tissues for suspicious granulomas or tubercles.

Lesions may appear yellow, caseous, calcified, or abscess-like. Some are very small and can be missed without careful examination and tissue sectioning. A suspicious lesion does not always prove bovine TB because other infections can look similar.

### Laboratory confirmation

Veterinary laboratories may use:

- Histopathology
- Acid-fast staining
- Bacterial culture
- Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR
- Other approved molecular tests

Culture can confirm the presence of viable bacteria, but *M. bovis* grows slowly and laboratory confirmation may take time. PCR can provide faster information, although laboratories may use culture and molecular testing together for confirmation. 

## What Happens if a Cow Tests Positive?

If a cow has a positive or inconclusive bovine TB test, do not move, sell, or mix the animal unless veterinary authorities approve it. Contact your accredited veterinarian or local animal-health authority immediately.

Typical response steps may include:

1. Identifying the animal and recording its official tag number.
2. Restricting movement of the animal or herd.
3. Testing other cattle in the herd.
4. Tracing recent animal movements.
5. Investigating possible wildlife exposure.
6. Sending tissue samples for laboratory examination.
7. Removing confirmed infected or high-risk animals according to official rules.
8. Cleaning and managing contaminated facilities.
9. Completing follow-up testing before restrictions are lifted.

The exact response differs by country and disease-control program. In many production systems, treatment of infected cattle is not recommended. Treatment is costly, prolonged, difficult to verify, and may leave an infected animal capable of spreading disease. Control programs therefore commonly rely on testing, removal of infected animals, movement controls, surveillance, and biosecurity. 

## Bovine TB Prevention and Biosecurity

No single action prevents every case, but a layered biosecurity plan can greatly reduce risk.

### Buy cattle carefully

Before purchasing cattle, ask for:

- Recent bovine TB test records
- Herd health history
- Source-herd information
- Movement history
- Official identification
- Evidence of disease-free or accredited status, where applicable

Avoid buying animals from unknown sources or mixing animals immediately after arrival.

### Quarantine new arrivals

Keep purchased cattle separate from your resident herd until your veterinarian determines that they are safe to mix. Use separate feed and water equipment when possible.

Your veterinarian may recommend additional testing based on the animal’s origin, risk level, travel history, and local regulations.

### Reduce cattle movement risks

Movement controls are a major part of bovine TB prevention. Keep accurate records for every animal entering or leaving your property.

Limit unnecessary commingling at:

- Livestock markets
- Shows
- Grazing arrangements
- Shared transport
- Community pastures
- Boarding facilities
- Neighboring fence lines

Maintain secure identification and traceability so animal-health officials can investigate quickly if infection is found.

### Improve housing and ventilation

Good ventilation reduces the buildup of respiratory droplets. Avoid overcrowding and maintain clean, dry housing.

You should also:

- Clean high-contact surfaces regularly.
- Disinfect equipment between groups where practical.
- Avoid sharing trailers without cleaning them.
- Separate sick animals promptly.
- Keep feed and water protected from contamination.
- Provide adequate nutrition to support immune function.

### Manage wildlife contact

Use covered or secured feed storage, wildlife-resistant fencing, and protected water sources. Prevent wildlife from gathering around cattle feed or mineral stations.

Do not bait wildlife near livestock, and follow local wildlife-management requirements. Your veterinarian or animal-health authority can help create a site-specific wildlife risk plan.

## Protecting People From Bovine TB

Bovine TB is zoonotic, meaning *M. bovis* can infect humans. Human infection most commonly occurs through consuming unpasteurized milk or dairy products from infected animals. People who work closely with cattle, bison, deer, elk, hides, carcasses, or animal products may also face increased exposure risk.  

The most important precautions are:

- Drink pasteurized milk.
- Choose dairy products clearly labeled “pasteurized.”
- Avoid homemade or imported dairy products with uncertain processing.
- Wear suitable protective equipment when handling suspected infected animals or tissues.
- Follow slaughterhouse and veterinary hygiene procedures.
- Wash hands after handling cattle, carcasses, manure, or equipment.
- Seek medical advice after a significant exposure.

Pasteurization eliminates *M. bovis* from milk, and proper cooking helps protect consumers from contaminated meat. Meat from animals identified with suspicious lesions is removed from the food chain under meat-inspection procedures.  

People with possible exposure should tell their healthcare provider about contact with cattle, wildlife, raw dairy products, or a known infected herd. Human testing may involve a tuberculin skin test or an interferon-gamma blood test, depending on medical advice.

## Is There a Vaccine for Bovine TB?

Vaccination is complicated because available animal TB vaccines can interfere with commonly used diagnostic tests. A vaccinated animal may become difficult to distinguish from an infected animal using traditional testing methods.

For this reason, most national cattle-control programs continue to emphasize surveillance, biosecurity, movement controls, and removal of infected animals. Researchers are developing improved vaccines and diagnostic systems that may allow vaccination while still identifying infected cattle accurately.  

Do not vaccinate cattle against bovine TB unless specifically authorized by the relevant animal-health authority.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Bovine Tuberculosis

### Can cattle have bovine TB without showing symptoms?

Yes. Many infected cattle appear normal, particularly during the early or latent stages. Routine testing and slaughter surveillance are important because symptoms alone cannot reliably identify infected animals. 

### Is bovine tuberculosis contagious?

Yes. Bovine TB can spread between cattle, wildlife, and other susceptible mammals. In cattle, inhalation of respiratory droplets during close contact is the most common route, although ingestion of contaminated materials can also occur.

### Can humans get bovine tuberculosis from cattle?

Yes. People can become infected with *M. bovis*, especially through unpasteurized dairy products or close occupational contact with infected animals and tissues. The risk is substantially reduced by cattle-control programs, meat inspection, and milk pasteurization. 

### Can bovine TB be treated in cattle?

Treatment is generally not used in commercial cattle because it is prolonged, expensive, difficult to monitor, and may allow infection to remain in the herd. Official programs typically rely on testing, movement restrictions, and removal of infected animals.

### Does cooking kill bovine TB bacteria?

Proper cooking kills *M. bovis* in meat, while pasteurization eliminates the bacteria from milk. You should still follow food-safety rules and avoid raw milk or unpasteurized dairy products. 

### What should I do if I suspect bovine TB in my herd?

Isolate the affected animal if it can be done safely, stop unnecessary animal movement, and contact your veterinarian or animal-health authority immediately. Do not sell, slaughter, or transport a suspected animal outside official instructions.

### Can wildlife spread bovine TB back to cattle?

Yes. In some regions, infected wildlife acts as a reservoir and can transmit *M. bovis* to cattle. Protecting feed, water, housing, and pasture from wildlife contact is an important part of herd biosecurity.

## Conclusion

Bovine tuberculosis is a slow, often silent disease that can affect cattle health, farm profitability, wildlife, food safety, and human health. Because infected animals may look normal, the strongest protection comes from routine official testing, careful livestock purchasing, quarantine, traceability, wildlife-risk management, and prompt reporting of suspected cases.

If you manage cattle, work with livestock, or produce dairy products, take bovine TB seriously. A practical prevention plan and close partnership with your veterinarian can help detect infection early, limit transmission, and protect both your herd and the wider community.
