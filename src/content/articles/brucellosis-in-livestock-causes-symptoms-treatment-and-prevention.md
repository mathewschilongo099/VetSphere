# Brucellosis in Livestock: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Brucellosis in Livestock is a serious bacterial disease that affects cattle, sheep, goats, pigs and other farm animals, causing abortions, infertility, reduced milk yield and major financial losses for farmers.  It is also a zoonotic disease, meaning it can infect people who handle sick animals or consume unpasteurized milk and dairy products.  Because of its health, productivity and trade impacts, brucellosis is considered one of the most important infectious diseases of farm animals worldwide.  

This article explains what Brucellosis in Livestock is, the main causes, symptoms, diagnosis, how to treat Brucellosis in Livestock in practical terms, and the key measures for prevention of Brucellosis in Livestock on farms. It is written in simple, clear language for farmers, students and animal owners, but based on current veterinary and international guidelines. 

## What is Brucellosis in Livestock?

Brucellosis is an infectious disease caused by bacteria of the genus Brucella. These bacteria mainly infect cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and dogs, but can also affect camels, bison, elk and some wildlife species.  In livestock, the disease is often known by traditional names such as “contagious abortion” or “Bang’s disease” in cattle, because late-term abortion is one of the most characteristic signs.  

Different Brucella species tend to prefer different animal hosts. Brucella abortus mainly affects cattle, bison and elk; Brucella melitensis primarily infects goats and sheep; Brucella suis affects pigs; Brucella ovis causes disease in rams; and Brucella canis infects dogs.  Among these, B. abortus, B. melitensis and B. suis are the most important for livestock and human infections worldwide.  Brucella melitensis is considered the most virulent for humans and is often linked to infected goats and sheep.  

Brucellosis in Livestock is a chronic disease. Once animals become infected, they may carry the bacteria for life, even if they do not always show obvious signs.  Infected animals shed Brucella in abortion material, placentas, uterine discharges, milk, semen and sometimes urine, which contaminates the environment and spreads infection within and between herds and flocks.  This persistent shedding and the zoonotic risk make brucellosis a regulated, notifiable disease in most countries. 

## Causes of Brucellosis in Livestock

The immediate cause of Brucellosis in Livestock is infection with Brucella bacteria. Transmission usually occurs through direct contact with infected birth materials (aborted fetuses, placentas, vaginal discharges), ingestion of contaminated feed or water, or nursing from infected dams.  The bacteria can enter through the mouth, nose, eyes, or breaks in the skin. Breeding (natural service or infected semen) is another important route, especially in small ruminants and pigs.  

On the farm, several management practices increase the risk and are therefore important “causes” in a practical sense. Buying animals from unknown or infected herds, mixing species (for example, keeping cattle together with infected goats or sheep), poor hygiene at calving and lambing, and allowing animals to lick or eat aborted fetuses and placentas all favour spread of the disease.  Lack of routine testing, absence of vaccination in endemic areas, and uncontrolled animal movement further maintain infection in a region.  

In many developing countries, Brucellosis in Livestock persists because of limited veterinary services, low awareness among farmers, and inadequate implementation of national control plans.  Expansion of animal industries, intensification of production, and poor hygiene in animal husbandry also contribute to the continuing spread of brucellosis and its spillover to humans.  Thus, the causes of Brucellosis in Livestock include both the bacteria themselves and the farm-level and national-level factors that allow them to circulate.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock

### General reproductive signs

The main symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock are related to the reproductive system. In cattle, the classic sign is late-term abortion, usually in the last third of gestation, often followed by retention of the placenta and metritis (uterine infection).  In goats and sheep, Brucella melitensis infection commonly causes abortion, stillbirths and the birth of weak kids or lambs.  Similar reproductive problems occur in pigs infected with B. suis.  

Infection can also lead to reduced fertility. Cows, ewes and does may fail to conceive, show repeat breeding, or have prolonged calving or lambing intervals.  In males, especially rams and sometimes bulls and boars, Brucella can cause orchitis and epididymitis (inflammation of the testes and epididymis), which leads to swollen, painful testicles and infertility.  Over time, this results in fewer pregnancies and declining flock or herd productivity.

### Production and general health signs

Besides reproductive symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock, farmers may notice a drop in milk yield, particularly in dairy cattle and goats.  Chronic infection can be associated with reduced weight gain, poor body condition and overall reduced performance, although these signs are not specific to brucellosis.

Many infected animals, especially those infected at a young age, may appear clinically normal between pregnancies.  They can still shed Brucella bacteria during calving or lambing and through milk, making them a hidden source of infection for other animals and for people. This “silent carrier” status is one reason why serological testing is essential for detecting Brucellosis in Livestock on farms.  

Because brucellosis is zoonotic, farmers, veterinarians and abattoir workers exposed to infected animals can develop undulating fever, joint pain and other flu-like symptoms.  Any cluster of abortions in a herd or flock, particularly when handlers develop fever, should immediately raise suspicion of Brucellosis in Livestock and human brucellosis.

## How to Diagnose Brucellosis in Livestock

Diagnosis of Brucellosis in Livestock relies mainly on a combination of herd history, clinical signs and laboratory tests. A sudden increase in abortions, stillbirths, weak offspring, reduced fertility, or testicular swelling in males should prompt testing for brucellosis.  However, because many other diseases can also cause abortions, laboratory confirmation is essential.

The most commonly used tests are serological (blood) tests that detect antibodies against Brucella. Standard tests include the Rose Bengal Plate Test (RBPT), complement fixation test (CFT), and various ELISA tests (I-ELISA, C-ELISA).  These tests are used both for individual animals and for herd-level screening. In many countries, regular serological screening of cattle and small ruminants, testing of animals at slaughterhouses and markets, and milk ring testing in dairy herds are part of national brucellosis control programmes.  

In some situations, bacterial culture or PCR may be used to confirm Brucellosis in Livestock by detecting the organism in aborted fetuses, placentas, vaginal swabs, milk, or tissues from slaughtered animals.  These methods require specialized laboratories and strict biosafety, because Brucella is highly infectious to humans. Positive animals are usually considered infected for life, and in most official programmes they are culled (sent to slaughter) to prevent further spread. 

## Treatment of Brucellosis in Livestock

From a practical and regulatory standpoint, there is generally no recommended curative treatment of Brucellosis in Livestock. In most countries and under international guidelines, infected cattle, sheep, goats and pigs are not treated with antibiotics but are removed from the herd or flock and slaughtered.  This is because Brucella infection tends to become chronic and is difficult to eliminate completely with drugs, and because treated animals may still carry and shed the bacteria.

Using antibiotics on infected livestock herds is also discouraged due to cost, the risk of treatment failure, the possibility of creating carrier animals, and concerns about antimicrobial resistance and food safety.  Therefore, when considering how to treat Brucellosis in Livestock, veterinarians and authorities usually focus not on curing individual animals but on controlling and eradicating the disease at herd and regional level through test-and-slaughter, vaccination and biosecurity.

Supportive care may be provided to valuable animals in some settings (for example, treatment of secondary uterine infections after abortion), but this does not change the infected status of the animal.  In breeding males with orchitis or epididymitis, culling is usually advised, because fertility is often permanently affected and the animal is a significant source of infection.  

Because human brucellosis is treatable with specific antibiotic combinations, it is very important that farmers and workers who may have been exposed to infected animals seek medical care.  While this does not treat Brucellosis in Livestock itself, prompt human treatment reduces illness and highlights the need to address infection at the animal source.

## Prevention and Control of Brucellosis in Livestock

The prevention of Brucellosis in Livestock is based on three main pillars: vaccination, surveillance and testing, and strict biosecurity and hygiene. Combined, these methods have successfully eradicated or greatly reduced brucellosis in many high-income countries and are the backbone of control programs worldwide. 

### Vaccination

Vaccination is widely recognized as the most effective method for controlling brucellosis in livestock in endemic areas.  In cattle, commonly used vaccines include Brucella abortus strain 19 and RB51; in goats and sheep, the preferred vaccine is Brucella melitensis Rev-1.  These are live attenuated vaccines that stimulate good immunity and reduce abortions and bacterial shedding in vaccinated herds.

Vaccination is usually targeted at young animals (for example, heifer calves between 3–8 months of age in many programmes) to provide protection before they reach breeding age.  In high-prevalence areas, mass vaccination of whole herds or flocks may be recommended.  National veterinary services generally regulate which vaccines are allowed and how they should be used, so farmers should follow official guidelines and work closely with their veterinarian. 

### Surveillance, testing and culling

Regular serological screening, especially of breeding animals and animals moved through markets and slaughterhouses, is essential for the early detection and control of Brucellosis in Livestock. “Test-and-slaughter” policies, in which all animals that test positive for brucellosis are removed and humanely slaughtered, are standard control strategies where disease prevalence is low and eradication is the goal.  

Market cattle testing, slaughterhouse surveillance, and milk ring testing are proven strategies that provide both surveillance data and direct control by eliminating infected animals from the population.  Countries that have successfully eradicated bovine brucellosis, such as Canada and several European nations, have used long-term combinations of vaccination, testing and culling, along with strict movement controls. 

### Biosecurity, hygiene and farm management

Good farm management is vital for the prevention of Brucellosis in Livestock. Key measures include: prompt isolation of animals that abort; safe disposal (burning or deep burial) of aborted fetuses, placentas and contaminated bedding; thorough cleaning and disinfection of calving and lambing areas; and preventing other animals from accessing contaminated materials.  Newly purchased animals should come from brucellosis-free herds or be tested before introduction, and contact with wildlife reservoirs should be minimized where relevant.  

Educating farmers and workers about the disease, encouraging the use of protective clothing when handling birth materials, and promoting pasteurization or boiling of milk all help to reduce both animal and human brucellosis.  Coordinated “One Health” approaches that link veterinary and public health services are strongly recommended by international organizations for sustainable control of Brucellosis in Livestock. 

## Frequently Asked Questions About Brucellosis in Livestock

### Q: What is the main cause of Brucellosis in Livestock?

**A:** The main cause is infection with Brucella bacteria, especially Brucella abortus in cattle, Brucella melitensis in goats and sheep, and Brucella suis in pigs.  Animals usually become infected through contact with aborted fetuses, placentas, uterine discharges, contaminated environments, or through ingestion of contaminated feed, water or milk. 

### Q: What are the most common symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock?

**A:** The most common symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock are late-term abortions, stillbirths, birth of weak offspring, retained placenta, metritis, reduced fertility, and swollen testes or epididymis in males.  Many infected animals may appear healthy between pregnancies but still shed the bacteria at calving or lambing and in milk. 

### Q: How do you treat Brucellosis in Livestock?

**A:** In most countries there is no approved antibiotic treatment to cure Brucellosis in Livestock. Infected animals are usually permanently infected and are removed from the herd or flock and slaughtered as part of official control programs.  Treatment efforts focus on controlling the disease at herd and regional level through testing, culling, vaccination and improved biosecurity rather than curing individual animals. 

### Q: How can farmers prevent Brucellosis in Livestock on their farms?

**A:** Prevention of Brucellosis in Livestock depends on vaccinating animals where recommended, buying replacement stock only from brucellosis-free herds or after testing, promptly isolating animals that abort, safely disposing of aborted materials, maintaining good hygiene at calving and lambing, and avoiding contact with wildlife or unknown-status herds.  Participation in national surveillance and control programs, including regular testing, is also essential. 

### Q: Is Brucellosis in Livestock dangerous to humans?

**A:** Yes. Brucellosis is a zoonotic disease and is considered one of the most widespread zoonoses worldwide.  Humans can become infected through direct contact with infected animals and their birth products, by consuming unpasteurized milk and dairy products, or by inhaling contaminated aerosols (for example, in laboratories and abattoirs).  It causes a long-lasting febrile illness in people, so preventing brucellosis in animals is a key strategy to protect human health. 

### Q: Can Brucellosis in Livestock be eradicated?

**A:** Brucellosis in Livestock can be eradicated from a country or region through long-term, coordinated programs that combine vaccination, testing and slaughter of infected animals, movement controls, surveillance, and public education.  Several high-income countries, including parts of Europe, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, have successfully eliminated bovine brucellosis using such integrated strategies. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Farmers should call a veterinarian immediately if they observe a sudden increase in abortions, especially in the last trimester of pregnancy, or if multiple cows, ewes, does or sows abort over a short period.  The presence of retained placentas, birth of weak or dead offspring, or unexplained infertility problems should also raise concern. Because these are key symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock but can also be caused by other diseases, professional investigation is essential.

Any case of swollen, painful testes or epididymis in breeding males (bulls, rams, bucks, boars) warrants urgent veterinary attention, since this may indicate brucellosis or other serious infections that affect fertility and carry a high risk of spreading disease.  In addition, if farm workers develop persistent fever, sweating and joint pains after handling abortion materials or raw milk, both medical doctors and veterinarians should be informed, as animal and human brucellosis must be considered together.  

A veterinarian can organize appropriate diagnostic testing, advise on quarantine and movement restrictions, and work with veterinary authorities to implement test-and-slaughter, vaccination and biosecurity measures.  Early veterinary involvement reduces economic losses and helps protect the health of both animals and people.

## Conclusion

Brucellosis in Livestock is a major infectious and zoonotic disease that affects cattle, sheep, goats, pigs and other animals, causing abortions, infertility, reduced productivity and serious economic losses, especially in low- and middle-income countries.  It is caused by Brucella bacteria, which are transmitted mainly through reproductive materials, milk and close contact between animals, and from animals to humans via direct exposure or consumption of unpasteurized dairy products.  

Because effective treatment of Brucellosis in Livestock at the individual animal level is generally not available or recommended, control relies on prevention: strategic vaccination, regular testing and culling of infected animals, strong farm biosecurity, and coordinated national and regional programmes.  Understanding the causes of Brucellosis in Livestock, recognizing the symptoms of Brucellosis in Livestock early, and applying strict prevention of Brucellosis in Livestock through vaccination and good management are essential steps for farmers, veterinarians and authorities working to protect animal health, human health and livelihoods.
