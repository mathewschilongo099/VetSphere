Canine distemper is one of the most serious infectious diseases a dog can develop. This highly contagious virus can affect a dog’s respiratory system, digestive tract, immune system, skin, and nervous system. In severe cases, canine distemper can be fatal or leave lifelong neurological problems.

The good news is that canine distemper is largely preventable. Routine vaccination offers excellent protection, while early veterinary care can improve the outlook for dogs that become infected. Understanding the signs of distemper, how it spreads, and how to protect your dog can help you act quickly when it matters most.

## What Is Canine Distemper?

Canine distemper is a viral disease caused by canine distemper virus, or CDV. It belongs to a family of viruses related to measles and can infect domestic dogs as well as several wild carnivores, including raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes, wolves, and ferrets.   

The virus attacks multiple body systems rather than affecting only one organ. Early symptoms may look like a mild cold, but the disease can progress to pneumonia, vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration, seizures, paralysis, and other neurological complications.

Canine distemper occurs worldwide. It is most common among puppies, unvaccinated dogs, dogs with unknown vaccination histories, and animals living in crowded conditions such as shelters or breeding facilities.

Although canine distemper can be deadly, widespread vaccination has made it much less common in properly vaccinated pet dogs.

## How Do Dogs Get Canine Distemper?

Canine distemper spreads mainly through respiratory droplets. An infected dog can release the virus into the air when coughing, sneezing, barking, or breathing near another animal.

Dogs may also become infected through contact with:

- Saliva
- Nasal discharge
- Eye discharge
- Urine
- Feces
- Vomit
- Shared food and water bowls
- Bedding, toys, crates, or other contaminated objects

Direct contact is not always necessary. A susceptible dog may become infected after spending time in an area contaminated by an infected animal.

Wildlife can also be a source of infection. Raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes, and other affected animals may spread canine distemper to dogs through close contact or contaminated secretions. For this reason, it is safest to prevent your dog from approaching sick, injured, unusually tame, or dead wildlife.

An infected dog may spread the virus before obvious symptoms appear. Some dogs develop only mild signs or no visible signs at all while still shedding the virus. Depending on the dog’s immune response and disease severity, virus shedding may continue for weeks or months.  

## Which Dogs Are Most at Risk?

Any dog can contract canine distemper if exposed and not adequately protected. However, certain dogs have a greater risk of severe disease.

High-risk dogs include:

- Puppies younger than four months
- Dogs that have not received core vaccinations
- Dogs with incomplete puppy vaccine series
- Dogs with unknown vaccination histories
- Dogs recently adopted from crowded environments
- Dogs with weakened immune systems
- Dogs exposed to infected wildlife
- Dogs living in areas with low vaccination rates

Puppies are especially vulnerable because maternal antibodies passed through their mother’s milk gradually decline. These antibodies may provide temporary protection, but they can also interfere with early vaccine responses. This is why puppies need a series of distemper vaccinations rather than a single dose.

## Canine Distemper Symptoms

Canine distemper symptoms vary widely. Some dogs develop mild respiratory signs, while others become critically ill within a short period. Symptoms may appear one to six weeks after exposure, although the timing depends on the dog’s immune status, the viral strain, and the amount of exposure.   

Common early signs include:

- Fever
- Tiredness or depression
- Reduced appetite
- Clear or thick discharge from the eyes
- Nasal discharge
- Coughing
- Sneezing
- Difficulty breathing
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Weight loss
- Dehydration

Eye and nose discharge may become thick, yellow, or green if secondary bacterial infections develop. Some dogs develop pneumonia, which may cause rapid breathing, labored breathing, weakness, or bluish gums.

### Skin and Footpad Changes

Canine distemper may cause thickening or hardening of the nose and paw pads. This is sometimes called “hard pad disease.” The skin may become dry, cracked, crusted, or uncomfortable.

Puppies infected while their teeth are developing may later develop enamel defects. These teeth can be more sensitive and vulnerable to damage.

### Neurological Symptoms

Neurological signs are among the most concerning symptoms of canine distemper. They may occur during the initial illness, after respiratory and digestive symptoms improve, or weeks or months later.

Watch for:

- Muscle twitching
- Facial tremors
- Stiff movements
- Poor coordination
- Stumbling
- Circling
- Head tilt
- Abnormal eye movements
- Weakness
- Partial or complete paralysis
- Seizures
- Drooling
- Repetitive jaw movements

A classic distemper seizure may involve rhythmic jaw movements that appear as though the dog is chewing gum. However, not every dog develops this sign.

Neurological disease generally carries a more guarded prognosis than respiratory or digestive disease. Some dogs recover, while others develop permanent muscle twitching, seizures, coordination problems, or progressive neurological damage. Neurological signs can occasionally appear long after the initial infection.  

## When Should You Contact a Veterinarian?

Contact your veterinarian promptly if your dog develops fever, eye or nose discharge, coughing, vomiting, diarrhea, unusual tiredness, or loss of appetite—especially if your dog is young, unvaccinated, or has recently been exposed to other dogs or wildlife.

Seek emergency veterinary care if your dog has:

- Difficulty breathing
- Repeated vomiting or diarrhea
- Severe weakness or collapse
- Signs of dehydration
- A seizure lasting more than a few minutes
- Multiple seizures
- Sudden paralysis
- Confusion or severe loss of coordination
- Blue, gray, or very pale gums

Do not wait for neurological symptoms to appear. Canine distemper may already be affecting the body before seizures or paralysis develop.

Until your dog is examined, keep the dog separated from other dogs. Call the veterinary hospital before arriving so staff can prepare an isolation area and reduce exposure to other patients.

## How Is Canine Distemper Diagnosed?

Canine distemper can resemble several other diseases, including canine parvovirus, kennel cough, infectious canine hepatitis, pneumonia, poisoning, and rabies. A veterinarian cannot confirm distemper based only on symptoms.

Diagnosis usually involves:

1. **Medical history**  
   Your veterinarian will ask about vaccination status, recent exposure, travel, contact with wildlife, and when symptoms began.

2. **Physical examination**  
   The veterinarian will check your dog’s temperature, hydration, breathing, eye and nose discharge, neurological function, and overall condition.

3. **Blood testing**  
   A complete blood count may reveal changes associated with infection or immune suppression, although bloodwork alone cannot confirm distemper.

4. **PCR testing**  
   Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction testing can detect genetic material from the virus in samples such as nasal, ocular, blood, urine, or other tissue samples.

5. **Antibody testing**  
   Antibody tests may help identify an immune response to the virus. Recent vaccination can complicate interpretation, so your veterinarian will consider test results alongside the dog’s symptoms and history.

6. **Additional tests**  
   Chest X-rays may be recommended when pneumonia is suspected. Dogs with neurological symptoms may need further testing, including cerebrospinal fluid analysis.

Testing is important because a dog may be contagious even when symptoms are mild or absent.

## Can Canine Distemper Be Treated?

There is no universally effective medication that eliminates canine distemper virus from an infected dog. Treatment is primarily supportive, meaning it helps the body manage complications while the immune system fights the virus.   

Depending on the dog’s symptoms, treatment may include:

- Intravenous or subcutaneous fluids
- Nutritional support
- Easily digestible food
- Anti-nausea medication
- Medication to control diarrhea
- Antibiotics for secondary bacterial infections
- Oxygen therapy for pneumonia
- Fever and pain management
- Anti-seizure medication
- Careful nursing and temperature support
- Hospitalization and isolation

Antibiotics do not kill the distemper virus. They may be used because canine distemper can weaken the immune system and make secondary bacterial infections more likely.

Dogs with mild respiratory or digestive disease may recover with veterinary-supervised outpatient care. Dogs with pneumonia, severe dehydration, repeated vomiting, or neurological symptoms often need hospitalization.

Never give your dog human medications unless your veterinarian specifically instructs you to do so. Some common pain relievers and cold medications can be toxic to dogs.

## What Is the Prognosis for Canine Distemper?

The prognosis depends on several factors, including:

- The dog’s age
- Vaccination status
- Overall health
- Strength of the immune response
- Severity of pneumonia or digestive disease
- Presence of secondary infections
- Whether neurological signs develop
- Whether neurological signs are stable or worsening

Dogs with mild respiratory or gastrointestinal disease may recover fully with prompt supportive care. However, dogs with progressive seizures, paralysis, or severe neurological disease have a more uncertain outlook. Neurological damage may be permanent, even when the dog survives the initial infection.   

Some recovered dogs continue to experience muscle twitching or seizures. Others may develop delayed neurological disease weeks, months, or, in rare cases, years later.

Your veterinarian will discuss quality of life, treatment response, comfort, and the likelihood of recovery when making care decisions.

## How to Prevent Canine Distemper

Vaccination is the best way to prevent canine distemper. The distemper vaccine is included in core combination vaccines commonly labeled DAPP, DA2PP, or DHPP. These vaccines may also protect against canine adenovirus, parvovirus, and parainfluenza.

A typical puppy vaccination schedule begins between 6 and 8 weeks of age. Vaccines are usually repeated every two to four weeks until at least 14 to 16 weeks of age, depending on your veterinarian’s recommendations and local risk factors. A booster is commonly given about one year later, with additional boosters generally scheduled according to veterinary guidelines.   

Your veterinarian may recommend a different schedule for dogs with special medical needs, shelter dogs, dogs traveling to high-risk areas, or dogs with uncertain vaccine histories.

To reduce exposure:

- Keep puppies away from high-risk dog gatherings until their initial vaccine series is complete.
- Avoid dog parks, boarding facilities, daycares, and grooming facilities that do not require vaccination.
- Do not allow your dog to approach unfamiliar or sick animals.
- Prevent contact with raccoons, skunks, foxes, and other wildlife.
- Keep sick dogs isolated from other pets.
- Wash your hands after handling an infected or potentially exposed dog.
- Clean food bowls, bedding, crates, floors, and other contaminated items.
- Follow your veterinarian’s instructions before ending isolation.

Most household disinfectants can inactivate canine distemper virus when used correctly. Remove visible organic material first, then use the product according to its label instructions, including the required contact time.

## Can Humans Catch Canine Distemper?

No. Canine distemper virus is not considered transmissible to humans. It is a disease of dogs and certain other animals, not people.   

However, people can accidentally carry infectious material on their hands, shoes, clothing, or equipment and expose another dog. Good hygiene remains important, especially if you own multiple dogs, foster animals, work in a shelter, or visit veterinary facilities.

Also remember that some neurological signs in wildlife can resemble rabies. Never handle a wild animal that is disoriented, unusually aggressive, unusually tame, circling, or having seizures. Contact local animal control or wildlife authorities instead.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Canine Distemper

### 1. Can a vaccinated dog get canine distemper?

Vaccination provides strong protection, and properly vaccinated dogs are usually immune. However, no vaccine protects every animal in every situation. Vaccine failure may be more likely if a dog has an incomplete vaccine series, a serious immune disorder, improper vaccine storage, or significant exposure before immunity develops.

### 2. Is canine distemper the same as kennel cough?

No. Kennel cough is a broad term for contagious respiratory disease caused by several viruses and bacteria. Canine distemper can cause coughing, but it may also affect the digestive system, skin, immune system, and nervous system.

### 3. How long is a dog with distemper contagious?

The contagious period varies. Some dogs shed the virus for weeks, while others may continue shedding it for months. Dogs with neurological disease may require especially careful isolation. Your veterinarian should determine when isolation can safely end.

### 4. Can a dog survive canine distemper?

Yes, some dogs survive, particularly when they have a strong immune response and receive prompt supportive care. Dogs with severe or progressive neurological signs have a poorer prognosis and may suffer permanent complications.

### 5. Can puppies get distemper after one vaccine?

Yes. One vaccine does not always provide complete protection, particularly in young puppies. Maternal antibodies may interfere with the first doses, which is why puppies need repeated vaccinations until the series is complete.

### 6. Can canine distemper spread through the air?

Yes. Respiratory droplets from coughing, sneezing, barking, or close contact are important routes of transmission. Shared bowls, bedding, toys, and other contaminated items can also contribute to spread.

### 7. Does canine distemper affect cats?

Canine distemper virus is different from feline panleukopenia, which is sometimes called feline distemper. Domestic cats are not affected by canine distemper in the same way dogs are, but ferrets and several wild carnivores are susceptible.

### 8. Should I vaccinate an adult dog with an unknown history?

In many cases, vaccination is still appropriate, but your veterinarian should evaluate your dog first. The recommended plan may depend on age, health, previous records, lifestyle, and local disease risk.

## Final Thoughts

Canine distemper is a dangerous, highly contagious disease that can affect nearly every major body system. Fever, eye or nose discharge, coughing, vomiting, diarrhea, thickened paw pads, muscle twitching, and seizures should never be ignored—especially in a puppy or unvaccinated dog.

There is no reliable cure that directly eliminates the virus, so prevention is essential. Keep your dog’s core vaccinations current, limit exposure to unvaccinated animals and wildlife, and contact your veterinarian quickly if illness develops.

Protecting your dog from canine distemper starts with a simple step: follow a veterinarian-recommended vaccination schedule throughout your pet’s life.
