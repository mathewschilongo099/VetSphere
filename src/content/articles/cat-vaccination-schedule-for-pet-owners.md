Bringing home a kitten is exciting, but caring for a young cat involves more than food, toys, and a comfortable bed. Vaccinations are one of the most important parts of your cat’s preventive healthcare plan. The right cat vaccination schedule helps protect your pet from contagious, painful, and sometimes fatal diseases.

Many pet owners wonder which vaccines their cats need, how often boosters are required, and whether indoor cats need vaccinations. The answer depends on your cat’s age, health, lifestyle, exposure to other animals, and local laws. However, most kittens begin core vaccines between 6 and 8 weeks of age, with booster doses given every 3–4 weeks until approximately 16–20 weeks of age.   

This guide explains the recommended kitten vaccine schedule, adult cat boosters, core and lifestyle-based vaccines, possible side effects, and practical ways to keep your cat’s vaccinations up to date.

## Why Cat Vaccinations Matter

Vaccines prepare your cat’s immune system to recognize and fight specific infectious diseases. If your vaccinated cat is exposed later, its immune system can respond more quickly and effectively.

Vaccination can:

- Reduce the risk of serious illness
- Help prevent disease outbreaks among cats
- Protect vulnerable kittens and senior cats
- Reduce the spread of contagious infections
- Help protect people from zoonotic diseases such as rabies
- Satisfy legal, boarding, travel, or grooming requirements

The American Animal Hospital Association and Feline Veterinary Medical Association classify feline vaccines as core, non-core, or generally not recommended. Core vaccines are recommended for virtually all cats because they protect against widespread or severe diseases. Non-core vaccines are chosen according to lifestyle and exposure risk.   

Vaccines are not a substitute for regular veterinary examinations, parasite prevention, good nutrition, or safe housing. They are one important part of a complete cat healthcare plan.

## The Main Cat Vaccines Explained

### FVRCP vaccine

FVRCP is a combination vaccine that protects against three important feline diseases:

- **Feline viral rhinotracheitis**, usually caused by feline herpesvirus type 1
- **Feline calicivirus**
- **Feline panleukopenia virus**

Panleukopenia is sometimes called feline distemper or feline parvovirus. It can cause severe vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration, immune-system damage, and death, particularly in kittens.

Feline herpesvirus and calicivirus commonly cause upper respiratory disease. Affected cats may sneeze, develop eye and nose discharge, lose their appetite, or develop mouth ulcers. Although vaccination does not prevent every infection, it can reduce the severity of disease and help limit transmission.

FVRCP is considered a core vaccine for cats. The first dose is generally given at 6–8 weeks of age, followed by additional doses every 3–4 weeks until the kitten is at least 16 weeks old. Some veterinary protocols continue the series until 20 weeks, depending on the kitten’s risk and vaccine product.  

### Rabies vaccine

Rabies is a fatal viral disease that affects the nervous system and can infect people. Cats may encounter rabies through wildlife, including bats, raccoons, skunks, and foxes.

Rabies vaccination is recommended for all cats, including indoor-only cats. Local and state laws determine whether vaccination is legally required, the minimum vaccination age, and whether a one-year or three-year product must be used. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises that cats receive rabies vaccination according to local laws and product instructions.  

A kitten typically receives its first rabies vaccine at approximately 12–16 weeks of age, although the exact timing depends on the vaccine and local regulations. A booster is commonly given one year after the first dose. Later boosters may be required every one or three years.

### FeLV vaccine

The feline leukemia virus, or FeLV, can weaken the immune system and contribute to anemia, recurrent infections, and certain cancers. It spreads primarily through close contact between cats, including grooming, bite wounds, shared food and water bowls, and mutual contact with saliva or other bodily fluids.

FeLV vaccination is considered a core vaccine for kittens and cats younger than one year because young cats are more susceptible to persistent infection. The initial series generally consists of two doses given 3–4 weeks apart, beginning at approximately 8 weeks of age. A FeLV test is recommended before vaccination.  

For adult cats, continued FeLV vaccination depends on exposure risk. It is especially important for cats that:

- Go outdoors
- Live with a FeLV-positive cat
- Live with cats of unknown FeLV status
- Visit boarding facilities or multi-cat environments
- Are adopted from shelters or foster homes with uncertain exposure histories

A strictly indoor adult cat living in a stable, FeLV-negative household may not need ongoing FeLV boosters. Your veterinarian should reassess this decision whenever your cat’s lifestyle changes.

## Kitten Vaccination Schedule

Kittens need several vaccine visits because maternal antibodies can interfere with early vaccination. These antibodies provide temporary protection from the mother but gradually decline. Because veterinarians cannot predict exactly when maternal immunity will disappear in each kitten, repeated vaccines improve the chance that protection develops successfully.  

Here is a typical kitten vaccination schedule:

### 6–8 weeks

Your kitten may receive:

- First FVRCP vaccine
- First FeLV vaccine, depending on the veterinarian’s protocol

Some kittens begin FeLV vaccination at 8 weeks, while others start later within the kitten series. The decision depends on the kitten’s health, testing status, household, and likely exposure.

### 10–12 weeks

Your kitten may receive:

- Second FVRCP vaccine
- First or second FeLV vaccine

The second FeLV dose should generally be administered 3–4 weeks after the first.

### 14–16 weeks

Your kitten may receive:

- Final FVRCP kitten dose
- Rabies vaccine
- Second FeLV dose, if due

The final FVRCP dose should be administered at or after 16 weeks of age in many protocols. Earlier doses may be blocked by maternal antibodies, leaving a kitten vulnerable if the series ends too soon.  

### Around 6 months

Some veterinarians recommend an additional FVRCP revaccination at approximately 6 months rather than waiting until the one-year visit. This approach is intended to reduce the potential period when a kitten may not have responded fully to the final kitten vaccine. Discuss this option with your veterinarian.

### Around 1 year

Your cat may receive:

- FVRCP booster
- Rabies booster, if required by the vaccine label or local law
- FeLV booster, based on age and risk

Your veterinarian may combine some appointments, but the timing between doses matters. Keep every appointment as close as possible to the recommended date.

## Adult Cat Vaccination Schedule

After completing the kitten series and receiving the appropriate early booster, many low-risk adult cats need core vaccines less frequently.

A general adult cat vaccination schedule may include:

- **FVRCP:** commonly every three years for low-risk adult cats, depending on product labeling and veterinary assessment
- **Rabies:** every one or three years, depending on local laws and the vaccine used
- **FeLV:** annually or every 1–3 years for cats with ongoing exposure risk, depending on the product and risk level

The schedule is not identical for every cat. An outdoor cat that fights or encounters unknown cats may need more frequent protection than an indoor cat living alone. Cats that board, attend shows, live in shelters, or share homes with many cats may also have different vaccination needs.

Annual wellness visits remain valuable even when a vaccine is not due. Cats are skilled at hiding illness, and routine examinations can detect dental disease, weight changes, kidney problems, heart disease, parasites, and other conditions early.

## Do Indoor Cats Need Vaccines?

Yes. Indoor cats should still receive core vaccines.

An indoor cat may be exposed to disease when:

- An infected cat enters the home
- You bring infectious material inside on shoes or clothing
- Your cat escapes outdoors
- Your cat visits a veterinary hospital, groomer, or boarding facility
- A bat or other wildlife enters the home
- You adopt another cat
- Your cat is exposed during travel or emergencies

Panleukopenia virus can survive in the environment, and indoor cats are not completely isolated from infectious risks. Rabies vaccination is also important because rabies is fatal and bats can enter homes unexpectedly. Veterinary organizations recommend rabies vaccination even for cats that live exclusively indoors.  

## Core Versus Non-Core Vaccines

### Core vaccines

Core vaccines are generally recommended for all cats or all kittens within a particular age group. They include:

- FVRCP
- Rabies
- FeLV for kittens and cats younger than one year

### Non-core vaccines

Non-core vaccines may be appropriate for cats with specific risks. Examples may include vaccines for:

- Chlamydia felis
- Bordetella bronchiseptica

These vaccines are not routinely necessary for every household cat. They may be considered in shelters, catteries, high-density homes, or facilities where a specific infection has been confirmed. The protection may be incomplete, and some products may cause more side effects than core vaccines.  

There is also a vaccine for feline infectious peritonitis, or FIP, but current feline vaccination guidelines do not generally recommend it for most pet cats. Your veterinarian can explain which vaccines are appropriate for your cat’s particular situation.

## What Happens If Your Cat Is Overdue?

Do not assume your cat must restart every vaccine series if a booster is late. The appropriate response depends on the vaccine, the length of the delay, your cat’s age, health, previous vaccination history, and local regulations.

If your cat is overdue:

1. Call your veterinary clinic.
2. Provide any available vaccination records.
3. Explain whether your cat goes outdoors or lives with other cats.
4. Tell the veterinarian about previous vaccine reactions.
5. Follow the recommended catch-up schedule.

A kitten that misses a dose may need a revised series. An adult cat with a documented vaccination history may only need a booster. Never rely on memory alone if records are unavailable; your veterinarian can help determine the safest plan.

## Cat Vaccine Side Effects and Safety

Most cats tolerate vaccination well. Mild, short-lived effects may include:

- Sleepiness
- Reduced appetite
- Mild fever
- Tenderness at the injection site
- Temporary reluctance to jump or play

These effects usually resolve without treatment. However, severe reactions require immediate veterinary attention. Emergency warning signs include:

- Facial swelling
- Hives or intense itching
- Repeated vomiting
- Severe diarrhea
- Difficulty breathing
- Weakness or collapse

Serious allergic reactions are rare, but they can occur shortly after vaccination.   

You should also monitor the injection site for a lump. Use the “3-2-1” rule: ask your veterinarian to evaluate a mass if it remains for three months, is larger than two centimeters, or continues to increase in size one month after vaccination.  

Tell your veterinarian about any previous vaccine reaction before your cat receives another dose. The clinic may adjust the vaccine type, timing, number of vaccines administered during one visit, or post-vaccination monitoring plan.

## How to Prepare for Your Cat’s Vaccine Appointment

A little preparation can make the visit less stressful.

Before the appointment:

- Locate your cat’s previous vaccine records.
- Write down the dates and types of earlier vaccines.
- Note any previous side effects.
- Record whether your cat goes outdoors.
- Mention contact with unfamiliar cats or wildlife.
- Tell the veterinarian about pregnancy, chronic illness, medications, or recent symptoms.
- Use a secure carrier with a familiar blanket.
- Avoid vaccinating a cat that is obviously ill unless your veterinarian recommends it.

After vaccination, keep your cat indoors and observe its behavior. Provide fresh water, a quiet resting area, and normal meals. Contact your veterinarian if symptoms seem severe, worsen, or last longer than expected.

## A Simple Cat Vaccination Checklist

Use this checklist to stay organized:

- Schedule the first kitten exam at 6–8 weeks.
- Start the FVRCP series on time.
- Repeat FVRCP every 3–4 weeks until at least 16 weeks.
- Ask about FeLV testing and vaccination.
- Schedule rabies according to local law.
- Book the one-year booster visit before leaving the clinic.
- Keep a written or digital vaccine record.
- Ask which vaccines are due at every wellness exam.
- Update your veterinarian when your cat’s lifestyle changes.
- Monitor your cat after every vaccination.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often do cats need vaccinations?

Kittens need multiple vaccines every 3–4 weeks until approximately 16–20 weeks of age. After the kitten series and early booster, many low-risk adult cats receive core boosters every three years, while rabies and FeLV schedules depend on local law, vaccine labeling, and lifestyle. 

### What vaccines do kittens need?

Most kittens need FVRCP, rabies, and FeLV vaccination. FVRCP protects against feline herpesvirus, calicivirus, and panleukopenia. Rabies protects against a fatal zoonotic disease, while FeLV vaccination is especially important during the first year of life.

### Can an indoor-only cat skip vaccinations?

Indoor cats should not automatically skip core vaccines. They can still encounter infectious material, escape outdoors, meet other cats, or be exposed to wildlife such as bats. Your veterinarian can create a schedule appropriate for an indoor lifestyle.

### Is FVRCP the same as feline distemper?

No. FVRCP is a combination vaccine. The “P” refers to panleukopenia, which is sometimes called feline distemper. FVRCP also protects against feline herpesvirus and calicivirus.

### Does my cat need a rabies vaccine every year?

Not necessarily. Some rabies vaccines are labeled for one year and others for three years. Local laws may require a specific interval regardless of your cat’s lifestyle. Follow the vaccine label and your local regulations.

### What if I do not know whether my adopted cat was vaccinated?

Tell your veterinarian that the vaccination history is unknown. The clinic may recommend a catch-up vaccination plan based on your cat’s age, health, risk factors, and available records.

### Can my kitten go outside after the first vaccine?

A first vaccine does not provide complete protection. Kittens should generally avoid unsupervised outdoor exposure and contact with unknown cats until they have completed the recommended series and your veterinarian confirms that their protection is adequate.

### Are vaccine reactions common in cats?

Most reactions are mild and temporary. Serious reactions are uncommon, but facial swelling, breathing difficulty, repeated vomiting, hives, or collapse require immediate veterinary care.

## Conclusion

Following a cat vaccination schedule is one of the simplest ways to protect your pet from serious infectious diseases. Start kitten vaccines at the recommended age, keep booster appointments on schedule, and remember that the final kitten FVRCP dose is particularly important.

Indoor cats still need core protection, while FeLV and other lifestyle-based vaccines should be evaluated according to exposure risk. Because vaccine recommendations vary by age, health, lifestyle, product, and local law, your veterinarian should create the final plan for your cat.

Keep accurate records, watch for unusual reactions, and review your cat’s vaccine needs at every wellness visit. A consistent vaccination plan gives your cat stronger protection and helps support a longer, healthier life.
