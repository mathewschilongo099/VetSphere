# Coccidiosis in Poultry: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Coccidiosis in Poultry is one of the most common and costly diseases affecting chickens and other farmed birds worldwide. It damages the intestines, reduces growth and egg production, and can cause high mortality in young birds if not controlled. Coccidiosis in Poultry is especially serious in floor-raised or free‑range systems where birds have constant contact with their droppings and contaminated litter.  

Good knowledge of the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of Coccidiosis in Poultry helps farmers, backyard keepers, and students plan effective control programs. With proper management, vaccination, and correct use of anticoccidial medicines, it is possible to greatly reduce losses and protect flock health.  

This article explains in clear language what Coccidiosis in Poultry is, the main species of parasites involved, how the disease spreads, typical clinical signs, how veterinarians diagnose it, and practical recommendations on how to treat Coccidiosis in Poultry and how to prevent it in both commercial and smallholder settings. 

---

## What is Coccidiosis in Poultry?

Coccidiosis in Poultry is an intestinal disease caused by microscopic protozoan parasites of the genus *Eimeria*. These parasites invade and multiply inside the cells lining the gut, causing inflammation, bleeding, poor digestion, and sometimes death.  

In chickens, several *Eimeria* species are important, including *Eimeria tenella*, *E. acervulina*, *E. maxima*, *E. necatrix*, *E. brunetti*, *E. mitis*, and *E. praecox*. Each species prefers a particular part of the intestine and causes slightly different lesions and levels of disease. *E. tenella* infects the caeca and is well known for severe caecal coccidiosis with blood‑stained droppings and high mortality. *E. acervulina* generally affects the upper small intestine, and *E. maxima* the middle intestine, often causing malabsorption and poor growth.  

These parasites have a direct life cycle, meaning there is no intermediate host. Birds become infected when they ingest infective oocysts (tiny “eggs” of the parasite) from contaminated litter, feed, water, or equipment. Inside the intestine, the parasite multiplies rapidly and destroys cells. New oocysts are then shed into the environment in the droppings, where they develop (sporulate) under warm, moist, oxygen‑rich conditions and become infective for other birds.

## Causes of Coccidiosis in Poultry

The primary cause of Coccidiosis in Poultry is ingestion of sporulated oocysts of *Eimeria* species present in the environment. The source is always contaminated faeces. Birds pick up the parasite when they peck at litter, drink contaminated water, eat contaminated feed, or contact dirty equipment, boots, or transport crates.  

Several management and environmental factors increase the risk and severity of coccidiosis:

1. **Poor litter management and wet floors**  
   Moisture and warmth in the litter promote sporulation of oocysts, making them infective. Wet patches under drinkers, leaking pipes, and poor drainage create ideal conditions for coccidia to multiply. High humidity in the house also contributes to oocyst survival and spread.  

2. **Overcrowding and high stocking density**  
   High bird numbers in a small area mean more droppings per square metre, more oocysts in the litter, and greater chance of birds ingesting a large infective dose. Crowding also makes it harder to keep litter dry and to provide uniform access to feeders and drinkers.  

3. **Poor biosecurity and hygiene**  
   Coccidia oocysts are tough and can survive in the environment for a long time. They are spread mechanically on boots, equipment, vehicles, rodents, wild birds, and insects. Inadequate cleaning and disinfection between flocks, and uncontrolled access of visitors or other animals, introduce and spread infection between houses or farms.  

4. **Lack of immunity or sudden changes in exposure**  
   Young birds and naïve flocks without previous exposure or vaccination are very susceptible. Sudden increases in environmental contamination—for example, when litter becomes wet after a water spill—can overwhelm the birds’ developing immunity and cause clinical disease, even in older birds.  

5. **Concurrent diseases and poor nutrition**  
   Other gut pathogens, such as *Clostridium* or *Salmonella*, and nutritional deficiencies can worsen intestinal damage and make coccidiosis more severe. Birds under stress or with weak immunity are less able to control the infection.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Coccidiosis in Poultry

The symptoms of Coccidiosis in Poultry depend on the *Eimeria* species, the infection level, the age and immunity of the birds, and whether other diseases are present. In general, coccidiosis affects the intestines, so the most common signs are related to digestion and general condition.  

Common clinical signs and symptoms of Coccidiosis in Poultry include:

- Depression, drooping wings, and birds standing apart from the flock  
- Ruffled feathers and a hunched or tired appearance  
- Reduced feed intake and poor appetite  
- Weight loss or failure to gain weight as expected  
- Watery, mucoid, or foul‑smelling diarrhoea  
- Blood‑stained droppings, especially with *E. tenella* (caecal coccidiosis)  
- Pale combs and wattles due to blood loss and anaemia  
- Dehydration, sunken eyes, and increased thirst  
- Sudden death in severe outbreaks, especially in young chicks  

In laying hens, coccidiosis may cause a drop in egg production, poor eggshell quality, and reduced egg weight because damaged intestines cannot absorb nutrients efficiently. Subclinical infections, where birds show few obvious signs, are also important; they cause reduced feed conversion, poor growth, uneven bodyweights, and increased susceptibility to other intestinal diseases.  

It is important to remember that similar signs—diarrhoea, poor growth, depression—can also be caused by other diseases, such as necrotic enteritis, worm burdens, bacterial enteritis, and viral infections. Therefore, confirmation by proper diagnostic methods is essential before specific treatment is started.

## How to Diagnose Coccidiosis in Poultry

Diagnosis of Coccidiosis in Poultry is based on a combination of flock history, clinical signs, post‑mortem lesions, and laboratory tests. A veterinarian or poultry diagnostic laboratory is usually required to confirm the disease and identify the main *Eimeria* species involved.  

Key diagnostic steps include:

1. **History and clinical examination**  
   The vet will look at bird age, type (broiler, layer, breeder), stocking density, litter condition, vaccination and medication programs, and recent changes in management. Sudden diarrhoea, blood in droppings, poor growth, and mortality in young birds raised on litter strongly suggest coccidiosis.  

2. **Post‑mortem examination (necropsy)**  
   Examination of freshly dead or euthanised birds is essential. Characteristic lesions in specific parts of the gut help identify the species:  
   - *E. tenella*: swollen, bloody caeca, sometimes with “cecal cores” (clotted blood, tissue debris, and oocysts).  
   - *E. necatrix*: ballooned mid‑small intestine with “salt and pepper” white and red spots.  
   - *E. acervulina*: white plaques or ladder‑like lines in the upper small intestine.  
   - *E. maxima*: orange, mucoid intestinal contents, sometimes with blood, in the mid‑intestine.  

   Different lesion scoring systems (e.g., Johnson & Reid) are used to rate severity and help distinguish species based on intestinal location and appearance.  

3. **Microscopic examination of faeces or intestinal scrapings**  
   Fecal flotation or oocyst counts (oocysts per gram, OPG) are used to detect and estimate the level of coccidial oocyst shedding. Intestinal scrapings from specific gut segments may be examined for developmental stages of *Eimeria* (merozoites, schizonts). Presence of large numbers of oocysts along with characteristic lesions supports a diagnosis of clinical coccidiosis.  

In subclinical cases, diagnosis may focus on poor performance data, lesion scoring at slaughter, and periodic faecal oocyst counts. This helps adjust prevention programs before full disease outbreaks occur.

## Treatment of Coccidiosis in Poultry

Knowing how to treat Coccidiosis in Poultry quickly and correctly is essential to reduce deaths and production losses. Once coccidiosis is diagnosed or strongly suspected, treatment should begin immediately under veterinary guidance.  

Main treatment options include:

1. **Anticoccidial medicines (coccidiostats and coccidiocides)**  
   - **Amprolium** is a commonly used anticoccidial drug given in drinking water. It works by blocking thiamine (vitamin B1) uptake in coccidia, stopping their multiplication. It is widely used in backyard and small flocks because it is effective and often available over the counter in many countries. 
   - **Sulfonamides (sulfa drugs)** such as sulfamethazine and sulfadimethoxine may be used, particularly in severe cases or when mixed infections are suspected. These often require veterinary prescription and careful attention to withdrawal times before meat or eggs are consumed. 
   - In commercial operations, a range of feed‑based anticoccidial products (ionophores like monensin, salinomycin; and chemical coccidiostats such as nicarbazin, diclazuril, robenidine, clopidol) are used for prevention, and sometimes therapeutically according to label instructions.  

   The choice of drug depends on bird type, age, severity of disease, local regulations, and existing anticoccidial programs. Rotating or “shuttling” between different products is common to help delay development of resistance.  

2. **Supportive therapy and flock management**  
   Treatment of Coccidiosis in Poultry is not only about killing the parasites. Supporting the birds during recovery is also critical:  
   - Ensure easy access to clean, fresh water at all times; use electrolytes if birds are dehydrated.  
   - Maintain good ventilation and comfortable temperature to reduce stress.  
   - Reduce litter moisture by adding dry bedding, improving drainage, and fixing leaks.  
   - Provide high‑quality feed with adequate energy, protein, vitamins, and minerals to help repair the damaged gut.  

3. **Monitoring and follow‑up**  
   After starting treatment, farmers should monitor droppings, bird behaviour, feed and water intake, and mortality daily. If birds fail to improve or if mortality continues to rise, the veterinarian may adjust the medication, check for secondary infections, or investigate other possible diseases.  

Always follow label directions and veterinary advice regarding dosage, duration of treatment, and withdrawal periods to avoid drug residues in meat and eggs.

## Prevention and Control of Coccidiosis in Poultry

The most effective approach to Coccidiosis in Poultry is prevention. Once the parasite is established in the environment, complete elimination is almost impossible, but its impact can be greatly reduced by combining good husbandry, biosecurity, vaccination, and strategic use of anticoccidials.  

Key pillars of prevention of Coccidiosis in Poultry include:

1. **Litter and environmental management**  
   - Keep litter dry and well‑fluffed; regularly remove wet or caked areas, especially around drinkers.  
   - Provide adequate ventilation to control humidity and ammonia levels.  
   - Maintain appropriate stocking density to reduce faecal contamination and stress.  
   - Thoroughly clean and, where possible, disinfect houses and equipment between flocks. Some disinfectants have limited effect on oocysts, but good washing, drying, and exposure to high temperatures or freezing can reduce their numbers.  

2. **Biosecurity**  
   - Restrict access to poultry houses; use dedicated footwear and clothing.  
   - Control rodents, wild birds, and insects which may carry oocysts mechanically.  
   - Clean and disinfect tools, crates, and vehicles moving between farms.  
   - Implement “all‑in, all‑out” systems where possible to allow full cleaning and resting of houses between flocks.  

3. **Vaccination**  
   Vaccination is now a major tool in the prevention of Coccidiosis in Poultry. Live coccidiosis vaccines containing one or several attenuated or non‑attenuated *Eimeria* species are administered to day‑old chicks by spray, gel, or drinking water, mainly in hatcheries or early on the rearing farm. This controlled exposure allows birds to develop strong, species‑specific immunity while minimizing clinical disease.  

   For an effective vaccine program:  
   - Give vaccine uniformly to all chicks at the recommended age.  
   - Allow birds to be re‑exposed to their own oocysts in the litter for 3–4 weeks to build stable immunity.  
   - Avoid using certain anticoccidial drugs immediately after vaccination, as they may interfere with vaccine strain cycling.  

4. **Strategic use of anticoccidials**  
   In intensive systems, anticoccidial drugs are routinely included in feed to prevent clinical disease. Rotational and shuttle programs using different products in different flocks or growth stages help manage resistance. However, continuous drug use has led to widespread resistance, so many producers now integrate vaccination to reduce dependence on chemicals and ionophores.  

Integrated coccidiosis control—combining vaccination, biosecurity, good litter management, nutrition, and careful use of anticoccidials—offers the most sustainable and cost‑effective way to protect poultry health and productivity.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Coccidiosis in Poultry

### Q: What causes Coccidiosis in Poultry?

**A:** Coccidiosis in Poultry is caused by protozoan parasites of the genus *Eimeria*. Birds become infected when they ingest sporulated oocysts from contaminated litter, feed, water, or equipment. The parasites invade the cells lining the intestine, multiply rapidly, and damage the gut, leading to diarrhoea, poor growth, and sometimes death. 

### Q: What are the main symptoms of Coccidiosis in Poultry?

**A:** Typical symptoms of Coccidiosis in Poultry include depression, ruffled feathers, reduced appetite, poor growth, diarrhoea (often watery or mucoid), and sometimes blood‑stained droppings. Birds may show pale combs and wattles from blood loss, dehydration, and increased mortality, especially in severe *Eimeria tenella* outbreaks. In laying hens, a drop in egg production can also occur. 

### Q: How do you treat Coccidiosis in Poultry?

**A:** Treatment of Coccidiosis in Poultry usually involves anticoccidial medicines such as amprolium in drinking water or sulfonamide drugs under veterinary guidance. In commercial flocks, specific feed‑based anticoccidials may also be used according to label directions. Supportive care—clean water, good nutrition, dry litter, and comfortable housing—is essential to help birds recover. Always follow veterinary advice and observe withdrawal periods. 

### Q: How can I prevent Coccidiosis in my chickens?

**A:** Prevention of Coccidiosis in Poultry relies on dry, clean litter; good ventilation; appropriate stocking density; strong biosecurity; and, in many cases, vaccination. Live coccidiosis vaccines for day‑old chicks help birds develop natural immunity. Strategic use of anticoccidial feed additives, rotation of products to slow resistance, and strict hygiene between flocks all play important roles. 

### Q: Is Coccidiosis in Poultry contagious to humans or other animals?

**A:** The *Eimeria* species that cause Coccidiosis in Poultry are host‑specific, meaning the chicken coccidia do not infect humans and generally do not infect other animal species. However, poor hygiene associated with heavily contaminated poultry environments can encourage other diseases, so good personal and farm hygiene remain important for human health and animal welfare. 

### Q: Can older birds get coccidiosis, or is it only a problem in chicks?

**A:** Coccidiosis in Poultry is most common and severe in young birds which have not yet developed immunity. However, older birds can still be affected, particularly if they were not properly vaccinated, if management conditions suddenly worsen (for example, very wet litter), or if they are stressed or immunosuppressed. Subclinical coccidiosis in older birds may reduce egg production and feed efficiency without obvious signs of disease.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Farmers and backyard poultry keepers should contact a veterinarian promptly if they observe the following warning signs that may indicate Coccidiosis in Poultry:

- Sudden onset of diarrhoea or blood‑stained droppings, especially in young birds raised on litter  
- Rapid increase in mortality or a noticeable number of weak, depressed birds  
- Poor weight gain, uneven growth, or a drop in feed intake across the flock  
- A sudden fall in egg production or deterioration in eggshell quality without an obvious management change  

A veterinarian can perform or arrange for post‑mortem examinations and laboratory tests to confirm coccidiosis, identify the main *Eimeria* species involved, and check for other concurrent diseases. They will design an appropriate treatment and prevention plan, including selection of anticoccidial drugs, adjustments to vaccination programs, and improvements in management and biosecurity.  

Early veterinary involvement improves outcomes by allowing rapid, targeted therapy and helps avoid misuse of medications, drug resistance problems, and unnecessary economic losses. For commercial farms, regular veterinary monitoring of lesion scores, oocyst counts, and production data is a key part of long‑term coccidiosis control.

## Conclusion

Coccidiosis in Poultry is a major intestinal disease of chickens and other birds, caused by host‑specific *Eimeria* parasites that attack the gut lining. It leads to diarrhoea, weight loss, reduced performance, and sometimes high mortality, particularly in young birds and in poorly managed environments. Because the parasites are widespread and their oocysts survive well in warm, moist litter, almost all floor‑raised poultry are exposed at some stage.  

Understanding the causes of Coccidiosis in Poultry, recognizing the early symptoms, and knowing how to diagnose and how to treat Coccidiosis in Poultry are essential skills for farmers, students, and poultry professionals. Anticoccidial medicines, combined with good husbandry, support many flocks through outbreaks, but long‑term success depends on integrated prevention—dry litter, strong biosecurity, appropriate stocking densities, effective vaccination, and strategic use of anticoccidials to manage resistance.  

By applying these principles, producers can reduce the impact of Coccidiosis in Poultry, protect bird welfare, and maintain profitable, sustainable poultry production in both commercial and backyard systems.
