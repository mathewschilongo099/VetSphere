Coccidiosis in poultry is one of the most common and costly intestinal diseases affecting chickens, turkeys, and other domestic birds. It can appear as mild poor growth and reduced feed efficiency, or progress rapidly to bloody diarrhea, severe intestinal damage, dehydration, and death.

The disease is caused by microscopic parasites called *Eimeria*. These parasites multiply inside intestinal cells, damaging the gut lining and interfering with nutrient absorption. Because coccidiosis can spread quickly through contaminated litter, feed, water, footwear, and equipment, early recognition and consistent prevention are essential.   

Whether you manage a commercial poultry farm, backyard flock, or small homestead, understanding coccidiosis in chickens can help you protect bird health, reduce production losses, and respond quickly when problems develop.

## What Is Coccidiosis in Poultry?

Coccidiosis is a parasitic disease caused primarily by species of the genus *Eimeria*. These single-celled protozoan parasites invade specific areas of the intestinal tract, reproduce inside intestinal cells, and release new infective stages into the environment.

Most poultry coccidia are host-specific. This means the *Eimeria* species that affect chickens generally do not infect turkeys, ducks, or mammals in the same way. Different parasite species also prefer different parts of the intestine and produce different types of lesions.   

In chickens, important species include:

- *Eimeria acervulina*
- *Eimeria maxima*
- *Eimeria tenella*
- *Eimeria necatrix*
- *Eimeria brunetti*
- *Eimeria mitis*
- *Eimeria praecox*

Some species mainly cause poor growth and reduced feed conversion, while others can cause severe hemorrhagic disease and high mortality.

## How Does Poultry Coccidiosis Spread?

Coccidiosis spreads through the fecal-oral route. Infected birds shed immature oocysts, which are parasite eggs, in their droppings. Under suitable conditions, these oocysts mature, or sporulate, in litter, soil, feed, and water.

A susceptible bird becomes infected after swallowing sporulated oocysts. This commonly happens when birds peck at contaminated litter or consume feed and water contaminated with manure.

The life cycle is rapid. Depending on the species, new oocysts may be shed within approximately four to seven days after infection. This allows the parasite population to multiply quickly in crowded or poorly managed poultry houses.   

Oocysts can also be moved mechanically by:

- Farm workers’ boots and clothing
- Shovels, crates, and other equipment
- Rodents, insects, and wild birds
- Shared feeders and drinkers
- Contaminated vehicles
- Movement of birds between pens or houses

Wild birds may carry oocysts mechanically, but poultry *Eimeria* parasites are generally host-specific, so wild birds are not usually a biological reservoir for chicken coccidia. 

## Which Birds Are Most at Risk?

Young poultry are at the greatest risk of clinical coccidiosis. Chicks are generally not susceptible during the first 10 to 14 days of life, but disease commonly develops after exposure increases and the parasite population builds up. Backyard poultry are often affected between one and four months of age.  

Risk increases when birds are kept in:

- Wet or heavily soiled litter
- Overcrowded housing
- Poorly ventilated buildings
- Warm, humid conditions
- Pens with leaking drinkers
- Housing with inadequate bedding
- Areas contaminated by previous flocks

Birds that are stressed, poorly nourished, immunosuppressed, or affected by another disease may develop more severe coccidiosis. Concurrent infections can also make intestinal damage worse.

Although young birds are most vulnerable, older chickens are not completely protected. Adult layers and breeders may become sick if immunity is inadequate, if they are exposed to a different *Eimeria* species, or if management conditions suddenly increase their exposure.

## Common Signs of Coccidiosis in Chickens

The signs of coccidiosis depend on the parasite species, number of oocysts ingested, age of the birds, immune status, and overall flock conditions.

Watch for the following symptoms:

- Watery or mucoid diarrhea
- Bloody droppings
- Ruffled feathers
- Lethargy and depression
- Huddling or reluctance to move
- Reduced appetite
- Reduced water consumption
- Weight loss
- Pale combs and wattles
- Dehydration
- Poor growth and uneven flock size
- Reduced egg production
- Increased mortality
- Soiling around the vent

Mild or subclinical coccidiosis may not cause obvious diarrhea. Instead, the flock may show poor feed conversion, slow growth, reduced uniformity, or a gradual increase in culls. These hidden production losses can be economically significant even when mortality is low.   

Blood in the droppings is especially concerning. It may occur with severe infection involving the ceca or lower intestine, particularly with *Eimeria tenella* or *Eimeria necatrix*. Birds may also become pale because of blood loss and intestinal damage.

## Important *Eimeria* Species in Chickens

### *Eimeria tenella*

This parasite primarily affects the ceca. Severe infections can cause enlarged, hemorrhagic ceca filled with blood, clotted material, and tissue debris. Affected chickens may pass bloody droppings, become weak, and die rapidly.

### *Eimeria necatrix*

*Eimeria necatrix* affects the middle portion of the small intestine and can cause severe hemorrhage. At necropsy, the intestinal surface may have a distinctive “salt-and-pepper” appearance, with white areas mixed with red hemorrhagic spots. 

### *Eimeria acervulina*

This is a common cause of intestinal coccidiosis. It affects the upper small intestine and may produce white, elongated plaques or ladder-like lesions. Birds may show poor growth, watery droppings, and reduced feed efficiency.

### *Eimeria maxima*

This species affects the small intestine and may cause intestinal thickening, dilation, petechial hemorrhages, and pink, orange, or reddish mucus. It is also associated with poor growth and an increased risk of secondary intestinal disease.

### *Eimeria brunetti*

This parasite commonly affects the lower small intestine, rectum, ceca, and cloaca. Severe infection can cause extensive mucosal damage, necrosis, and sloughing of the intestinal lining.

### *Eimeria mitis* and *Eimeria praecox*

These species may produce less obvious intestinal lesions but can still reduce growth, nutrient absorption, and production performance. A flock does not need to show dramatic bloody diarrhea to be affected by coccidiosis.

Turkeys are affected by different *Eimeria* species, including *Eimeria adenoeides*, *Eimeria dispersa*, *Eimeria gallopavonis*, and *Eimeria meleagrimitis*. These parasites can cause intestinal disease, poor growth, diarrhea, and mortality in young poults. 

## How Is Coccidiosis Diagnosed?

A veterinarian may diagnose poultry coccidiosis using a combination of:

1. Clinical signs  
2. Flock age and management history  
3. Fecal examination  
4. Postmortem examination  
5. Intestinal lesion scoring  
6. Microscopic examination of intestinal scrapings  
7. Oocyst identification or molecular testing  

Fecal flotation can detect *Eimeria* oocysts. However, finding oocysts does not automatically prove that coccidiosis is causing disease. Many poultry flocks carry some coccidia without showing clinical illness, and oocyst numbers may not directly match disease severity.   

For this reason, a veterinarian should interpret fecal results alongside:

- The number of sick or dead birds
- The age of the flock
- Growth and feed-conversion records
- The appearance of droppings
- Intestinal lesions
- Recent medication or vaccination history
- Litter moisture and housing conditions

If birds are dying, submit fresh carcasses for necropsy as soon as possible. Examining several birds is often more useful than examining only one, because coccidiosis may occur together with bacterial infections, nutritional problems, worms, viruses, or necrotic enteritis.

## Coccidiosis and Necrotic Enteritis

Coccidiosis can damage the intestinal lining and make birds more vulnerable to secondary bacterial disease. One important complication is necrotic enteritis, which is associated with overgrowth of *Clostridium perfringens*. Severe necrotic enteritis can cause rapid mortality in broilers.  

If a flock has sudden deaths, depression, severe diarrhea, or poor response to a coccidiosis treatment, do not assume coccidia are the only problem. Veterinary testing is important because treating the wrong disease can delay effective control.

## Treatment of Coccidiosis in Poultry

Treatment should be guided by a veterinarian and based on the bird species, production purpose, disease severity, and locally approved products.

Anticoccidial medicines may be administered through feed or drinking water. Preventive medications are commonly incorporated into feed, while water medication may be used during an active outbreak because it can be started quickly and does not require replacing the entire feed supply.  

Treatment may include:

- An approved anticoccidial medication
- Clean, easily accessible drinking water
- Supportive care for dehydration
- Correction of wet litter and ventilation problems
- Veterinary treatment for secondary bacterial infections
- Removal of severely contaminated bedding
- Close monitoring of mortality and feed and water intake

Do not guess at dosage or combine products without professional advice. Some medications are appropriate for broilers but not laying hens. Others may have restrictions for turkeys, young birds, meat birds, or birds producing eggs for human consumption.

Sulfa drugs, for example, may have long withdrawal periods and should not be used in laying hens unless specifically approved and prescribed for that purpose.  

Always follow the product label, veterinary instructions, and local regulations. Record the medication name, dosage, dates of treatment, and withdrawal period.

## Prevention of Coccidiosis in Poultry

The best approach to coccidiosis is prevention. Once clinical signs appear, significant intestinal damage may already have occurred.

### Keep Litter Dry

Wet litter allows oocysts to sporulate and become infective. Check the areas below drinkers, around feeders, and in corners of the house every day.

Practical steps include:

- Repair leaking drinkers immediately.
- Remove wet or caked litter.
- Add fresh, dry bedding.
- Improve ventilation without creating drafts.
- Prevent water spills.
- Adjust drinker height and pressure.
- Avoid excessive stocking density.

Dry, loose litter helps dilute manure and reduces the number of infective oocysts consumed when birds peck at the floor. 

### Control Stocking Density

Overcrowding increases manure contamination, competition around feed and water, and stress. It also makes it harder to keep litter dry.

Use a stocking density appropriate for your bird type, housing system, climate, and ventilation capacity. If litter becomes wet or caked quickly, your flock may be too large for the available space or the housing system may need improvement.

### Use Biosecurity

Because oocysts are easily transported mechanically, biosecurity is essential.

- Use dedicated footwear for each poultry house.
- Clean boots before entering another flock area.
- Keep visitors away from poultry housing.
- Disinfect equipment between pens.
- Control rodents and insects.
- Avoid sharing feeders, crates, and tools.
- Quarantine new birds.
- Remove dead birds promptly.
- Clean and dry houses between flocks.

Common disinfectants may not reliably destroy coccidial oocysts. Cleaning, drying, litter removal, and effective flock separation are therefore important parts of a prevention program.  

### Consider Vaccination

Live coccidiosis vaccines expose birds to controlled strains of *Eimeria* and help them develop species-specific immunity. Vaccination programs are used in commercial poultry and may be available for chicks from selected hatcheries.

Vaccinated chicks should not automatically receive medicated feed containing coccidiostats. Certain anticoccidial products can interfere with the vaccine’s ability to cycle and establish immunity.  

Follow the hatchery’s instructions carefully, especially regarding brooding, litter conditions, feed, and flock management.

### Use Anticoccidials Strategically

Coccidiostats and coccidicides can help prevent clinical disease and reduce production losses. Common products used in poultry production include compounds such as amprolium, decoquinate, diclazuril, lasalocid, monensin, narasin, nicarbazin, and salinomycin, depending on local approval and bird category.  

Continuous use of the same product can encourage drug resistance. Veterinary-managed rotation or shuttle programs may help preserve effectiveness, but changing medications should be based on flock history, monitoring, and professional advice.  

Never use human medicines, unapproved products, or leftover medication without veterinary guidance.

## Monitoring Your Poultry Flock

Early detection can prevent a small problem from becoming a serious outbreak. Keep daily records of:

- Mortality
- Feed intake
- Water intake
- Body weight
- Egg production
- Dropping appearance
- Litter condition
- Medication use
- Flock uniformity

Walk through the flock at least once daily. Look for birds that are isolated, hunched, pale, ruffled, or reluctant to eat. Pay special attention to sudden changes in manure, especially blood or excessive mucus.

Routine fecal examinations can also be useful for backyard flocks, particularly when birds have recurring diarrhea, poor growth, or unexplained weight loss. 

## Frequently Asked Questions About Coccidiosis in Poultry

### Can coccidiosis kill chickens?

Yes. Severe coccidiosis can cause intestinal bleeding, dehydration, anemia, weakness, and death. Young birds are particularly vulnerable, although birds of any age can become ill under the right conditions.

### Is coccidiosis contagious?

Coccidiosis spreads readily through contaminated feces, litter, feed, water, footwear, and equipment. It is not spread by casual airborne transmission in the same way as some respiratory diseases, but contaminated materials can move the parasite rapidly through a flock.

### What does coccidiosis poop look like?

Droppings may be watery, loose, mucoid, orange, reddish, or bloody. However, abnormal droppings are not specific to coccidiosis. Worms, bacterial disease, feed changes, toxins, and other intestinal conditions can look similar.

### Can adult chickens get coccidiosis?

Yes. Adult chickens often develop partial immunity after exposure, but immunity is species-specific and may not protect them against every *Eimeria* species. Adult birds can also become sick after severe environmental contamination or immune stress.

### Does medicated chick feed prevent coccidiosis?

Medicated feed may reduce the risk of clinical coccidiosis when it contains an approved anticoccidial used correctly. It is not a substitute for dry litter, adequate space, ventilation, sanitation, and good biosecurity.

### Can I treat coccidiosis with antibiotics?

Antibiotics do not directly kill *Eimeria*, because coccidiosis is caused by protozoal parasites. A veterinarian may prescribe antibiotics when secondary bacterial infections are suspected, but an appropriate anticoccidial is needed to address the primary disease.

### Should I use coccidiostats with a coccidiosis vaccine?

Not automatically. Some coccidiostats can interfere with live vaccine cycling and immunity development. Follow the vaccine manufacturer’s instructions and consult a poultry veterinarian before combining programs.

### How quickly should I call a veterinarian?

Contact a veterinarian immediately if birds have bloody droppings, sudden deaths, severe weakness, dehydration, rapidly increasing mortality, or a sharp decline in feed, water, or egg production.

## Conclusion

Coccidiosis in poultry is more than a problem involving diarrhea. It can damage the intestinal lining, reduce nutrient absorption, slow growth, worsen feed conversion, increase susceptibility to necrotic enteritis, and cause serious mortality.

The most effective strategy combines dry litter, proper ventilation, sensible stocking density, strict biosecurity, flock monitoring, vaccination when appropriate, and carefully managed anticoccidial programs. If you notice blood in droppings, poor growth, or unexplained deaths, act quickly and seek veterinary assistance.

Early diagnosis and a prevention-focused management plan are the keys to keeping your poultry flock healthy and productive.
