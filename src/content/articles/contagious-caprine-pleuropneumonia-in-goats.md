A goat that suddenly develops a high fever, painful breathing, and a harsh cough may have more than an ordinary respiratory infection. Contagious caprine pleuropneumonia, commonly called **CCPP**, is one of the most serious diseases affecting goats. It can spread rapidly through a herd and may cause severe illness, sudden death, and major economic losses.

CCPP is caused by the bacterium *Mycoplasma capricolum* subsp. *capripneumoniae*, often shortened to **Mccp**. The disease primarily affects goats, although sheep and some wild ruminants may also be infected in certain situations. In susceptible herds, illness rates can approach 100%, while death rates may reach 80% or higher.   

If you suspect contagious caprine pleuropneumonia in goats, treat the situation as an emergency. Isolate affected animals, stop animal movement, and contact a veterinarian or animal-health authority immediately.

## What Is Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia?

Contagious caprine pleuropneumonia is a severe bacterial disease of the lungs and pleura. The pleura are the thin membranes surrounding the lungs. During infection, inflammation can cause pneumonia, fibrin deposits, and a large buildup of fluid in the chest cavity.

The disease was formerly associated with *Mycoplasma* type F-38. Today, the recognized causative organism is *Mycoplasma capricolum* subsp. *capripneumoniae*. It belongs to a group of bacteria that lack a conventional cell wall, which is important when veterinarians select appropriate antimicrobial drugs.  

CCPP is different from other goat diseases caused by related *Mycoplasma* organisms. Some related infections may cause mastitis, arthritis, eye disease, septicemia, or neurological symptoms. Classical CCPP is primarily confined to the respiratory system, producing severe pleuropneumonia.  

The disease is reported mainly in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Middle East. Its distribution is not completely understood because the bacterium can be difficult to isolate and some reports are based on clinical signs rather than laboratory confirmation. 

## How Does CCPP Spread?

Contagious caprine pleuropneumonia spreads mainly through **infectious respiratory droplets and aerosols**. When an infected goat coughs, sneezes, or breathes heavily, nearby susceptible goats may inhale the organism.

Transmission is most likely when goats are:

- Housed closely together
- Transported over long distances
- Gathered at markets or livestock fairs
- Kept in poorly ventilated shelters
- Mixed with newly purchased animals
- Exposed to stressful weather or sudden climate changes

Direct nose-to-nose contact is especially risky. The infection can move quickly through a herd because goats often share sleeping areas, feeding spaces, water points, and transport vehicles.

The typical incubation period is approximately **6 to 10 days**, although reported incubation periods may range from 2 days to 4 weeks. This means a goat can appear healthy when introduced to a herd and develop clinical disease later.   

Long-term carrier animals remain a concern, but the importance and frequency of chronic carriage are not fully established. Outbreaks have sometimes followed the introduction of apparently healthy goats, so relying only on visual inspection is unsafe. 

## Which Animals Are Most at Risk?

Goats of any age, sex, or breed can become infected. However, animals with no previous exposure may experience especially severe disease.

Risk increases when goats are:

- Purchased from areas where CCPP occurs
- Transported or mixed with unfamiliar animals
- Kept in crowded or damp housing
- Stressed by poor nutrition
- Exposed to extreme heat, cold, or sudden weather changes
- Introduced without quarantine
- Housed with goats showing coughing or nasal discharge

Goats are the primary host. Sheep may become infected in mixed goat-and-sheep outbreaks, and infection has also been documented in some wild ruminants, including wild goats, Nubian ibex, mouflon, gerenuk, and certain gazelles.  

CCPP is **not considered a zoonotic disease**. There is no evidence that *M. capripneumoniae* infects humans. Nevertheless, normal hygiene practices, gloves, and careful handling of sick or dead animals are always recommended. 

## Symptoms of Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia in Goats

CCPP can occur in peracute, acute, subacute, or chronic forms. The severity and speed of disease may vary between outbreaks.

### Peracute CCPP

In peracute cases, goats may die within 1 to 3 days with few obvious warning signs. Sudden death may be the first indication that a highly contagious respiratory disease is present.

### Acute CCPP

Acute disease often begins with:

- Very high fever
- Depression and weakness
- Loss of appetite
- Reluctance to move
- Separation from the herd

After a short period, respiratory signs become prominent. These may include:

- Frequent or violent coughing
- Rapid breathing
- Labored breathing
- Painful breathing
- Nasal discharge
- Open-mouth breathing
- Frothy saliva
- Grunting or bleating because of chest pain
- A stiff, extended neck
- Standing with the front legs held away from the body

A goat with severe respiratory distress may use its abdomen to breathe. It may refuse to walk because movement worsens the pain. In advanced cases, the animal can collapse and die from respiratory failure.

Pregnant goats may abort. Acutely affected goats commonly die within 7 to 10 days after clinical signs appear, although the course can vary.  

### Chronic CCPP

Chronic cases may be less dramatic but still important for herd health. Signs can include:

- Persistent cough
- Recurrent nasal discharge
- Weight loss
- Poor body condition
- Weakness
- Reduced growth
- Exercise intolerance

A goat that appears to have “recovered” may continue to have permanent lung damage. Chronic lesions can include pleural adhesions and encapsulated areas of infection. Such animals may require ongoing veterinary assessment before they are returned to the herd.

## What Do CCPP Lung Lesions Look Like?

The most characteristic lesions are found in the lungs and pleural cavity.

At post-mortem examination, a veterinarian may find:

- Severe fibrinous pneumonia
- One-sided or occasionally two-sided lung consolidation
- Large amounts of straw-colored fluid in the chest
- Pleuritis, or inflammation of the pleura
- Adhesions between the lungs and chest wall
- Enlarged bronchial or regional lymph nodes
- Areas of necrosis
- Firm, liver-like sections of consolidated lung

These findings can strongly suggest CCPP, especially during an outbreak. However, post-mortem findings alone cannot reliably distinguish CCPP from every other serious goat respiratory disease.  

Do not perform an unplanned necropsy yourself. Opening the carcass or moving it without veterinary guidance can increase contamination risks and may violate disease-reporting requirements.

## How Is CCPP Diagnosed?

Clinical signs can raise suspicion, but laboratory testing is needed for confirmation. CCPP can resemble several other diseases, including:

- Pasteurellosis
- Peste des petits ruminants
- Other *Mycoplasma* infections
- Contagious agalactia
- Severe bacterial pneumonia
- Viral respiratory disease
- Parasitic or environmental respiratory conditions

The preferred diagnostic methods include:

### PCR Testing

Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, can detect Mccp genetic material directly from appropriate samples. Useful samples may include:

- Pleural fluid
- Affected lung tissue
- Exudate from active lesions
- Regional lymph nodes

PCR is particularly valuable because Mccp is fastidious and difficult to grow in routine culture. WOAH identifies PCR as the preferred assay for identifying *M. capripneumoniae* in suitable clinical material. 

### Bacterial Culture

Culture of Mccp from lung tissue or pleural fluid can provide confirmatory evidence. However, the organism requires special media, careful sample handling, and extended incubation. A negative routine bacterial culture does not rule out CCPP. 

### Serology

Serological tests, including ELISA and other antibody-based tests, may help with herd-level surveillance. They are less useful for confirming an individual goat during the early phase of illness because antibodies may not be detectable immediately. Cross-reactions with related *Mycoplasma* organisms can also complicate interpretation.  

Your veterinarian may collect samples from several animals because herd-level testing often provides a clearer picture than testing only one sick goat.

## Treatment for Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia

CCPP requires urgent veterinary treatment. Early antimicrobial therapy may reduce deaths, but treatment does not guarantee that the organism will be eliminated from every infected animal.

Antimicrobials that may be considered by a veterinarian include:

- Macrolides
- Tetracyclines
- Florfenicol
- Certain fluoroquinolones

Merck Veterinary Manual reports that early treatment may include tylosin or long-acting oxytetracycline, but drug selection, dosage, withdrawal periods, and legal use must be determined by a veterinarian.  

Never treat an entire herd with leftover medication or use human antibiotics without professional direction. Incorrect treatment can delay diagnosis, cause drug residues in meat or milk, and contribute to antimicrobial resistance.

Supportive care may include:

- Providing clean water
- Offering palatable feed
- Minimizing handling and exercise
- Keeping the goat in a well-ventilated, dry area
- Managing fever and pain under veterinary direction
- Monitoring breathing rate and effort
- Treating dehydration when appropriate

Goats with severe breathing difficulty may deteriorate quickly even after treatment begins. Humane-euthanasia decisions may be necessary when suffering is severe and recovery is unlikely.

## What to Do if You Suspect CCPP

Take immediate action if one or more goats develop high fever, violent coughing, or severe breathing difficulty.

1. **Isolate sick goats** from the rest of the herd.
2. **Stop movement** of goats, sheep, equipment, and people between groups.
3. **Contact your veterinarian** before collecting samples or administering medication.
4. **Record clinical signs**, temperatures, deaths, recent purchases, and animal movements.
5. **Avoid mixing exposed goats** with apparently healthy animals.
6. **Use dedicated clothing, footwear, buckets, and tools** for the sick group.
7. **Ask about official reporting requirements** in your country or region.
8. **Do not sell, transport, or slaughter suspect animals** without veterinary guidance.

CCPP is a serious transboundary animal disease, and authorities may require quarantine, testing, movement controls, or other outbreak measures.

## Preventing Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia

Prevention is far easier and less costly than managing an outbreak. Your herd biosecurity plan should focus on preventing infected goats from entering the property.

### Quarantine New Goats

Keep new or returning goats separate from your established herd for several weeks. Quarantine should include:

- A separate enclosure
- Separate feed and water equipment
- No nose-to-nose contact
- Daily observation
- Temperature checks when advised
- Veterinary examination and testing when appropriate

Quarantine is essential even when a goat appears healthy because the incubation period may last days or weeks.

### Buy Carefully

Ask where a goat came from and whether respiratory disease has occurred in its source herd. Avoid purchasing animals from unknown markets or herds with recent unexplained deaths.

### Improve Ventilation

Good airflow helps reduce the concentration of infectious respiratory droplets. Housing should be dry, clean, and large enough to avoid overcrowding. Avoid placing sick goats in poorly ventilated sheds.

### Reduce Stress

Transport, extreme weather, poor nutrition, and overcrowding can increase the risk of disease spread and worsen clinical signs. Plan animal movement carefully and provide adequate water, feed, shade, and shelter.

### Clean and Disinfect

Remove organic material before applying disinfectant. Clean housing, trailers, handling areas, buckets, and tools after an outbreak. Many routine disinfectants can inactivate mycoplasmas, but products must be used at the correct concentration and contact time. 

### Ask About Vaccination

CCPP vaccines are available in some countries and may provide good to excellent protection. Inactivated, adjuvanted vaccines are used in certain endemic regions, but vaccine availability, schedules, and regulations vary. Ask your veterinarian or livestock authority whether vaccination is recommended for your location.  

Vaccination is not a substitute for quarantine and movement control. It should form part of a wider herd-health program.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can CCPP kill goats suddenly?

Yes. Peracute CCPP can cause death within 1 to 3 days, sometimes with minimal warning signs. Sudden deaths combined with fever or respiratory disease in other goats require immediate veterinary investigation. 

### Is contagious caprine pleuropneumonia contagious to humans?

There is no evidence that CCPP infects humans. It is an important disease of goats and some other ruminants, not a recognized human infection. Normal hygiene and protective handling practices are still recommended. 

### Can sheep catch CCPP?

Sheep may be infected during outbreaks involving mixed goat-and-sheep herds. Their role as a reservoir is not fully understood, so sheep should be included in outbreak control and veterinary assessment. 

### Can antibiotics cure CCPP?

Early antimicrobial treatment may be effective, but it does not always eliminate the organism or prevent chronic lung damage. Treatment must be prescribed and monitored by a veterinarian.

### Is there a vaccine for CCPP?

Vaccines are available in some countries, particularly in areas where CCPP is endemic. Protection and vaccination schedules vary, so consult your local veterinarian or animal-health authority.

### How long should a new goat be quarantined?

The appropriate period depends on local disease risk, veterinary guidance, and testing options. Because CCPP commonly has an incubation period of 6 to 10 days but may take longer to appear, a several-week quarantine is a prudent herd-health practice.

### What is the fastest way to confirm CCPP?

PCR testing of suitable samples, especially pleural fluid or affected lung tissue, is commonly preferred because Mccp is difficult to culture. Your veterinarian should coordinate sample collection and laboratory submission.

## Conclusion

Contagious caprine pleuropneumonia in goats is a fast-spreading and potentially fatal respiratory disease caused by *Mycoplasma capricolum* subsp. *capripneumoniae*. High fever, painful breathing, violent coughing, nasal discharge, open-mouth breathing, and sudden death are important warning signs.

The best protection for your herd is a combination of strict quarantine, careful purchasing, good ventilation, reduced crowding, movement control, vaccination where available, and rapid veterinary intervention. If CCPP is suspected, isolate affected goats immediately and seek professional help rather than waiting for the disease to spread.
