Respiratory illness is a constant challenge in young stock, and winter housing design plays a major role in reducing long-term lung damage and mortality. Balancing fresh air with the prevention of harmful draughts protects your calves during cold weather.

## Position Your Shed for Natural Airflow

Where you place the building matters just as much as how you build it. A calf shed should always be a stand-alone structure. Locate it upwind of other cattle housing facilities so stale air and pathogens from older animals do not blow directly into the calves. Position the building at right angles to the prevailing wind to make the most of natural air currents and help pull stale air out of the structure without relying on power sources.

## Keep Shed Width in Mind

![several cows eating feed from a trough in a barn](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1629313471551-ba2052384b9b?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyfHxjYXR0bGUlMjBob3VzaW5nfGVufDF8MHx8fDE3OTU2NTYxOTh8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


Building dimensions directly affect how well air moves through the space. The width of a long, narrow calf shed should not exceed 11m to ensure fresh air reaches the center of the building. Natural ventilation works best for youngstock facilities. In climates similar to Wisconsin's, mechanically ventilated facilities are not recommended for calves because it is extremely difficult to produce a reliably-sized inlet at winter ventilation rates. Uninsulated naturally ventilated barns help reduce airborne pathogens. A Danish study noted that uninsulated, naturally ventilated barns had the least airborne bacteria and fungi.

## Control Air Speed and Volume

![A brown and white calf lies down on green grass in a pasture.](https://images.pexels.com/photos/31669670/pexels-photo-31669670.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


Fresh air is necessary, but fast-moving air chills young animals quickly. Draught speeds must be controlled in calf housing, and conditions must be draught-free at less than 2 meters per second (<2m/sec) as noted by NADIS. For preweaned calves, an indoor ventilation rate of 100 cubic feet per minute [cfm] is noted by Cornell University College of ... to maintain a healthy environment. Design your sidewalls and roof outlets to ensure a proper balance of fresh air inlets and stale air removal.

## Add Positive-Pressure Tubes for Cold Weather

When winter temperatures drop and natural wind speeds slow down, natural ventilation may need a boost. Positive-pressure tubes can supplement ventilation in cold weather. They deliver fresh air directly to calf zones, but hole size, spacing, tube pressure, fan output, building leakage, and seasonal settings must be engineered correctly. If these factors are not balanced, the system will either create unwanted draughts or fail to clear stale air.

## Set Up Pens for Comfort and Contact

Air quality is only part of the system. Provide a clean, dry bed in a well-ventilated environment with draughts kept under control. Ensure calf pens are large enough to allow calves to groom themselves, lie down, stretch their limbs, and rise without difficulty. Calves also benefit from social interaction, so design pens or hutches to allow visual and tactile contact between calves in adjoining spaces.

## What to Do This Week

Walk through your calf housing and check the current airflow and pen layout. Measure or estimate your building dimensions to see if the shed width fits the natural ventilation limits. Inspect your bedding to ensure it is clean and dry, and check that sidewalls and roof outlets are clear to maintain the right balance of fresh air inlets and stale air removal. If you are considering positive-pressure tubes for cold weather, consult a local veterinary authority to ensure the fan output, tube pressure, and hole spacing are engineered correctly for your specific building layout.
