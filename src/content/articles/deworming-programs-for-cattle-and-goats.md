Internal parasites can quietly reduce weight gain, milk production, fertility, and overall herd performance. In severe cases, worms can cause anemia, diarrhea, weakness, respiratory problems, or death—especially in young goats and stressed animals.

However, effective parasite control is not as simple as giving every animal a dewormer every few months. Frequent, routine treatments can select for drug-resistant worms and eventually make important products ineffective. Modern cattle and goat deworming programs focus on monitoring, targeted treatment, correct dosing, pasture management, and veterinary guidance.

The best program for your farm depends on your animals, climate, grazing system, parasite species, production goals, and the dewormers that still work on your operation.

## Why Internal Parasites Matter

Cattle and goats can carry several types of internal parasites. These include stomach worms, intestinal worms, lungworms, liver flukes, tapeworms, and coccidia.

In goats, the barber pole worm, or *Haemonchus contortus*, is one of the most dangerous parasites. It feeds on blood and can cause severe anemia, bottle jaw, weakness, rapid weight loss, and sudden death. The barber pole worm does not usually cause diarrhea, so a goat may be critically ill even when its manure appears normal.

Cattle commonly face gastrointestinal nematodes such as *Ostertagia*, *Cooperia*, and *Haemonchus* species. These parasites can cause poor growth, reduced feed efficiency, diarrhea, rough hair coats, and lower milk production.

Parasite pressure is often greatest when conditions are warm and wet. Overgrazing, high stocking density, poor nutrition, and grazing animals close to the ground can increase exposure to infective larvae.

## The Goal of a Deworming Program

The goal is not to eliminate every parasite from your herd or flock. Some parasites will always be present, and animals can often tolerate a manageable burden.

Instead, your goal should be to:

- Prevent disease and death
- Protect weight gain and milk production
- Treat animals that genuinely need medication
- Preserve the effectiveness of available dewormers
- Reduce pasture contamination
- Avoid unnecessary drug costs and residues
- Identify animals that are repeatedly susceptible to parasites

A successful cattle and goat parasite control program uses dewormers as one tool—not the only tool.

## Why Calendar Deworming Can Fail

Many producers once treated all cattle or goats on a fixed schedule. For example, they might deworm every animal at turnout, every 60 days, or several times each year.

This approach may temporarily reduce parasite numbers, but it can accelerate anthelmintic resistance. When an entire herd is treated, susceptible worms are killed while resistant worms survive and reproduce. Repeated treatments gradually increase the proportion of resistant parasites.

Goat producers face an especially serious challenge because resistance to several dewormer classes is already common. Pour-on cattle products and incorrect dosing can also expose goats to ineffective or subtherapeutic drug levels.

Targeted selective treatment helps preserve “refugia.” Refugia refers to parasites that have not been exposed to the drug. These untreated, susceptible parasites can dilute resistant worms and slow the development of resistance. 

## Step 1: Monitor Animals Before Deworming

Do not choose a deworming date based only on the calendar. Regular observation helps you identify animals that need further testing or treatment.

Watch for:

- Weight loss or failure to gain weight
- Reduced appetite
- Diarrhea or manure staining
- Rough, dull hair or coat
- Pale gums or eyelids
- Bottle jaw
- Weakness or lethargy
- Coughing or difficult breathing
- Reduced milk production
- Poor body condition
- Reduced breeding performance

Remember that these symptoms can have causes other than parasites. Poor nutrition, infectious disease, coccidiosis, mineral deficiencies, and dental problems may look similar.

## Step 2: Use Fecal Egg Counts

A fecal egg count, or FEC, measures parasite eggs in manure. It provides a more objective picture than appearance alone and can help you decide whether treatment is necessary.

FEC testing can help you:

- Compare parasite burdens between animals
- Identify high-shedding animals
- Monitor young or high-risk animals
- Determine whether a dewormer is working
- Track seasonal parasite patterns
- Support targeted treatment decisions

Fecal egg counts are not perfect. They may underestimate immature worms that are not yet producing eggs, and diarrhea can dilute the egg concentration. Some worm eggs also cannot be identified to species using routine testing. For that reason, interpret FEC results alongside body condition, clinical signs, production records, and veterinary advice.  

For cattle, your veterinarian may recommend individual or pooled samples depending on the herd’s size and purpose. For goats, individual testing is especially useful when identifying animals that repeatedly require treatment.

## Step 3: Use FAMACHA and the Five-Point Check for Goats

FAMACHA scoring is a useful tool for detecting anemia caused primarily by barber pole worms. It involves comparing the color of the lower eyelid with a standardized color chart.

In general:

- Scores 1 and 2 indicate little or no anemia
- Score 3 requires additional evaluation
- Scores 4 and 5 indicate significant anemia and usually require prompt treatment with an effective product

FAMACHA does not identify every parasite. It will not reliably detect coccidia, many intestinal worms, or parasites that cause diarrhea without anemia.

For a broader evaluation, use the Five-Point Check:

1. **Eye:** Look for anemia using FAMACHA.
2. **Jaw:** Check for bottle jaw, or swelling beneath the jaw.
3. **Back:** Assess body condition and weight loss.
4. **Tail:** Look for diarrhea or fecal staining.
5. **Nose or coat:** Check for nasal discharge in sheep or poor coat condition in goats.

Goats with a borderline FAMACHA score may need treatment if they also have bottle jaw, poor body condition, diarrhea, weight loss, or other signs of parasitism.  

Learn FAMACHA from a qualified trainer when possible. Incorrect scoring can lead to delayed treatment or unnecessary drug use.

## Step 4: Test Whether Your Dewormer Works

A dewormer should not be assumed to work simply because it worked in the past. Resistance varies between farms, regions, parasite species, and drug classes.

The fecal egg count reduction test, or FECRT, is commonly used to evaluate efficacy. Collect fecal samples on the day of treatment and collect follow-up samples from the same animals approximately 10 to 14 days later. Compare the pretreatment and post-treatment results.

The basic calculation is:

**FECR percentage = [(pretreatment FEC − post-treatment FEC) ÷ pretreatment FEC] × 100**

Veterinary interpretation is important because sample size, egg-count variation, untreated controls, and parasite biology affect the result. The Merck Veterinary Manual identifies FECRT as the primary method for assessing dewormer efficacy within a herd and recommends periodic testing based on local parasite conditions.  

If a product fails, do not simply repeat the same treatment at a higher dose without professional advice. The problem may be resistance, inaccurate weight estimates, poor administration, reinfection, incorrect storage, or a disease that is not caused by worms.

## Common Dewormer Classes

The main anthelmintic classes used in cattle and goats include:

### Benzimidazoles

Examples include fenbendazole and albendazole. These products may control several gastrointestinal worms, but resistance is common in many goat operations.

Albendazole requires special pregnancy precautions. Never use it in a pregnant doe unless your veterinarian confirms that it is appropriate for that stage of pregnancy.

### Macrocyclic Lactones

This group includes ivermectin, doramectin, eprinomectin, and moxidectin. They may control internal and external parasites, depending on the specific product and label.

Resistance to this class is widespread in gastrointestinal nematodes, particularly where products have been used repeatedly or incorrectly. 

### Imidazothiazoles

Levamisole is an example. It can be effective against some resistant worm populations, but it has a relatively narrow safety margin. Accurate animal weights and careful dosing are essential.

### Tetrahydropyrimidines

Morantel is used in some cattle and goat parasite-control programs. Product concentrations and approved uses vary, so read the label carefully and consult your veterinarian.

No dewormer controls every parasite. Coccidia, for example, are not treated with standard dewormers and require separate prevention or treatment products. 

## Cattle Deworming Program Basics

A cattle deworming program should reflect the animals’ age, production stage, grazing conditions, and parasite risk.

High-risk cattle may include:

- Calves and yearlings
- Recently weaned animals
- Cattle entering contaminated pastures
- Thin or immunocompromised animals
- Replacement heifers
- Cattle under nutritional stress
- Animals grazing continuously in small areas

Many cattle operations use strategic treatment at times when parasite exposure is predictably high, such as turnout, weaning, or the end of the grazing season. However, strategic treatment should be based on local parasite patterns and herd data—not copied from another farm.

Monitor average daily gain, body condition, fecal egg counts, and pasture conditions. If cattle are gaining weight normally and parasite burdens are low, treatment may not be necessary.

## Goat Deworming Program Basics

Goats often require more intensive monitoring than adult cattle because they are highly susceptible to barber pole worms and can develop serious disease rapidly.

Pay particular attention to:

- Kids after weaning
- Does during late pregnancy and early lactation
- Thin animals
- Goats with repeated FAMACHA scores of 3 or higher
- Animals grazing short or heavily contaminated pasture
- Newly purchased goats
- Goats during warm, humid weather

Do not assume that a cattle dose is correct for a goat. Goats metabolize many drugs faster than sheep and cattle, and veterinary-prescribed goat doses may differ from the label dose for another species.

Many goat treatments are extra-label, meaning the product is not labeled for that particular species, dose, route, or condition. Extra-label use must be directed by a veterinarian within a valid veterinarian-client-patient relationship. Your veterinarian should also establish the legal meat and milk withdrawal period.

## Correct Dosing Is Essential

Underdosing is one of the fastest ways to encourage drug resistance. Before treatment:

1. Weigh each animal or use the most accurate weight estimate possible.
2. Check the product concentration.
3. Confirm the correct dose for the species.
4. Use the recommended route of administration.
5. Inspect the expiration date and storage conditions.
6. Calibrate the dosing equipment.
7. Record the animal, product, dose, date, and withdrawal information.

For oral goat drenches, the product should be delivered over the tongue toward the back of the mouth so the animal swallows it properly. Never use a pour-on cattle product in goats unless a veterinarian specifically directs an appropriate legal use.

Do not increase a dose, combine products, or repeat a treatment without veterinary guidance.

## Withdrawal Times and Food Safety

Every livestock dewormer has a withdrawal period. This is the time between the last treatment and when the animal’s meat or milk may legally enter the food supply.

Withdrawal periods depend on:

- Species
- Product formulation
- Dose
- Route of administration
- Frequency of treatment
- Whether use is on-label or extra-label
- Whether the animal produces milk or meat

A withdrawal time printed on a cattle label may not apply when the same product is used in a goat. Extra-label use can require a longer withdrawal period, and your veterinarian may need to consult an approved residue database.

Keep written treatment records for every animal. When in doubt, do not market the animal or its milk until the withdrawal period has been confirmed.

## Pasture and Management Strategies

Deworming works better when combined with management practices that reduce parasite exposure.

Useful strategies include:

- Avoiding overgrazing
- Maintaining adequate pasture height
- Reducing stocking density
- Providing browse for goats
- Rotating grazing areas appropriately
- Using multispecies grazing where practical
- Providing high-quality nutrition
- Keeping feeders and water sources clean
- Quarantining and testing new arrivals
- Selecting animals that show natural parasite resilience
- Using suitable tannin-rich forages where available

Most infective larvae are found close to the ground, so preventing animals from grazing pasture too short can reduce exposure. Goats may also benefit from browsing shrubs and higher vegetation rather than grazing continuously at soil level.

Pasture rotation is not automatically a solution. If animals are moved too quickly or treated before entering a clean pasture, resistant worms may be deposited on that pasture. Plan grazing and treatment decisions together.

## When to Call Your Veterinarian

Contact your veterinarian promptly if an animal has:

- Severe anemia
- A FAMACHA score of 4 or 5
- Bottle jaw
- Extreme weakness
- Collapse
- Rapid weight loss
- Persistent diarrhea
- Respiratory distress
- Failure to respond to treatment
- Suspected dewormer resistance
- Unknown drug exposure
- A pregnancy-related dosing concern

A severely anemic goat may need more than a dewormer. Supportive care, nutrition, fluids, iron assessment, treatment for secondary problems, or emergency intervention may be necessary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should cattle be dewormed?

There is no universal schedule. Treatment should be based on parasite risk, age, grazing conditions, production performance, fecal egg counts, and veterinary recommendations. Routine calendar deworming can accelerate resistance.

### How often should goats be dewormed?

Goats should not be dewormed automatically every few weeks or months. Monitor FAMACHA scores, body condition, fecal egg counts, coat condition, diarrhea, and weight. Treat individuals that meet your farm’s treatment criteria.

### Can I use cattle dewormer on goats?

Sometimes a cattle product may be prescribed for a goat, but the dose, route, safety, and withdrawal period may be different. Never assume the cattle label applies to goats. Consult your veterinarian first.

### Does FAMACHA detect all goat worms?

No. FAMACHA primarily detects anemia associated with barber pole worms. It does not reliably identify coccidia or all intestinal parasites. Combine it with fecal testing and the Five-Point Check.

### Should I rotate dewormers every time?

Simply rotating products on a calendar does not guarantee effective parasite control. Test which products work on your farm and use veterinary-guided strategies. In some situations, combination treatment with different classes may be recommended.

### What is the best dewormer for goats?

The best product is the one that is effective against the parasites on your farm, safe for the animal, legally appropriate, and administered at the correct dose. A fecal egg count reduction test can help identify effective options.

### How can I prevent dewormer resistance?

Treat only animals that need treatment, avoid underdosing, weigh animals accurately, preserve refugia, test drug efficacy, and combine deworming with pasture and nutrition management.

## Conclusion

An effective deworming program for cattle and goats is based on prevention, monitoring, and targeted treatment—not routine medication alone. Use fecal egg counts, FAMACHA scoring, body condition, production records, and veterinary guidance to decide when treatment is justified.

Dose accurately, follow legal withdrawal periods, and keep detailed records. By combining the right dewormer with grazing management, good nutrition, and careful animal selection, you can protect herd health while extending the useful life of essential parasite-control products.
