East Coast fever is one of the most serious cattle diseases in eastern, central, and southern Africa. Caused by the parasite *Theileria parva* and spread by infected ticks, the disease can progress quickly from a high fever to swollen lymph nodes, breathing difficulty, severe weakness, and death.

The risk is especially high when susceptible cattle are moved into areas where infected ticks are established. Young calves, exotic dairy breeds, and crossbred cattle may be particularly vulnerable. However, with early recognition, prompt veterinary treatment, effective tick control, and appropriate vaccination, you can significantly reduce losses in your herd.

This guide explains what causes East Coast fever, how to recognize it, how veterinarians diagnose and treat it, and the practical steps you can take to protect your cattle.

## What Is East Coast Fever?

East Coast fever, often abbreviated as ECF, is an acute tick-borne disease of cattle caused by the protozoan parasite *Theileria parva*. It is not the same as tropical theileriosis, which is mainly associated with *Theileria annulata*.

The parasite is transmitted primarily by the brown ear tick, *Rhipicephalus appendiculatus*. When an infected tick feeds on cattle, it can transmit *T. parva* into the animal’s bloodstream. The parasite first multiplies in white blood cells and later develops into forms found in red blood cells.

East Coast fever is a serious problem in several African countries, including Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and parts of South Sudan. Approximately half of the cattle population in affected regions may be at risk, and the disease causes major losses through cattle deaths, reduced milk production, weight loss, treatment expenses, and lower farm productivity. 

## How Is East Coast Fever Spread?

The disease cycle depends on infected cattle, susceptible cattle, and ticks.

### The role of the brown ear tick

The brown ear tick is the most important vector of *T. parva*. It commonly attaches around the:

- Ears
- Base of the ears
- Neck
- Dewlap
- Tail area
- Under the tail
- Perineum

An infected tick acquires the parasite when feeding on an infected animal. It may then transmit the parasite to another susceptible cow, calf, or bull during a later blood meal.

Cattle movement is a major risk factor. Introducing untreated animals into an endemic area, taking cattle to communal grazing land, or purchasing animals from unknown health backgrounds can introduce infected ticks or parasites into your herd.

Wild buffalo can also carry certain *T. parva* strains. In some areas, buffalo-associated infection is referred to as corridor disease. The disease may spread when infected ticks move between wildlife and cattle, although the epidemiology varies between regions.

## Which Cattle Are Most at Risk?

Cattle that have never encountered *T. parva* are generally at the greatest risk. This includes:

- Calves in endemic areas
- Cattle moved from disease-free zones
- Imported European dairy breeds
- High-producing exotic cattle
- Crossbred cattle without previous exposure
- Unvaccinated animals
- Cattle exposed after poor tick-control practices

Some indigenous cattle may develop a degree of resistance or partial protection after natural exposure. However, natural infection is dangerous and should not be relied upon as a disease-control strategy.

Stress can also worsen disease risk. Transport, poor nutrition, drought, calving, heavy parasite burdens, and other infections may reduce an animal’s ability to cope with *T. parva* infection.

## East Coast Fever Incubation Period

The incubation period is usually about 7 to 12 days after infection, although signs may appear later in some animals. Experimental and field cases may become apparent up to approximately three weeks after exposure.  

This means you should closely monitor cattle after:

- Moving them to a new grazing area
- Buying new animals
- Introducing cattle from another herd
- Ending a period of poor tick control
- Returning cattle from markets or communal grazing

Early observation can make the difference between successful treatment and death.

## Signs and Symptoms of East Coast Fever in Cattle

The first signs may be subtle, but the disease can deteriorate rapidly. Common symptoms include:

### 1. High fever

Affected cattle often develop a high temperature. Fever may be one of the earliest noticeable signs, particularly when several animals become sick within a short period.

### 2. Swollen lymph nodes

Enlarged lymph nodes are a classic sign of ECF. The lymph nodes behind the ears, below the jaw, in front of the shoulder, and in front of the hind legs may become visibly swollen.

The nodes may feel firm and enlarged when examined. Checking the superficial lymph nodes can be useful, but handling a sick animal should be done calmly and safely.

### 3. Loss of appetite and weight

Cattle with East Coast fever commonly stop eating or eat much less than usual. They may lose body condition quickly, become weak, and spend more time standing quietly or lying down.

Reduced feed intake can also cause a rapid fall in milk production in dairy cows.

### 4. Breathing difficulty

As the disease progresses, cattle may develop rapid or labored breathing. Coughing, nasal discharge, and respiratory distress may occur.

Fluid accumulation in the lungs, known as pulmonary edema, is a serious complication. Affected cattle may breathe with their mouths open, extend the neck, or show marked effort when breathing. Pulmonary edema is a common and potentially fatal feature of ECF. 

### 5. Nasal and eye discharge

Some cattle develop discharge from the nose or eyes. The discharge may be watery at first and become thicker as the illness progresses.

### 6. Diarrhea

Diarrhea can occur, especially in severe cases. It may contribute to dehydration and weakness.

### 7. Pale or damaged mucous membranes

Some affected cattle may develop anemia or changes in the color of the gums and inner eyelids. These signs can also occur with other tick-borne diseases, so laboratory testing is important.

### 8. Sudden death

Without timely treatment, East Coast fever can be fatal. Death may occur after severe respiratory distress, profound weakness, or complications involving the lungs and immune system.

Not every infected animal shows every sign. A cow with fever and enlarged lymph nodes in an area where ECF is present should be treated as an urgent veterinary case.

## How Is East Coast Fever Diagnosed?

A veterinarian may suspect East Coast fever from the animal’s history, clinical signs, tick exposure, and geographic location. However, several other diseases can cause fever, swollen lymph nodes, anemia, weakness, or breathing problems.

Possible differential diagnoses include:

- Anaplasmosis
- Babesiosis
- Tropical theileriosis
- Trypanosomiasis
- Bacterial pneumonia
- Anthrax
- Severe parasitic disease
- Other causes of fever and lymph node enlargement

Laboratory confirmation is recommended whenever possible.

### Lymph node examination

A veterinarian may collect a fine-needle aspirate from an enlarged lymph node. A stained smear can reveal *T. parva* schizonts inside infected white blood cells.

Microscopic examination of lymph node aspirates is an important method for confirming ECF. Parasite stages may also be detected in stained blood smears, although blood-smear findings can vary depending on the stage of disease. 

### PCR testing

Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, can detect *T. parva* DNA and may be more sensitive than microscopy, particularly when parasite levels are low.

Newer molecular approaches are also being developed for field use. A CRISPR-Cas12a test designed to detect *T. parva* DNA has demonstrated the ability to identify multiple parasite strains in blood samples and could help provide faster diagnosis in areas where laboratory access is limited. 

### Serology

Antibody tests may help identify previous exposure to *T. parva*. However, antibodies do not always prove that the animal currently has active clinical disease. For this reason, serology should be interpreted alongside clinical signs and other tests.

## Treatment of East Coast Fever

East Coast fever requires urgent veterinary attention. Treatment is most successful when started early, before severe respiratory disease and widespread tissue damage develop.

Common veterinary medicines used against ECF include:

- Buparvaquone
- Parvaquone
- Halofuginone
- Oxytetracycline in selected situations

Buparvaquone and parvaquone are antiprotozoal drugs used specifically against theileriosis. Their use, dosage, withdrawal period, and repeat-treatment decisions must be determined by a veterinarian and must follow the product label and local regulations.

Do not guess the dose. Underdosing may reduce treatment success and contribute to drug resistance, while overdosing may harm the animal.

Oxytetracycline may be useful very early in infection, but it is not a substitute for proper antitheilerial treatment once clinical disease is established. Veterinary references note that oxytetracycline has limited therapeutic effect after disease has developed, although it is used alongside the live infection-and-treatment immunization method. 

### Supportive treatment

A severely affected cow may also need supportive care, such as:

- Fluids for dehydration
- Anti-inflammatory medication
- Treatment for secondary bacterial infections
- Careful management of pulmonary complications
- Reduced handling and stress
- Good-quality feed and clean water

If pulmonary edema is present, the veterinarian may consider treatment aimed at reducing fluid accumulation in the lungs. Research on parvaquone combined with a diuretic reported faster resolution of pulmonary signs in some severe cases, but treatment decisions must be made by a qualified veterinarian. 

### Isolate sick cattle

Separate sick animals from the rest of the herd where practical. Isolation will not remove the risk from ticks already present, but it can reduce close contact and help you monitor the affected animal.

Avoid moving sick cattle to another farm, market, or grazing area. Movement can spread infected ticks and make disease control more difficult.

## Preventing East Coast Fever

Prevention is usually safer and more affordable than treating advanced ECF.

### 1. Use a reliable tick-control program

Work with your veterinarian or animal-health professional to establish a tick-control plan. Depending on local conditions, this may include:

- Dipping
- Hand spraying
- Pour-on acaricides
- Targeted treatment of high-risk animals
- Manual tick removal
- Treating the ears and tail areas carefully
- Rotating products only under veterinary guidance

Apply acaricides at the correct concentration and interval. Using weak mixtures, skipping applications, or applying products inconsistently can allow ticks to survive and may encourage resistance.

Acaricide resistance is a growing concern, and chemical tick control can be expensive. Your program should therefore be based on local tick pressure, cattle breed, grazing conditions, product performance, and veterinary advice.

### 2. Consider ECF vaccination

The main available immunization approach is the infection-and-treatment method, commonly associated with the Muguga cocktail.

This method uses a controlled live *T. parva* vaccine preparation together with long-acting oxytetracycline. The antibiotic limits the severity of infection while the animal develops an immune response. The procedure must be carried out by trained professionals because the vaccine contains live parasite material and can cause disease if used incorrectly.  

Cattle should generally be immunized before entering high-risk areas. Veterinary guidance indicates that vaccination should occur approximately three to four weeks before cattle are exposed to infected pasture.  

The vaccine may not provide identical protection against every *T. parva* strain. Availability, registration, handling, cold-chain requirements, and follow-up procedures also vary by country.

### 3. Quarantine newly purchased cattle

Keep newly purchased animals separate from your resident herd for an appropriate period. During quarantine:

- Inspect them for ticks
- Treat them according to veterinary advice
- Monitor temperature and general health
- Avoid sharing equipment unnecessarily
- Confirm vaccination history
- Test animals when recommended

Quarantine cannot guarantee that an animal is free from infection, but it reduces the chance of introducing disease and ticks into your herd.

### 4. Monitor cattle every day

Daily observation helps you identify early changes. Look for:

- Reduced appetite
- Fever
- Enlarged lymph nodes
- Drop in milk yield
- Coughing or fast breathing
- Lethargy
- Diarrhea
- Visible ticks
- Weight loss

Record sick animals, treatments, dates, temperatures, and outcomes. These records help your veterinarian identify patterns and improve herd-level control.

### 5. Improve nutrition and general health

Good nutrition does not prevent exposure to *T. parva*, but it supports immune function and recovery. Provide adequate energy, protein, minerals, and clean water.

Reduce unnecessary stress during transport, handling, weaning, and extreme weather. Healthy cattle are better able to cope with disease challenges than animals that are underfed or heavily parasitized.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Several management mistakes increase the risk of serious losses:

- Waiting several days before calling a veterinarian
- Treating every fever with the same medicine
- Using drugs intended for babesiosis or anaplasmosis without diagnosis
- Applying acaricides at the wrong concentration
- Failing to treat high-risk areas such as ears and the tail base
- Moving sick or recently purchased cattle without quarantine
- Relying on natural infection instead of vaccination
- Using traditional remedies as a replacement for veterinary treatment
- Stopping treatment when the animal appears slightly better
- Ignoring withdrawal periods for milk and meat

Because several tick-borne diseases have similar symptoms, diagnosis matters. A treatment that works for one disease may not work for East Coast fever.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can East Coast fever spread directly from one cow to another?

East Coast fever is mainly spread by infected ticks rather than direct contact between cattle. However, infected cattle can act as a source for ticks, which may later transmit *T. parva* to susceptible animals.

### Is East Coast fever contagious to humans?

East Coast fever is a cattle disease and is not considered a human infection. People can, however, transport infected ticks on animals, clothing, equipment, or vehicles and unintentionally contribute to disease spread between herds.

### Can cattle recover from East Coast fever?

Some cattle recover when treatment is started early. The chance of recovery decreases when the disease is advanced, especially when severe breathing difficulty or pulmonary edema is present.

### What is the best treatment for East Coast fever?

Veterinarians commonly use antitheilerial medicines such as buparvaquone or parvaquone. The best option depends on disease stage, product availability, the animal’s condition, local regulations, and veterinary assessment.

### Does dipping prevent East Coast fever?

Regular, correctly performed tick control can reduce the risk of transmission, but no tick-control program is perfect. The schedule and product must be appropriate for local tick populations, and acaricide resistance must be considered.

### Is there a vaccine for East Coast fever?

A live infection-and-treatment immunization method is available in some countries. It uses *T. parva* material together with long-acting oxytetracycline and must be administered by trained veterinary personnel.

### How quickly should you call a veterinarian?

Call immediately if cattle show fever, enlarged lymph nodes, sudden loss of appetite, rapid breathing, or weakness—especially after tick exposure or movement into an endemic area. Early treatment offers the best chance of survival.

## Final Takeaway

East Coast fever in cattle is a fast-moving and potentially fatal tick-borne disease. The combination of fever, swollen lymph nodes, poor appetite, breathing difficulty, and recent tick exposure should always be treated as an emergency.

Protect your herd through consistent tick control, quarantine of new animals, daily monitoring, good nutrition, and vaccination where approved and available. If you suspect East Coast fever, contact a veterinarian quickly, isolate the affected animal, avoid moving cattle, and do not rely on unconfirmed home treatment. Early diagnosis and a coordinated herd-health plan are your strongest tools against this costly disease.
