# Feline Leukemia Virus: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

![Feline Leukemia Virus](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1725409796872-8b41e8eca929?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxGZWxpbmUlMjBMZXVrZW1pYSUyMFZpcnVzJTIwYW5pbWFsfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NTY5ODR8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

*Photo: Feline Leukemia Virus — via Unsplash*

Feline Leukemia Virus is one of the most important infectious diseases of cats worldwide, especially for kittens, outdoor cats and cats living in groups. It attacks the immune system and blood-forming tissues, leading to anemia, cancer and life‑threatening infections.  Because of its impact on cat health and its ease of spread between cats, every owner should understand what Feline Leukemia Virus is, the symptoms to watch for, how it is diagnosed, how to treat Feline Leukemia Virus, and—most importantly—the prevention of Feline Leukemia Virus through testing, vaccination and good management.  

While a diagnosis of FeLV (Feline Leukemia Virus) is serious, it is not always an immediate death sentence. Many positive cats can live good‑quality lives for months to years with appropriate veterinary care, indoor housing and quick treatment of secondary problems.  With good information, cat owners, farmers and students can make better decisions to protect healthy cats and support those already infected.

---

## What is Feline Leukemia Virus?

Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV) is a retrovirus that infects only cats and closely related felids. It is one of the most common infectious agents in cats and is responsible for a large proportion of cancer‑related deaths in this species.  As a retrovirus, FeLV inserts its genetic material into the cat’s cells, especially white blood cells and bone marrow cells, where it can persist for life and interfere with normal immune and blood functions.  

FeLV is not contagious to humans, dogs or other non‑feline pets. It spreads only between cats and some wild felids such as bobcats and cougars.  Once a cat is persistently infected, it may shed the virus in saliva, nasal secretions, urine, feces and milk, posing a risk to other cats in close contact.  

The virus can cause different forms of disease. Some cats develop cancers such as lymphoma and leukemia; others develop severe anemia or chronic infections due to a weak immune system.  Some cats clear the virus or develop latent infection, while others remain persistently viremic and are at highest risk of illness and of spreading FeLV to others.

## Causes of Feline Leukemia Virus

![Causes of Feline Leukemia Virus](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1725409796872-8b41e8eca929?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxGZWxpbmUlMjBMZXVrZW1pYSUyMFZpcnVzJTIwZGlzZWFzZXxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTU2OTg0fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*

### How Feline Leukemia Virus Spreads

The main cause of Feline Leukemia Virus infection is close, prolonged contact with a shedding FeLV‑positive cat. The virus is most commonly spread through saliva—shared food and water bowls, mutual grooming, and bite wounds are key routes.  Virus can also be present in nasal secretions, urine, feces and milk, so sharing litter boxes or nursing from an infected queen may transmit infection.  

Transmission usually requires repeated or intimate contact, which means cats in multi‑cat households, shelters, feral colonies, and breeding catteries are at higher risk.  Short, casual contact across a fence or brief sniffing encounters are less efficient for spreading the virus, but can still be risky in high‑density environments.

### Risk Factors for Infection

Certain cats are more likely to become infected and to develop disease from FeLV. Kittens and young cats are especially susceptible because their immune systems are immature; they are also more likely to develop persistent infection and serious illness.  Cats with outdoor access, intact (unneutered) males that fight, and cats living with known FeLV‑positive companions are at significantly higher risk.  

Stress, poor nutrition and co‑infections (for example with Feline Immunodeficiency Virus, FIV) can worsen the outcome of infection.  In contrast, indoor‑only cats, neutered cats, and those vaccinated against FeLV have a reduced risk of exposure and disease, although vaccination does not guarantee complete protection.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus

![Symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1725409796872-8b41e8eca929?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxGZWxpbmUlMjBMZXVrZW1pYSUyMFZpcnVzJTIwc2ljayUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTU2OTg0fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*

### General Clinical Signs

The symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus are often vague and may develop slowly over months or years. In the early stages, many cats show no obvious illness. As the virus damages the immune system and blood‑forming cells, signs become more noticeable. Common symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus include poor appetite, weight loss, lethargy, persistent fever and pale gums due to anemia.  

Because FeLV weakens the immune system, affected cats develop chronic or recurrent infections: runny nose, eye discharge, persistent gingivitis or stomatitis (sore mouth), skin infections, and non‑healing wounds are frequent.  Repeated respiratory, urinary or gastrointestinal infections that do not respond well or keep coming back are classic red flags.

### Cancer, Anemia and Other Specific Problems

FeLV is a major cause of lymphoma and leukemia in cats. Tumors may occur in the chest (mediastinal lymphoma), abdomen or other organs. Chest tumors can cause breathing difficulty, open‑mouth breathing, coughing or fluid around the lungs.  Abdominal tumors may cause vomiting, diarrhea, constipation or a swollen belly. Lymph nodes can become enlarged.

Anemia is another major complication. Severely anemic cats may be very weak, have rapid heart and breathing rates, and show pale or white gums.  Some cats develop neurologic signs such as seizures, behavior changes or difficulty walking, as well as reproductive problems like infertility, abortions and fading kittens.  Because symptoms vary widely, any unexplained, chronic or severe illness should prompt FeLV testing.

## How to Diagnose Feline Leukemia Virus

### Screening Tests (ELISA)

The most common way to diagnose Feline Leukemia Virus is a screening blood test performed in the veterinary clinic, often called an ELISA or “snap” test. This test detects FeLV antigen (viral protein) in the blood and can usually give results within minutes from a small blood sample.  ELISA tests are recommended for all new cats entering a household, sick cats, and any cat with known or possible exposure to FeLV‑positive animals.  

Because FeLV infection can take weeks to become detectable, a cat that was only recently exposed may test negative at first. For this reason, veterinarians often recommend repeating the test about 60 days after the last possible exposure if there is ongoing concern. 

### Confirmatory and Follow‑Up Tests

If a cat tests positive on the screening test, further testing is usually advised to confirm infection and determine the stage. An IFA (indirect immunofluorescent antibody) test looks for virus in white blood cells and platelets and is often used as a confirmatory test. Cats that are IFA‑positive generally have persistent infection and a poorer long‑term prognosis.  

Additional tests such as PCR can help detect latent infections or clarify confusing results, especially in young kittens or cats with conflicting test outcomes.  Your veterinarian may also run a complete blood count, biochemistry profile and imaging (X‑rays or ultrasound) to assess anemia, organ function and the presence of tumors or other complications.  

Regular re‑testing may be recommended for cats at ongoing risk, such as outdoor cats or those living in multi‑cat environments, to catch new infections early and guide vaccination and management decisions.

## Treatment of Feline Leukemia Virus

*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*

### Is There a Cure for Feline Leukemia Virus?

At present there is no treatment that can completely eliminate Feline Leukemia Virus from the body or reliably reverse infection once established.  However, knowing how to treat Feline Leukemia Virus focuses on managing complications, prolonging good quality of life and reducing spread to other cats.

Many FeLV‑positive cats can remain stable for substantial periods with good supportive care. Lifespan after diagnosis varies widely; on average, many persistently infected cats live for a few years, but some survive significantly longer under attentive management. 

### Supportive and Symptomatic Treatment

Treatment of Feline Leukemia Virus centers on controlling secondary infections, managing anemia and cancer, and supporting overall health. Because FeLV suppresses immunity, early and aggressive treatment of bacterial, viral or parasitic infections is critical, often with antibiotics, fluids and other medications.  Blood transfusions may be necessary in severe anemia, and appetite stimulants or anti‑nausea drugs can help maintain food intake.  

Antiviral and immune‑modulating drugs, such as AZT and feline interferon‑omega, have shown benefit in improving clinical and immune status and prolonging survival in some FeLV‑positive cats.  Chemotherapy may be used for FeLV‑associated lymphoma or leukemia, though prognosis is guarded. Pain control, dental care, and high‑quality nutrition are essential components of long‑term management.  

Good home care is equally important. FeLV‑positive cats should be kept indoors to protect them from other infections and to prevent transmission. A calm, low‑stress environment, regular veterinary check‑ups (often at least twice yearly), and strict parasite control (fleas, worms) help maintain health.

## Prevention and Control of Feline Leukemia Virus

![Prevention of Feline Leukemia Virus](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1596272875729-ed2ff7d6d9c5?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxGZWxpbmUlMjBMZXVrZW1pYSUyMFZpcnVzJTIwZmFybSUyMHByZXZlbnRpb258ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk1Njk4NHww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*

### Testing, Isolation and Household Management

The most effective prevention of Feline Leukemia Virus starts with testing and responsible cat management. All new cats and kittens entering a home, shelter, farm or cattery should be tested for FeLV before contact with resident cats.  FeLV‑positive cats should ideally live indoors and separate from FeLV‑negative cats to prevent saliva contact and biting.  

If owners choose to keep FeLV‑positive and negative cats in the same home, strict control is needed: no shared food or water bowls, separate litter trays, and vaccination of negative cats. Even then, prolonged close contact still carries risk.  Because FeLV is fragile in the environment and survives only minutes outside the body, normal household disinfectants and cleaning are sufficient to remove the virus from surfaces; a new, healthy cat can safely enter a previously FeLV‑contaminated home after routine cleaning. 

### Vaccination and Risk Reduction

Vaccination is a key tool in the prevention of Feline Leukemia Virus. Major feline organizations recommend FeLV vaccination for all kittens and for adult cats at risk, such as outdoor cats or those living with potentially infected companions.  Kittens typically receive two initial FeLV injections a few weeks apart, followed by booster doses according to the veterinarian’s protocol.  

FeLV vaccines are effective at reducing the risk of disease and clinical illness, though they do not guarantee sterilizing immunity and some vaccinated cats may still become infected under heavy exposure.  Therefore, vaccination should always be combined with risk reduction: keeping cats indoors, preventing fighting, neutering to reduce roaming, and controlling entry of untested cats into the household.  

For farm cats and feral colonies, trap‑neuter‑vaccinate‑return programs can lower FeLV prevalence over time by reducing breeding, fighting and uncontrolled mingling of infected and uninfected cats.

### Q: What is Feline Leukemia Virus and how dangerous is it?

**A:** Feline Leukemia Virus is a retrovirus that infects only cats, damaging the immune system and blood‑forming tissues. It is one of the most common and serious feline infectious diseases, causing cancer, anemia and life‑threatening infections, and is responsible for many cat deaths worldwide.  However, with early diagnosis and good care, many FeLV‑positive cats can enjoy a reasonable quality of life for a period of years. 

### Q: How do cats get Feline Leukemia Virus?

**A:** The virus is mainly spread through saliva during close, repeated contact—shared bowls, grooming and bite wounds. It can also be present in nasal secretions, urine, feces and milk, so nursing kittens of infected mothers and cats sharing litter boxes are at risk.  Kittens, outdoor cats and cats in multi‑cat households or colonies are more likely to become infected. 

### Q: What are the early symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus?

**A:** Early symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus are often mild or absent. Over time, common signs include poor appetite, gradual weight loss, fever, lethargy, pale gums (anemia), swollen lymph nodes and recurrent infections of the mouth, skin, eyes or respiratory tract.  Any cat with repeated or unexplained illness should be tested for FeLV. 

### Q: How to treat Feline Leukemia Virus in a cat at home?

**A:** There is no home cure for FeLV, but owners can support their cat by keeping it strictly indoors, feeding a high‑quality diet, avoiding raw meat, reducing stress and bringing the cat for regular veterinary check‑ups.  Prompt veterinary treatment of any infection, maintaining parasite control, and following all prescribed medications are essential parts of how to treat Feline Leukemia Virus and extend your cat’s life. 

### Q: Can a FeLV‑positive cat live with FeLV‑negative cats?

**A:** It is safest to house FeLV‑positive cats separately from negative cats to avoid saliva and bite exposure.  In some carefully managed homes, positive and negative cats may live together with precautions—vaccinating the negative cats, avoiding shared food and water bowls, and minimizing close contact—but some risk of transmission remains.  Your veterinarian can help assess the specific situation and recommend the safest plan.

### Q: Is Feline Leukemia Virus contagious to humans or dogs?

**A:** No. Feline Leukemia Virus infects only cats and certain wild felids; it does not infect people, dogs, rabbits or other non‑feline pets.  Human family members and other animal species are not at risk from an FeLV‑positive cat.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Owners should contact a veterinarian promptly if they notice possible symptoms of Feline Leukemia Virus: poor appetite, weight loss, persistent fever, pale gums, swollen lymph nodes, difficulty breathing, or repeated infections that do not resolve with usual treatment.  Early testing and diagnosis help guide how to treat Feline Leukemia Virus and may allow better control of complications.

A veterinary visit is also essential whenever a new cat or kitten joins a household, farm or shelter. All newcomers should be tested for FeLV before meeting resident cats, especially if they come from unknown backgrounds, shelters or the streets.  Cats that go outdoors, have been in fights, or have had contact with FeLV‑positive animals should be tested and vaccinated based on veterinary advice.  

FeLV‑positive cats require more frequent veterinary check‑ups—often every six months or sooner if problems develop. Regular blood work, dental checks, parasite control and monitoring for weight loss or behavior changes allow earlier intervention and can significantly improve comfort and longevity.

## Conclusion

Feline Leukemia Virus is a major infectious threat to cats, capable of causing cancer, anemia and severe immune suppression. It spreads mainly via saliva during close contact, especially in multi‑cat settings and among kittens and outdoor cats.  Because early infection may be silent, routine testing of new and at‑risk cats is vital to protect existing pets and guide decisions about vaccination and housing.  

There is currently no cure for FeLV, but understanding how to treat Feline Leukemia Virus—through aggressive management of infections, careful monitoring, antivirals in selected cases, and high‑quality home care—can extend life and maintain good welfare for many affected cats.  Prevention of Feline Leukemia Virus through testing, vaccination, keeping cats indoors and reducing contact with unknown or feral cats remains the most powerful tool to control this disease on farms, in homes and in communities.  

By combining veterinary guidance with responsible cat ownership, farmers, students and pet guardians can significantly reduce the impact of Feline Leukemia Virus and give both healthy and infected cats the best possible chance for long, comfortable lives.
