Feline leukemia virus, commonly called **FeLV**, is one of the most important infectious diseases affecting cats. This contagious virus can weaken your cat’s immune system, cause anemia and other blood disorders, and increase the risk of cancers such as lymphoma and leukemia. However, an FeLV diagnosis does not automatically mean your cat is immediately sick or must be euthanized.

Some cats with feline leukemia virus remain healthy for years with attentive care, regular veterinary examinations, and protection from additional infections. Understanding how FeLV spreads, recognizing possible symptoms, and using appropriate testing and vaccination can help you protect your cat and other cats in your household.

## What Is Feline Leukemia Virus?

Feline leukemia virus is a **retrovirus** that infects cats. Retroviruses can insert genetic material into the cells they infect, allowing the virus to remain in the body. FeLV can affect the bone marrow, immune system, lymphatic tissues, salivary glands, and other organs.

Despite its name, feline leukemia virus does not only cause leukemia. Progressive FeLV infection may result in:

- Immune-system suppression
- Anemia and other blood disorders
- Lymphoma and leukemia
- Chronic infections
- Severe inflammation of the mouth
- Reproductive problems
- Neurologic disease
- Poor growth or “fading kitten syndrome”

FeLV affects cats only. It cannot infect people, dogs, rabbits, or other non-feline household pets. The virus has declined in many areas because of improved testing, vaccination, and prevention, but it remains a serious concern, especially for outdoor cats, kittens, and cats living in multi-cat environments.  

## How Is FeLV Spread Between Cats?

Feline leukemia virus spreads primarily through close contact with an infected cat. The virus is found in high concentrations in saliva and may also be present in nasal secretions, blood, urine, feces, and milk.

Common routes of transmission include:

- Mutual grooming
- Sharing food and water bowls
- Sharing litter boxes
- Bite wounds from fighting
- Close, prolonged contact
- Nursing from an infected mother
- Transmission before birth

FeLV is a fragile virus outside the cat’s body and does not survive well on household surfaces. Transmission generally requires repeated or intimate contact rather than brief exposure. Still, cats that live together may be at risk because they groom one another and share essential resources.   

An infected cat may look completely healthy while shedding the virus. This is why testing is important when you adopt a new cat, introduce cats into the same household, or discover that one cat has been exposed to FeLV.

### Which Cats Have a Higher Risk?

Any cat can become infected, but the risk is higher for:

- Kittens and young cats
- Cats that spend time outdoors
- Cats that fight with other cats
- Unneutered cats that roam
- Cats living in shelters or crowded multi-cat homes
- Cats exposed to a known FeLV-positive cat
- Cats with an unknown testing history
- Cats with frequent exposure to new animals

Young kittens are particularly vulnerable to progressive infection because their immune systems are still developing. Adult cats may have greater resistance, but no age group is completely protected. 

## What Happens After a Cat Is Exposed to FeLV?

Not every cat exposed to feline leukemia virus develops the same type of infection. A cat’s age, immune response, overall health, and the amount of virus involved can influence the outcome.

Veterinarians generally describe three main outcomes.

### Abortive Infection

In an abortive infection, the cat’s immune system controls and eliminates the virus before it becomes established. These cats do not develop persistent infection and do not shed the virus.

Routine testing may not identify a previous abortive exposure, so owners may never know it occurred.

### Regressive Infection

A regressive infection occurs when the cat controls viral replication but does not completely remove FeLV genetic material from the body. The virus may remain hidden in cells, including cells in the bone marrow.

Regressively infected cats usually have no detectable virus circulating in the blood and generally do not shed FeLV. They are unlikely to develop FeLV-related illness, although reactivation can rarely occur if the immune system becomes severely compromised.

### Progressive Infection

In a progressive infection, the immune system cannot control the virus. FeLV spreads through the body, including the bone marrow, and the cat remains persistently infected.

Progressively infected cats are more likely to:

- Shed the virus to other cats
- Develop immune suppression
- Develop anemia
- Develop lymphoma or leukemia
- Experience recurrent infections
- Have a shortened lifespan

A single positive screening test may not fully identify the type or stage of infection. Your veterinarian may recommend additional tests and repeat testing over time.  

## Feline Leukemia Virus Symptoms

Some cats show no symptoms during the early stages of infection. Others develop vague signs that may look like many common feline illnesses. Symptoms can appear weeks, months, or even years after exposure.

Possible signs of FeLV include:

- Loss of appetite
- Gradual weight loss
- Poor coat condition
- Persistent fever
- Weakness or unusual tiredness
- Pale gums
- Swollen lymph nodes
- Chronic diarrhea
- Repeated respiratory infections
- Recurrent skin or urinary tract infections
- Gingivitis or painful inflammation of the mouth
- Eye inflammation
- Breathing difficulty
- Neurologic changes or seizures
- Reproductive problems
- Yellowing of the gums or whites of the eyes
- Poor growth in kittens

FeLV-positive cats may experience cycles of illness followed by periods when they seem normal. Because a weakened immune system can make minor infections more serious, contact your veterinarian promptly if your cat develops poor appetite, lethargy, fever, vomiting, diarrhea, discharge, or breathing problems.

## How Is Feline Leukemia Virus Diagnosed?

FeLV is diagnosed with blood testing. The most common first test is an in-clinic antigen test, often an ELISA or a similar point-of-care test. It detects a viral protein called p27 in the blood.

Your veterinarian may recommend FeLV testing:

- When you first adopt a cat
- Before introducing a new cat to your household
- Before the first FeLV vaccination
- After a bite wound or possible exposure
- When a cat becomes ill
- If a cat goes outdoors
- When a cat lives with an FeLV-positive or unknown-status cat

A positive screening test should generally be confirmed, particularly if the cat is healthy or considered low risk. Confirmatory testing may include laboratory antigen testing, immunofluorescence assay, or polymerase chain reaction testing for FeLV genetic material.

A negative result may also require repetition if exposure occurred recently. Antigen may not be detectable immediately after infection, so your veterinarian may recommend retesting at least 30 days after the last possible exposure.  

### Does the FeLV Vaccine Cause a Positive Test?

No. FeLV vaccination does not cause a positive result on commonly used FeLV antigen tests, including ELISA and IFA testing. However, cats should still be tested before vaccination because the vaccine does not treat an existing infection. 

## Is There a Cure for FeLV?

At this time, there is no definitive cure that removes feline leukemia virus from an infected cat’s body. Treatment focuses on supporting the immune system, preventing complications, and managing specific diseases as early as possible.

Veterinary care may include:

- Antibiotics for bacterial infections
- Dental treatment for severe gingivitis or stomatitis
- Blood transfusions for serious anemia
- Fluid therapy for dehydration
- Nutritional support
- Parasite prevention
- Treatment for cancer when appropriate
- Medication for inflammation or pain
- Antiviral medication in selected cases

Some antiviral drugs may reduce viral levels or help with specific clinical signs, but they are not universally effective and may have side effects. Your veterinarian will determine whether a particular treatment is appropriate based on your cat’s symptoms, test results, and overall health. 

## Caring for an FeLV-Positive Cat

An FeLV-positive cat may still enjoy a good quality of life. A positive result alone is not a reason to assume your cat is suffering or to make an immediate end-of-life decision. Instead, work with your veterinarian to create a long-term health plan.

### Keep Your Cat Indoors

Indoor living helps prevent exposure to additional infections, reduces the risk of injuries, and prevents transmission to other cats. An indoor lifestyle can include window perches, climbing structures, interactive toys, food puzzles, and safe enclosed outdoor spaces.

### Schedule Veterinary Visits Every Six Months

FeLV-positive cats should generally receive preventive examinations at least twice a year. These visits allow your veterinarian to monitor:

- Body weight and muscle condition
- Lymph nodes
- Mouth and teeth
- Eyes and skin
- Complete blood count
- Kidney and liver function
- Urinalysis
- Signs of infection or cancer

A complete blood count may be recommended at least every six months because FeLV can cause blood-cell abnormalities. Blood chemistry testing and urinalysis are commonly performed at least annually or more often when medically indicated. 

### Feed a Complete, Balanced Diet

Good nutrition supports your cat’s overall health. Feed a commercially prepared, complete, and balanced diet appropriate for your cat’s life stage. Avoid raw meat, raw eggs, and unpasteurized dairy products because foodborne bacteria and parasites may cause more serious illness in an immunocompromised cat.

### Treat Illness Early

Do not wait several days to see whether symptoms improve. FeLV-positive cats can become seriously ill from infections that might be mild in a healthy cat. Call your veterinarian if your cat develops:

- Reduced appetite
- Vomiting or diarrhea
- Sneezing or nasal discharge
- Coughing or difficulty breathing
- Pale gums
- Mouth pain
- Fever
- Sudden weakness
- Weight loss
- Changes in urination

### Reduce Stress

Stress can negatively affect a cat’s health and behavior. Provide a quiet, predictable home with clean litter boxes, resting areas, hiding places, and separate resources in multi-cat households.

## Should an FeLV-Positive Cat Live With Other Cats?

Ideally, FeLV-positive cats should be separated from cats that test negative. Cats in the same household should be tested, and uninfected cats should be protected from exposure.

If separation is not possible, discuss the risk with your veterinarian. In some households, FeLV vaccination may reduce the risk to uninfected cats, but vaccination is not 100% protective. Preventing close contact remains important.

Do not introduce an untested cat into a home with an FeLV-positive cat or a vulnerable FeLV-negative cat. Test newly acquired cats before bringing them into shared living spaces, and follow your veterinarian’s quarantine and retesting recommendations.

## Preventing Feline Leukemia Virus

Prevention begins with reducing exposure and identifying infection early.

### Test New Cats

Have every newly adopted cat tested before introducing it to resident cats. A recently infected cat may initially test negative, so follow-up testing may be needed if there is a known exposure or uncertain history.

### Keep Cats From Roaming

Outdoor roaming increases the chance of fighting, bite wounds, and contact with cats of unknown FeLV status. A secure catio, enclosed yard, or supervised outdoor time can provide enrichment without the same level of exposure.

### Vaccinate When Appropriate

FeLV vaccination is recommended for kittens and cats with meaningful exposure risk. The initial series commonly begins at approximately 8 weeks of age, followed by a second dose 3 to 4 weeks later and a booster one year after the initial series. Future boosters depend on lifestyle and risk.  

Vaccination is especially important for:

- Kittens
- Outdoor cats
- Cats that may escape outdoors
- Cats living with cats of unknown status
- Cats in foster, shelter, or group-housing environments
- Cats living with a known FeLV-positive cat

Strictly indoor adult cats in stable, FeLV-negative households may have lower risk. Your veterinarian can help you weigh the benefits and potential risks based on your cat’s lifestyle.

## Frequently Asked Questions About FeLV

### Can people catch feline leukemia virus?

No. FeLV infects cats and does not spread to people, dogs, or other common household pets. 

### Can an FeLV-positive cat live a normal life?

Some FeLV-positive cats remain healthy for years, particularly when they receive routine veterinary care, live indoors, eat a balanced diet, and avoid exposure to additional infections. Progressive infection generally reduces life expectancy, but individual outcomes vary considerably.

### Should a healthy cat be euthanized after a positive FeLV test?

Not necessarily. A positive screening test should be confirmed, and your veterinarian should determine whether the cat has a progressive, regressive, or other form of infection. Healthy FeLV-positive cats may have a good quality of life for a prolonged period.

### Can FeLV be spread by sharing a litter box?

Yes, shared litter boxes may contribute to transmission, although prolonged close contact, saliva, grooming, and bite wounds are more important routes. Separating infected and uninfected cats is the safest approach.

### Can an FeLV-positive cat be vaccinated?

The FeLV vaccine does not benefit a cat that is already infected. However, your veterinarian may recommend other vaccines based on the cat’s health, lifestyle, and risk of infectious disease. Vaccination decisions should be individualized.

### Is feline leukemia virus the same as FIV?

No. FeLV and feline immunodeficiency virus are different retroviruses. They can both weaken a cat’s immune system, but they differ in transmission patterns, testing, disease progression, and management. Your veterinarian may test for both viruses.

### How often should an FeLV-positive cat see a veterinarian?

At least every six months is commonly recommended, with more frequent visits if your cat has symptoms or an ongoing medical condition. Regular examinations help detect infections, anemia, dental disease, and other complications early.

## Final Thoughts

Feline leukemia virus is a serious but manageable disease. It spreads primarily through close contact between cats and can lead to immune suppression, anemia, cancer, and repeated illness. Testing, indoor living, careful household management, and appropriate vaccination are the most effective ways to reduce risk.

If your cat tests positive, focus on practical next steps: confirm the result, test household cats, prevent transmission, schedule regular checkups, and seek veterinary care at the first sign of illness. With attentive care and a supportive home, many FeLV-positive cats can continue to experience comfort, companionship, and meaningful quality of life.
