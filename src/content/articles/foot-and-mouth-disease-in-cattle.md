Foot and mouth disease in cattle is one of the most contagious livestock diseases in the world. It can spread rapidly through a herd, reduce milk and meat production, cause painful mouth and hoof lesions, and create serious restrictions on animal movement and trade.

Although adult cattle often survive, recovery does not always mean a complete return to normal health or productivity. Young calves may die suddenly from heart complications, while adult cattle can experience long-term weight loss, chronic lameness, mastitis, and reduced milk yield.

Knowing how to recognize foot and mouth disease symptoms, how the virus spreads, and what to do if you suspect an infection is essential for every cattle owner, farmer, livestock worker, and veterinary professional.

## What Is Foot and Mouth Disease in Cattle?

Foot and mouth disease, commonly called FMD, is a severe viral infection caused by foot-and-mouth disease virus, or FMDV. The virus belongs to the *Aphthovirus* genus in the *Picornaviridae* family.

FMD primarily affects animals with divided or cloven hooves, including:

- Cattle
- Water buffalo
- Pigs
- Sheep
- Goats
- Deer
- Antelope
- Other susceptible wild ruminants

There are seven recognized FMD virus serotypes: O, A, C, Asia 1, SAT 1, SAT 2, and SAT 3. Immunity to one serotype does not necessarily protect cattle against another. This is one reason why foot and mouth disease vaccination must be carefully matched to the virus strains circulating in a particular region.  

FMD is not the same as hand, foot, and mouth disease in children. The two illnesses are caused by different viruses. Foot and mouth disease in livestock is not generally considered a human health or food safety threat, although people can rarely become infected after very close contact with the virus. 

## Why Foot and Mouth Disease Is a Serious Threat

FMD is highly contagious. In a susceptible, unvaccinated population, almost every animal may become infected. Adult mortality is usually low, but death rates can be considerably higher in newborn calves and other young animals.

The greatest damage often comes from:

- Sudden loss of milk production
- Reduced weight gain
- Chronic lameness
- Abortion and reproductive problems
- Secondary bacterial infections
- Calf deaths from myocarditis
- Restrictions on livestock movement
- Slaughter or depopulation requirements
- Disruption of domestic and international trade

A country or region can lose its disease-free status after a confirmed outbreak. Even one detection may lead to movement controls, quarantine zones, trade suspensions, tracing investigations, and the humane destruction of infected or exposed livestock. 

## How Foot and Mouth Disease Spreads

FMDV can spread through direct contact and indirect contact. Infected cattle shed virus in saliva, milk, breath, urine, feces, and other secretions. Animals may also spread the virus before obvious clinical signs appear.

Common routes of transmission include:

### Direct animal contact

Healthy cattle can become infected when they share air, feed, water, housing, transport vehicles, or pasture with infected animals. Nose-to-nose contact is particularly risky.

### Contaminated clothing and footwear

People can carry the virus on boots, clothing, gloves, and hands. A visitor who has recently been around infected livestock may unknowingly introduce FMDV to another farm.

### Vehicles and equipment

Trailers, trucks, tractors, handling equipment, feed containers, milking equipment, and veterinary tools may all become contaminated. Mud, manure, saliva, milk, and other organic material can protect the virus and make cleaning more difficult.

### Feed, water, and animal products

Contaminated feed and water can transmit FMD. The virus may also be carried in infected milk, meat products, hides, semen, and other biological materials.

### Airborne spread

Under suitable conditions, FMDV can travel through aerosols and wind. This is especially important near high-density livestock operations. The distance depends on weather, humidity, terrain, the amount of virus released, and the animal species involved. 

### Wildlife

Some wild cloven-hoofed animals can become infected. African buffalo are especially important carriers for certain FMD virus serotypes. Wildlife contact can complicate disease control in affected regions.

The virus can survive outside the animal in moist, cool environments and organic materials. It may persist in manure, slurry, hay, soil, hair, vehicles, and livestock facilities for variable periods depending on temperature, humidity, pH, and contamination levels. 

## Foot and Mouth Disease Symptoms in Cattle

The incubation period in cattle is generally two to 14 days. Some animals develop signs within only a few days, while others may show mild or inapparent infection.

The most important foot and mouth disease symptoms include:

- Fever
- Depression or unusual weakness
- Reduced appetite
- Excessive drooling
- Sticky, foamy, or stringy saliva
- Lip smacking or teeth grinding
- Nasal discharge
- Mouth blisters or erosions
- Tongue lesions
- Foot lesions
- Sudden lameness
- Reluctance to stand or walk
- Reduced milk production
- Weight loss
- Abortion
- Poor conception
- Sudden death in young calves

### Mouth lesions

Blisters, also called vesicles, may develop on the tongue, gums, lips, dental pad, hard palate, muzzle, nostrils, and other areas inside the mouth. These blisters often rupture quickly, leaving painful raw erosions.

Because the blisters may disappear soon after forming, you may see only red, ulcerated, or damaged tissue. Pain makes cattle reluctant to eat, chew, or drink. Excessive salivation is common.

### Hoof and leg lesions

Lesions may form between the toes, around the coronary band, above the hoof, and on the heel bulbs. As the lesions rupture, cattle may become severely lame.

Affected animals may:

- Shift weight from one foot to another
- Walk stiffly
- Stand with their feet spread apart
- Lie down more than usual
- Refuse to move
- Stamp their feet
- Develop hoof damage or sloughing in severe cases

Secondary bacterial infections can make hoof lesions worse and may lead to chronic lameness.

### Udder and teat lesions

Dairy cattle may develop vesicles and erosions on the teats and udder. These lesions can make milking painful and increase the risk of mastitis.

A sudden drop in milk production may be one of the earliest noticeable signs in a dairy herd. Milk output may remain below normal even after the animal appears to recover. 

### Signs in young calves

Young calves may show few obvious blisters. Instead, they may develop myocarditis, or inflammation and damage of the heart muscle.

Possible signs include:

- Weakness
- Collapse
- Rapid breathing
- Sudden death
- Poor nursing
- Failure to thrive

Calf deaths can occur before the classic mouth and hoof lesions are recognized. For this reason, unexplained deaths in young stock during a suspected outbreak require urgent veterinary investigation.

## FMD Can Look Like Other Cattle Diseases

Foot and mouth disease cannot be diagnosed reliably by appearance alone. Several diseases can cause blisters, mouth erosions, lameness, fever, or reduced production.

Possible look-alike conditions include:

- Vesicular stomatitis
- Bluetongue
- Bovine viral diarrhea
- Foot rot
- Chemical burns
- Traumatic mouth injuries
- Other vesicular diseases

The only way to confirm FMD is through appropriate laboratory testing. Diagnostic methods may include real-time RT-PCR, virus isolation, antigen ELISA, and antibody testing. Fresh vesicular epithelium or fluid is particularly valuable for laboratory diagnosis. 

## What to Do if You Suspect FMD

Treat any suspected case as an emergency. Do not wait for more animals to become sick and do not move cattle away from the farm.

Follow these steps:

1. **Stop animal movement.** Do not sell, transport, show, slaughter, or relocate cattle.
2. **Contact your veterinarian immediately.** Explain the signs, recent animal movements, visitor history, and possible exposures.
3. **Notify the appropriate animal health authority.** FMD is a reportable or notifiable disease in many countries.
4. **Isolate visibly sick animals if authorities advise it.** Avoid unnecessary handling and do not mix them with other livestock.
5. **Restrict access to the farm.** Stop visitors, contractors, delivery vehicles, and nonessential personnel from entering.
6. **Do not share equipment.** Keep tools, clothing, footwear, vehicles, and handling facilities away from other farms.
7. **Do not open blisters or collect samples yourself.** Veterinary authorities should collect and transport samples safely.
8. **Record animal movements.** Document purchases, sales, markets, shows, visitors, vehicles, and farm contacts.

In the United States, suspected FMD should be reported immediately to a veterinarian, state animal health official, or the USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service. 

## Is There a Treatment for Foot and Mouth Disease?

There is no specific antiviral treatment that eliminates FMDV from infected cattle. Treatment decisions are controlled by veterinary and animal health authorities because FMD is a major reportable disease.

In endemic areas, supportive care may be considered under veterinary direction. This may include:

- Providing clean water
- Offering soft, palatable feed
- Protecting damaged feet from further trauma
- Cleaning lesions appropriately
- Managing secondary bacterial infections
- Providing shade, dry bedding, and comfortable housing
- Monitoring milk production and calf health

Do not give antibiotics, pain medication, antiseptics, or home remedies without veterinary guidance. Antibiotics do not kill the FMD virus, although a veterinarian may prescribe them when secondary bacterial infections are present.

In FMD-free regions, control commonly involves quarantine, movement restrictions, tracing, environmental disinfection, and the humane destruction of infected and high-risk contact animals. Vaccination may also be used depending on national policy and the outbreak situation. 

## How to Prevent Foot and Mouth Disease in Cattle

Strong daily biosecurity is your best defense.

### Control farm access

Use a single controlled entrance when possible. Keep a visitor log and ask visitors about recent contact with livestock. Do not allow unnecessary access to barns, pastures, feed areas, or livestock handling facilities.

### Use clean clothing and footwear

Provide farm-specific boots and clothing. Require visitors to use clean protective equipment and leave it on the farm when they depart.

### Clean before disinfecting

Disinfectants work poorly when manure, mud, milk, and other organic material are present. First remove visible contamination, wash the surface, and then apply an approved disinfectant at the correct concentration and contact time.

Clean and disinfect:

- Boots
- Vehicle tires and wheel wells
- Trailers
- Chutes
- Scales
- Handling equipment
- Milking equipment
- Feed and water containers

### Quarantine new cattle

Keep new or returning animals separate from the established herd for a period recommended by your veterinarian and animal health authority. Monitor them daily for fever, lameness, drooling, appetite changes, and lesions.

Quarantine is especially important for animals returning from:

- Livestock markets
- Shows and fairs
- Sales
- Feedlots
- Shared grazing areas
- Other farms
- Veterinary or breeding facilities

### Manage animal movement

Avoid mixing cattle from different sources. Maintain accurate records of where animals came from and where they have gone.

Limit contact between neighboring herds. Fencing can reduce nose-to-nose contact, and separate water sources may reduce contamination.

### Follow vaccination rules

FMD vaccines are available in many regions, but vaccination policy differs by country. Vaccines must match the relevant virus serotype and may not provide protection against every strain.

Vaccinated cattle can still become infected or carry virus, so vaccination does not replace biosecurity, surveillance, movement controls, or reporting. Always follow your veterinarian’s vaccination recommendations and local regulations. 

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can foot and mouth disease affect humans?

FMD primarily affects cloven-hoofed animals. It is not considered a routine public health or food safety threat, and it is unrelated to human hand, foot, and mouth disease. People can mechanically carry FMDV on clothing, footwear, hands, or equipment, so human biosecurity remains important.

### What is the first sign of FMD in cattle?

Early signs may include fever, depression, reduced appetite, excessive salivation, decreased milk production, and lameness. Blisters and erosions in the mouth, on the feet, or on the teats may appear later.

### Can cattle recover from foot and mouth disease?

Many adult cattle survive, but recovery may be followed by weight loss, chronic lameness, mastitis, reduced milk production, and reproductive problems. Young calves are at greater risk of death from heart damage.

### Is foot and mouth disease the same as foot rot?

No. Foot rot is a bacterial infection that usually causes swelling, pain, and tissue damage around the foot. FMD is a highly contagious viral disease that causes vesicles and erosions in the mouth, on the feet, and sometimes on the teats. Laboratory testing is needed to distinguish FMD from similar diseases.

### Should you move a sick cow away from the herd?

Do not move the animal off the property. Contact your veterinarian and animal health authorities first. They will advise whether and how isolation should be carried out while minimizing contamination and disease spread.

### Can disinfectant kill FMD virus?

Appropriate disinfectants can inactivate FMDV when used correctly, but cleaning must come first. Manure and other organic material can reduce disinfectant effectiveness. Use products approved by your animal health authority and follow label instructions carefully.

### Does vaccination completely prevent FMD?

Vaccination can reduce disease severity and transmission, but protection depends on the vaccine matching the circulating serotype and strain. Vaccinated cattle may still become infected or carry virus, so vaccination must be combined with biosecurity and surveillance.

## Conclusion

Foot and mouth disease in cattle demands rapid recognition and immediate action. Fever, drooling, mouth erosions, hoof lesions, lameness, and sudden production losses should never be ignored, especially when multiple animals are affected.

Do not attempt to diagnose FMD based on appearance alone. Stop animal movement, contact your veterinarian, notify animal health authorities, and follow official instructions for testing, quarantine, cleaning, vaccination, and control.

The most effective protection comes from prevention: control farm access, quarantine new cattle, disinfect equipment, monitor animals every day, maintain movement records, and report suspicious signs without delay. Early detection can protect your herd, neighboring farms, livestock markets, and the wider agricultural industry.
