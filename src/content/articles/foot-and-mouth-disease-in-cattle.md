# Foot and Mouth Disease in Cattle

## Introduction

Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) is a highly contagious viral disease that affects cattle and other cloven-hoofed animals such as sheep, goats and pigs. It is considered one of the most important animal diseases in the world because it spreads very quickly, causes severe production losses, and can shut down national and international trade in animals and animal products. Although adult cattle usually survive, the economic impact on farmers and entire countries can be devastating.  

The disease is caused by the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), an aphthovirus in the Picornaviridae family. There are multiple serotypes (strains) of the virus, and immunity to one serotype does not protect against the others. This makes long-term control difficult in regions where the virus is common. In many countries, FMD is a legally notifiable disease and any suspected case must be reported immediately to veterinary authorities.  

FMD does not infect humans in the same way as it affects cattle, but outbreaks can still indirectly threaten human livelihoods and food security through loss of milk, meat, and draft power, and through trade bans. In FMD-free countries, enormous effort goes into preventing the introduction of the virus. In endemic regions, farmers must work closely with veterinarians and veterinary authorities to reduce the impact of the disease and to prevent spread between farms and across borders. 

## Causes

FMD in cattle is caused by foot-and-mouth disease virus, which is extremely infectious and can be carried over long distances in various ways. The virus mainly spreads through direct contact with infected animals and their secretions, including saliva, milk, semen, respiratory droplets, and fluid from blisters. Cattle can start shedding virus before they show visible signs, which makes early spread silent and hard to detect without laboratory testing.  

Indirect transmission is also very important. The virus can survive on contaminated equipment, vehicles, clothing, footwear, feed, and bedding. People moving between farms, sharing tools or vehicles, and uncontrolled movement of animals are major risk factors. In some outbreaks, slaughterhouse waste, swill feeding, and windborne spread from infected pig units to cattle herds have been documented as significant routes of transmission. Birds and other wild animals can also carry the virus mechanically over distance.  

Carrier animals add another layer of complexity. Some cattle that recover from FMD may continue to carry the virus in their pharynx for months, even when they look healthy. These carriers may help maintain the virus in endemic regions. Poor farm-level biosecurity, lack of quarantine for new arrivals, and inadequate disinfection practices allow the virus to persist in the environment and move between herds and villages. 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms

The typical incubation period in cattle (time from infection to first signs) is about 2–14 days, depending on the strain of virus, the dose, and the immune status of the animal. Early signs often include sudden fever (around 40°C or 104°F), depression, loss of appetite, and a sharp drop in milk production. Cattle may stand apart from the herd, look dull, and show excessive salivation, sometimes with strings of frothy drool hanging from the mouth.  

Within a short time after the fever, characteristic blisters (vesicles) appear in the mouth and on the feet. In the mouth, vesicles develop on the tongue, dental pad, gums, lips, and sometimes the hard palate. These blisters quickly burst, leaving raw, painful ulcers. Affected cattle may champ their jaws, grind their teeth, or appear to “smack” their lips because of mouth pain. They often refuse to eat or drink because chewing and swallowing are painful, leading to weight loss and further drop in milk yield.  

On the feet, vesicles form at the coronary band (where hair meets the hoof), in the interdigital space between the claws, and sometimes above the hooves. When these blisters rupture, they cause severe lameness; animals may lie down and resist standing or walking. The teats of lactating cows can also develop vesicles and ulcers, making milking painful and predisposing to mastitis. In young calves, the virus can attack the heart muscle, causing sudden death from myocarditis, sometimes without the typical mouth or foot lesions. Survivors may suffer from chronic poor growth and reduced productivity. 

## Diagnosis

Because FMD is a regulated, trade-sensitive disease, diagnosis must be made quickly and accurately. Initial suspicion usually arises from clinical signs: fever, excessive salivation, and vesicles or erosions in the mouth, on the feet, and on teats in multiple animals in a herd. However, several other diseases—such as vesicular stomatitis and some other vesicular diseases—can produce very similar signs in cattle. Relying on clinical signs alone is not safe; laboratory confirmation is essential.  

When FMD is suspected, a veterinarian must be contacted immediately, and authorities may impose immediate movement controls on the farm. The veterinarian will collect appropriate samples, often including pieces of fresh vesicle epithelium, vesicle fluid, blood, and sometimes swabs from the mouth or nose. These samples are sent to a specialized reference laboratory capable of handling FMD virus under high biosecurity. The laboratory may use tests such as real-time reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR), virus isolation, antigen capture ELISA, or other molecular assays to detect and type the virus.  

Modern point-of-care diagnostic methods, such as isothermal amplification combined with lateral flow strips, are being developed and refined to allow faster testing closer to the farm. Still, for international reporting and trade, official confirmation usually relies on accredited laboratories. Rapid and accurate diagnosis not only guides control actions on the affected farm but also helps map the spread of the virus and identify which serotype is involved, which is crucial for vaccination planning and regional control programs. 

## Treatment

There is no specific antiviral drug that kills the FMD virus in cattle. Once animals are infected, treatment is mainly supportive, aimed at reducing pain, preventing secondary infections, and keeping them comfortable until they recover. In some endemic regions, animals that are not severely affected may be nursed through the disease. This includes providing soft, palatable feed that is easy to chew, ensuring clean and abundant water, and keeping animals on dry, comfortable bedding to reduce pain from foot lesions.  

Topical treatments may be used on mouth and foot lesions to reduce pain and encourage healing. Some studies have suggested that certain topical wound products, applied directly to FMD lesions, can speed recovery and reduce the severity of secondary infections, although these do not replace the need for strict biosecurity and official control measures. Systemic antibiotics do not act on the virus itself but may be prescribed to control bacterial complications such as foot infections or mastitis. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can help reduce fever and pain under veterinary supervision.  

In FMD-free countries, the standard policy during an outbreak is often to cull infected and high-risk contact animals rather than treat them, in order to rapidly stop virus circulation and regain FMD-free status. In endemic areas, decisions about treating versus culling depend on national policies, economic factors, and the scale of the outbreak. Regardless of the strategy, strict isolation of sick animals, disinfection of facilities and equipment, and control of movements on and off the farm are always essential. 

## Prevention and Control

Prevention of FMD in cattle relies on strong biosecurity, appropriate vaccination where used, and rapid response to any suspected outbreak. On the farm, this means controlling the entry of animals, people, vehicles, and equipment. New or returning cattle should be quarantined and observed before joining the main herd. Visitors should wear clean, dedicated clothing and footwear or use disinfectant footbaths and vehicle wash points. Sharing of equipment, trucks, or trailers between farms should be minimized or followed by thorough cleaning and disinfection.  

Vaccination is an important tool in many endemic regions and sometimes in emergency situations in FMD-free countries. Vaccines are serotype-specific and must match the circulating virus strains as closely as possible. Immunity from vaccination is not lifelong and requires regular boosters according to veterinary or national guidelines. Even in vaccinated populations, good biosecurity and surveillance are needed, because vaccine failures can occur and vaccinated animals can sometimes still become infected and shed virus.  

In the event of an outbreak, authorities may impose standstill orders on animal movement, set up restriction and surveillance zones, and carry out tracing to find all possible contacts of infected herds. In some control plans, all susceptible animals on affected premises are humanely culled, and their carcasses are safely disposed of. Buildings, equipment, and vehicles must then be thoroughly cleaned and disinfected with approved disinfectants effective against FMD virus. Long-term control at national and regional levels combines vaccination strategies, systematic surveillance, rapid laboratory diagnosis, and farmer education. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Farmers should contact a veterinarian immediately if they notice several cattle in a group showing rapid onset of fever, excessive salivation, reluctance to eat, and lameness, especially if blisters or raw erosions are seen in the mouth, on the tongue, or around the hooves and coronary bands. A sudden, sharp drop in milk yield across a herd, combined with mouth or foot lesions, should also raise strong suspicion. Do not wait to see if the condition “passes”; delays allow the virus to spread further and make control more difficult and costly.  

Young calves dying suddenly, especially during or shortly after an episode of mouth and foot lesions in the herd, is another urgent warning sign. Because FMD looks similar to other vesicular diseases, farmers should not attempt to make a final diagnosis themselves. Instead, they should isolate the affected animals as far as possible, stop animal movements on and off the farm, and follow veterinary instructions exactly. Quick reporting allows veterinary authorities to start proper investigation, sampling, and, if necessary, wider control measures to protect neighboring farms and regions. 

## Conclusion

Foot and Mouth Disease in cattle is a highly contagious viral infection that rarely kills adult animals but causes major suffering, production losses, and severe economic damage to individual farmers and to entire countries. The virus spreads quickly through both direct and indirect contacts, and infected cattle can shed virus before they show obvious signs. For these reasons, FMD is treated as an emergency disease requiring rapid recognition, immediate veterinary involvement, and strict control measures.  

Understanding the causes, typical signs, and basic principles of diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and control helps farmers, students, and animal owners play their part in protecting herds. Good biosecurity, appropriate vaccination where recommended, and early reporting of suspicious cases are the pillars of FMD control. Working closely with veterinarians and following national guidelines gives the best chance of limiting damage when outbreaks occur and of moving toward long-term reduction or elimination of this important disease.
