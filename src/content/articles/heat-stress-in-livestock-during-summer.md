Summer heat can become a serious health and welfare threat for livestock. When temperatures rise, animals must work harder to maintain a safe body temperature. High humidity, direct sunlight, poor ventilation, overcrowding, dehydration, and handling can quickly turn a hot day into a dangerous heat event.

Heat stress in livestock may reduce feed intake, growth, milk production, egg production, fertility, and immune function. In severe cases, it can cause collapse, organ damage, or death. The good news is that practical steps—especially reliable water, shade, airflow, and careful summer management—can greatly reduce the risk.

This guide explains how to recognize heat stress in cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, and poultry, and how to protect livestock throughout the hottest months of the year.

## What Is Heat Stress in Livestock?

Heat stress occurs when an animal absorbs or produces more heat than it can release. Livestock normally regulate body temperature through methods such as sweating, panting, changing posture, seeking shade, and drinking water.

During extreme weather, these cooling mechanisms may not be enough. High humidity is particularly dangerous because it reduces the effectiveness of evaporation from the skin and respiratory tract. Consecutive hot days and warm nights are also risky because animals may not have enough time to recover before the next heat event.  

Several conditions increase the likelihood of summer heat stress, including:

- High air temperature
- High relative humidity
- Direct sunlight
- Limited shade
- Poor air movement
- Dirty or inadequate water supplies
- Overcrowded pens
- Long-distance transport
- Exercise or stressful handling
- Heavy body weight
- Dark-colored coats
- Illness, lameness, or dehydration
- High milk production or rapid growth

The Temperature-Humidity Index, or THI, combines temperature and humidity to estimate heat risk. However, THI should not be used alone. Animals in direct sunlight, poorly ventilated buildings, or crowded holding areas may become stressed even when general weather readings appear moderate.

## Why Heat Stress Matters to Animal Health and Farm Productivity

Heat stress is more than temporary discomfort. It changes how animals eat, drink, move, reproduce, and respond to disease.

When livestock become overheated, they often reduce feed intake because digestion generates additional metabolic heat. This can lead to slower growth, poorer body condition, reduced milk yield, and lower egg production. Research reviewed by the Food and Agriculture Organization links heat stress with reduced feed intake, milk production, meat production, egg production, fertility, and immune function.  

Heat stress can also affect reproduction. In cattle, severe heat exposure may reduce breeding activity, impair sperm quality, increase early embryo loss, and affect pregnancy outcomes. Some effects may continue after the weather cools.

Other possible consequences include:

- Dehydration
- Reduced rumination
- Increased respiratory disease risk
- Weakened immune response
- Lower average daily gain
- Poor feed efficiency
- Reduced colostrum quality
- Lower birth weights
- Reduced sow milk production
- Poor meat quality
- Increased mortality

Because heat stress can affect animals before obvious collapse occurs, prevention should begin before temperatures reach their highest point.

## Common Signs of Heat Stress in Livestock

Early recognition gives you the best chance to cool an animal before its condition becomes life-threatening. Watch the herd, flock, or group several times each day during heat waves.

Common signs include:

- Faster breathing
- Panting
- Open-mouth breathing
- Drooling or foaming around the mouth
- Increased water consumption
- Reduced feed intake
- Lethargy or unusual stillness
- Reluctance to move
- Seeking shade or crowding around water
- Standing instead of lying down
- Wings held away from the body in poultry
- Shaking or poor coordination
- Staggering
- Elevated body temperature
- Collapse or unresponsiveness

Cattle may extend their necks, breathe heavily, drool, and hold their heads up. Severe panting may include a fully extended tongue, heavy salivation, and labored abdominal breathing.

Poultry commonly pant, reduce activity, drink more, eat less, and hold their wings away from their bodies to improve heat loss. Egg production may decline, and birds may become weak or lethargic.  

Do not wait for an animal to collapse before taking action. A livestock animal that is breathing rapidly, separated from the group, or refusing feed needs immediate attention.

## Which Livestock Are Most Vulnerable?

All livestock can experience heat stress, but risk varies by species, breed, age, body condition, and production level.

### Cattle

Dairy cattle are especially vulnerable because milk production creates additional metabolic heat. Lactating cows may begin experiencing production-related heat stress at relatively modest THI levels, particularly in humid climates. Dark-hided, heavy, sick, lame, recently calved, and high-producing cattle require close observation.

Beef cattle are also at risk, particularly heavy feedlot cattle and animals with dark coats. Cattle have limited sweating ability compared with some species, so they depend heavily on shade, airflow, water, and evaporative cooling.

### Pigs

Pigs are highly susceptible to heat stress because they have very limited ability to sweat. Heavy pigs, breeding animals, and pigs exposed to transport or handling are at increased risk.

Pigs benefit from shade, cool drinking water, good ventilation, and properly designed sprinklers or wallows. Thoroughly wetting pigs with large water droplets and allowing them to dry between applications can be more effective than fine misting, especially when there is adequate airflow. 

### Sheep and Goats

Sheep and goats may be affected by high temperatures, especially when they have thick fleeces, dark coats, limited shade, or poor access to water. Newly shorn sheep may also need protection from direct sun because excessive shearing can remove natural insulation and increase sun exposure.

### Poultry

Chickens, turkeys, ducks, and other poultry are sensitive to heat because they do not sweat. High stocking density, poor ventilation, wet litter, and inadequate water can quickly worsen conditions inside a poultry house.

Birds need clean, accessible water at all times. Poultry may require substantially more water during hot weather, and inadequate water intake can quickly lead to serious health problems. 

## 1. Provide Plenty of Cool, Clean Water

Water is the first line of defense against summer heat stress. Animals should have continuous access to clean drinking water, and water systems must be able to meet increased demand during hot weather.

Inspect water sources every day. Check:

- Trough cleanliness
- Water flow and refill rate
- Pumps, pipes, floats, and hoses
- Leaks or overturned containers
- Algae, manure, mud, or other contamination
- Whether timid animals can reach the water

Large groups may need multiple water points so dominant animals do not prevent smaller, younger, or lower-ranking animals from drinking. Placing troughs in shade can help keep water cooler and encourage consumption.

Be careful when restoring water access to animals that have been without water for an extended period. Sudden unrestricted drinking after prolonged deprivation may create serious health risks, including salt toxicity. Contact your veterinarian for a gradual rehydration plan if this situation occurs. 

## 2. Create Effective Shade

Shade reduces the amount of solar radiation animals absorb. Natural shade from healthy trees can be highly effective, while artificial shade cloth, roofed shelters, and portable structures can protect animals in open pastures and feedlots.

Good shade should:

- Be large enough for all animals to use comfortably
- Allow animals to spread out
- Have strong airflow
- Provide protection throughout the day
- Avoid trapping heat
- Have dry, stable footing
- Be located away from excessive mud and manure buildup

A shaded area that is too small can cause crowding, which increases body heat and restricts airflow. Some extension guidance recommends approximately 20 to 30 square feet of shade per adult cow, although exact space requirements depend on animal size, housing design, and local conditions.  

Do not assume a closed barn is automatically cooler. A poorly ventilated building may hold hot, humid air and become more dangerous than a shaded outdoor area.

## 3. Improve Ventilation and Air Movement

Air movement helps remove heat and moisture from the animal’s environment. Open barn sides, ridge vents, properly positioned fans, and unobstructed air inlets can make a major difference.

For enclosed livestock facilities:

- Clean and test fans before summer
- Remove blocked vents and unnecessary wind barriers
- Keep bedding dry
- Avoid overcrowding
- Use fans over resting, feeding, and holding areas
- Monitor humidity as well as temperature
- Maintain backup power for critical ventilation systems

Fans work best when they move air across the animals rather than simply circulating hot air near the ceiling. Cooling systems that add water must be paired with adequate ventilation; otherwise, humidity may rise and reduce evaporative cooling.

## 4. Use Sprinklers, Misters, and Other Cooling Methods Carefully

Water-based cooling can help cattle and pigs during periods of serious heat stress. For cattle, coarse droplets that wet the hair coat and then evaporate with moving air are generally more useful than fine mist in humid conditions.

A practical cooling system may combine:

1. Coarse water droplets to wet the animal.
2. Fans to increase evaporation.
3. A dry period before repeating the cycle.
4. Adequate drainage to prevent mud and sanitation problems.

Avoid creating a hot, humid environment by wetting animals without airflow. Also avoid startling livestock with sudden spraying, particularly if they are already distressed.

For an animal showing severe heat stress, move it slowly—or not at all if movement could worsen its condition—to shade with good airflow. Provide access to water and contact a veterinarian promptly.

## 5. Adjust Feeding During Hot Weather

Livestock often eat less during the hottest part of the day. You can reduce heat load by offering more feed during cooler periods, such as early morning and evening.

For ruminants:

- Feed a larger portion of the ration in the evening when practical.
- Keep feed fresh and remove spoiled material.
- Monitor intake closely.
- Work with a nutritionist before changing fiber, energy, mineral, or electrolyte levels.
- Avoid sudden ration changes.

Do not make major diet changes without professional guidance. Reducing heat stress is important, but cattle, sheep, and goats still need adequate effective fiber and balanced nutrition for proper rumen function.

In poultry, check feeders frequently and protect feed from heat, moisture, mold, and nutrient deterioration.

## 6. Reduce Handling, Transport, and Other Stress

Handling raises body temperature and can push an already stressed animal into an emergency. Avoid working, sorting, vaccinating, loading, or transporting livestock during the hottest hours whenever possible.

Schedule necessary activities for early morning. Move animals calmly, quietly, and in small groups. Limit time in holding pens and avoid overcrowding trailers.

Before transport:

- Check the weather along the entire route.
- Confirm animals are healthy and fit to travel.
- Provide water before loading.
- Maximize trailer ventilation.
- Avoid unnecessary stops.
- Travel during the coolest part of the day.
- Reduce stocking density when conditions require it.

Cattle handling guidance recommends postponing work at elevated THI levels whenever possible and avoiding transportation during extreme heat. 

## A Practical Heat Stress Action Plan

Prepare before a heat wave arrives. Your summer livestock management plan should include:

### Before the heat event

- Check weather forecasts for temperature, humidity, and nighttime lows.
- Inspect waterers, pumps, pipes, and backup supplies.
- Clean troughs and test water flow.
- Repair fans and shade structures.
- Identify high-risk animals.
- Move vulnerable animals to the coolest available area.
- Reschedule handling and transport.
- Prepare emergency contact information for your veterinarian.

### During the heat event

- Check animals early in the morning, during the afternoon, and again in the evening.
- Observe breathing rate, posture, appetite, and water access.
- Run fans and cooling systems as planned.
- Maintain shade and avoid overcrowding.
- Offer feed during cooler hours.
- Separate and monitor weak or high-risk animals carefully.
- Record unusual illness, deaths, production drops, or water consumption changes.

### After the heat event

- Continue monitoring for several days.
- Check for dehydration, respiratory illness, lameness, and reduced appetite.
- Watch reproductive performance and milk or egg production.
- Inspect cooling equipment for failures.
- Review what worked and improve your plan before the next heat wave.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Contact a veterinarian immediately if an animal:

- Collapses
- Cannot stand
- Is unresponsive
- Has severe open-mouth breathing
- Shows seizures or staggering
- Has a very high body temperature
- Cannot drink
- Does not improve after cooling
- Has multiple group members showing serious signs

Do not force a severely heat-stressed animal to walk. Move it only when necessary and use low-stress handling. Veterinary treatment may include fluids, supportive care, and evaluation for complications.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Heat Stress in Livestock

### At what temperature does livestock heat stress begin?

There is no single temperature that applies to every animal. Risk depends on humidity, breed, age, coat color, body condition, production level, shade, airflow, and acclimation. Dairy cattle may show effects at lower temperatures than some beef cattle, particularly when humidity is high.

### What is the fastest way to cool a heat-stressed animal?

Move the animal to shade with good airflow, provide cool, clean water, and use appropriate water cooling if available. Avoid forcing the animal to walk or using stressful handling. Severe cases require immediate veterinary assistance.

### Is shade enough to prevent heat stress?

Shade is essential but may not be enough during extreme heat. The most effective plan combines shade, water, ventilation, air movement, reduced handling, and appropriate cooling systems.

### Should you mist cattle during humid weather?

Fine mist may increase humidity without adequately cooling the animal. Coarse droplets that wet the skin or hair coat, combined with strong airflow, are generally more effective. Make sure animals can dry between applications.

### Why are pigs especially vulnerable during summer?

Pigs have little ability to cool themselves through sweating. They depend on shade, drinking water, wallowing or sprinkling systems, and ventilation. Heavy pigs and breeding animals are particularly vulnerable.

### Can heat stress affect livestock after temperatures fall?

Yes. Animals may continue to experience reduced appetite, production losses, fertility problems, dehydration, or illness after the heat event. Keep monitoring them for several days after conditions improve.

### Should you feed livestock during the hottest part of the day?

Whenever practical, provide more feed during cooler morning and evening hours. This may reduce heat produced by digestion during peak temperatures. Consult a livestock nutritionist before changing the ration significantly.

## Conclusion

Heat stress in livestock during summer is preventable when you plan ahead and respond early. Fresh water, effective shade, strong ventilation, adequate space, sensible feeding schedules, and low-stress handling form the foundation of good summer livestock management.

Inspect your equipment before temperatures rise, monitor animals frequently, and pay attention to early changes in breathing, appetite, posture, and behavior. A proactive heat stress prevention plan protects animal welfare, supports productivity, and can save lives during the most dangerous days of summer.
