Keeping your dog healthy is about more than choosing a good food or taking an occasional walk. Your dog’s long-term wellness depends on a combination of preventive veterinary care, balanced nutrition, regular exercise, dental hygiene, parasite protection, mental stimulation, and a safe home environment.

The good news is that many of the most important dog health habits are simple. By paying attention to your dog’s daily routine and working closely with your veterinarian, you can help prevent illness, identify problems early, and support a longer, happier life.

## 1. Schedule Regular Veterinary Checkups

One of the best ways to keep your dog healthy is to schedule routine wellness examinations—even when your dog seems perfectly well. Dogs can hide pain and early disease, so visible symptoms may not appear until a condition has progressed.

A wellness visit may include:

- A complete physical examination
- Weight and body condition evaluation
- Dental and gum assessment
- Heart and lung checks
- Joint, muscle, and mobility evaluation
- Skin, coat, eyes, and ears examination
- Vaccination review
- Parasite prevention recommendations
- Nutrition and behavior discussion
- Age-appropriate blood, urine, or stool testing

Adult dogs should generally receive a veterinary examination at least once a year. Puppies, senior dogs, dogs with chronic illnesses, and dogs taking medication may need more frequent visits. AAHA guidance emphasizes that preventive care should be adapted to your dog’s life stage, breed, lifestyle, and health history.   

Before each appointment, make a list of changes you have noticed. Mention changes in appetite, thirst, sleep, activity, stool, urination, behavior, or mobility. Small details can help your veterinarian detect health problems earlier.

## 2. Feed a Complete and Balanced Diet

Good nutrition provides the foundation for your dog’s health. Choose a complete and balanced dog food appropriate for your dog’s age, size, activity level, and medical needs.

Puppies have different nutritional requirements from adult dogs, while senior dogs may benefit from dietary adjustments based on their joints, kidneys, weight, or other health concerns. Large-breed puppies may also need carefully controlled growth nutrition to reduce the risk of orthopedic problems.

When choosing dog food, consider:

- Your dog’s life stage
- Breed or body size
- Activity level
- Existing medical conditions
- Food allergies or sensitivities
- Feeding directions and calorie content
- Your veterinarian’s recommendations

Measure meals rather than estimating portions. Feeding too much—even high-quality food—can lead to excess weight. Treats should be included in your dog’s daily calorie intake, not added on top of unlimited meals.

Always provide fresh, clean water. Increased thirst may be caused by heat or exercise, but a sudden or persistent increase in drinking should be discussed with your veterinarian.

Avoid making frequent, abrupt diet changes. If you need to switch foods, transition gradually over several days unless your veterinarian gives you different instructions. A slow transition can reduce digestive upset.

## 3. Maintain a Healthy Weight

A healthy weight is one of the most important parts of dog wellness. Excess weight can place added strain on the joints and increase the risk of diabetes, heart and respiratory disease, arthritis, and other health problems. Veterinary guidelines recommend regular assessment of body weight, body condition, and muscle condition.   

You should be able to feel your dog’s ribs with gentle pressure, but the ribs should not be sharply visible in most dogs. Your dog should also have a visible waist when viewed from above and an abdominal tuck when viewed from the side.

To help your dog maintain a healthy weight:

- Measure every meal
- Limit table scraps
- Use small, healthy treats
- Avoid free-feeding unless your veterinarian recommends it
- Weigh your dog regularly
- Monitor body shape, not just the number on the scale
- Include daily exercise
- Ask your veterinarian for a target weight

If your dog is overweight, avoid putting them on a crash diet. Rapid weight loss can be unhealthy, and your dog may have an underlying medical condition. Your veterinarian can create a safe weight-management plan involving calories, exercise, and follow-up monitoring.

## 4. Provide Daily Exercise

Dogs need regular physical activity, but the right amount depends on age, breed, size, health, and temperament. Exercise helps support a healthy weight, strong muscles, cardiovascular health, joint function, and emotional well-being.

Daily dog exercise may include:

- Walks
- Supervised play
- Fetch
- Swimming
- Hiking
- Training games
- Scent-based activities
- Food puzzles
- Safe off-leash activity in an enclosed area

Start slowly if your dog is inactive, overweight, elderly, or recovering from illness. Increase activity gradually and watch for signs of fatigue, overheating, limping, or discomfort.

Avoid intense exercise during very hot or humid weather. Dogs can overheat quickly, especially flat-faced breeds, overweight dogs, puppies, seniors, and dogs with heart or breathing problems. Walk during cooler parts of the day and provide water and shade.

Exercise should be appropriate for your dog’s physical condition. A short sniffing walk may be more suitable for a senior dog than a long run, while a working breed may need both physical exercise and mentally challenging activities.

## 5. Support Your Dog’s Mental Health

Mental stimulation is just as important as physical exercise. Bored dogs may develop destructive behaviors, excessive barking, digging, anxiety, or other behavior problems.

You can support your dog’s mental health by offering:

- Short positive-reinforcement training sessions
- Puzzle feeders
- Treat-search games
- Rotating toys
- Scent work
- Obedience practice
- Safe social interaction
- Age-appropriate play
- New walking routes and environments

Training also strengthens communication between you and your dog. Use rewards, praise, toys, and patience rather than punishment. If your dog develops fear, aggression, separation anxiety, or a sudden behavior change, speak with your veterinarian. Behavior changes can sometimes signal pain or illness.

Puppies benefit from safe, positive socialization during early development. Adult and senior dogs also need continued enrichment and gentle training to maintain confidence and quality of life.

## 6. Protect Your Dog From Parasites

Fleas, ticks, heartworms, and intestinal parasites can cause serious health problems. Some parasites may also spread disease to people and other animals.

Ask your veterinarian about year-round protection for:

- Heartworms
- Fleas
- Ticks
- Roundworms
- Hookworms
- Whipworms
- Tapeworms
- Other parasites common in your region

The right product depends on your dog’s age, weight, lifestyle, travel history, and local disease risks. Never use a product intended for another species or give medication without veterinary guidance.

Routine testing may also be necessary. Veterinary organizations recommend individualized parasite screening based on age, geography, exposure, and lifestyle. Dogs that visit dog parks, boarding facilities, hiking areas, or regions with high parasite prevalence may require additional protection or testing.

Pick up dog waste promptly in your yard and on walks. Wash your hands after handling feces, even when you use a waste bag. Parasites and other germs can be transmitted through contaminated soil or waste.  

## 7. Keep Vaccinations Current

Vaccinations help protect dogs from potentially severe or fatal infectious diseases. Your veterinarian will recommend a vaccination schedule based on your dog’s age, lifestyle, geographic location, travel, and exposure to other animals.

Core vaccines commonly protect against diseases such as:

- Rabies
- Canine distemper
- Canine parvovirus
- Canine adenovirus

Additional vaccines may be recommended for dogs that board, attend daycare, travel, hunt, swim, spend time outdoors, or live in areas where specific diseases are common. Vaccination protocols should be individualized rather than copied from another dog’s schedule.   

Keep a record of your dog’s vaccinations and ask your veterinarian when boosters are due. Puppies typically require a series of vaccinations because their immune protection develops over time.

## 8. Make Dental Care Part of the Routine

Dental disease is common in dogs and can cause pain, bad breath, difficulty chewing, inflamed gums, loose teeth, and infection. Many dogs continue eating despite oral discomfort, so a normal appetite does not necessarily mean your dog’s mouth is healthy.

Start a home dental routine by:

1. Letting your dog become comfortable with mouth handling.
2. Introducing a dog-safe toothbrush or finger brush.
3. Using toothpaste made specifically for dogs.
4. Brushing gently and briefly at first.
5. Gradually increasing the length and frequency of brushing.

Never use human toothpaste. Some human products contain ingredients that may be dangerous to dogs.

Your veterinarian should examine your dog’s teeth and gums during wellness visits. Professional dental cleaning may be recommended when plaque, tartar, gum disease, or painful dental problems are present. Home brushing supports dental health, but it does not replace veterinary examinations or professional treatment when needed.   

Avoid extremely hard bones, antlers, and toys that may fracture teeth. Ask your veterinary team which dental chews and toys are appropriate for your dog.

## 9. Groom Your Dog and Check for Changes

Regular grooming keeps your dog comfortable and gives you an opportunity to notice health problems early.

Depending on your dog’s coat and breed, grooming may include:

- Brushing
- Bathing
- Nail trimming
- Ear cleaning
- Paw care
- Coat trimming
- Checking for fleas and ticks
- Inspecting the skin for lumps, sores, or irritation

Brush your dog as often as needed to prevent mats and remove loose hair. Matted fur can trap moisture and irritate the skin.

Trim nails before they become long enough to affect walking. If you are uncomfortable trimming them, ask a groomer or veterinary professional for help.

Do not place cotton swabs deep inside your dog’s ears. If you notice odor, redness, discharge, head shaking, or frequent scratching, schedule an examination. Ear infections can become painful and may require prescription treatment.

## 10. Keep Toxic Foods and Household Hazards Away

Dogs are curious and often eat things they should not. Store medications, cleaning products, pesticides, fertilizers, batteries, and chemicals in secure cabinets.

Common foods that can be dangerous to dogs include:

- Chocolate
- Grapes and raisins
- Xylitol
- Onions, garlic, and chives
- Alcohol
- Caffeine
- Macadamia nuts
- Raw yeast dough
- Excessively salty foods
- Certain human medications

Xylitol can cause dangerously low blood sugar and may contribute to liver damage. Grapes and raisins can cause kidney injury, while chocolate and caffeine may cause abnormal heart rhythms, tremors, seizures, or other serious signs.   

If your dog eats a potentially toxic substance, do not wait for symptoms and do not induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional specifically tells you to do so. Contact your veterinarian or an animal poison control service immediately.

The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center can be reached at (888) 426-4435. 

## 11. Practice Good Hygiene and Safety

Keeping your dog healthy also helps protect your household. Wash your hands after handling dog food, bowls, toys, saliva, feces, or other pet supplies. Handwashing is particularly important before eating or preparing food.  

Additional dog safety habits include:

- Keep your dog on a leash where required
- Use a properly fitted collar or harness
- Ensure your dog has identification tags
- Microchip your dog and keep registration details current
- Secure fencing and gates
- Keep toxic plants out of reach
- Provide shade and temperature control
- Use a crate or restraint during car travel
- Supervise interactions with children
- Prevent access to unsafe wildlife or standing water

Microchipping is especially important because even indoor dogs and fenced dogs can escape.

## 12. Know When to Call the Veterinarian

Contact your veterinarian if you notice a significant or persistent change in your dog’s normal behavior or health.

Warning signs may include:

- Difficulty breathing
- Collapse or extreme weakness
- Repeated vomiting or diarrhea
- Blood in vomit, stool, or urine
- Sudden loss of appetite
- Excessive thirst or urination
- Seizures
- Severe pain
- A swollen abdomen
- Pale, blue, or yellow gums
- Sudden blindness
- Inability to walk
- Persistent coughing
- Unexplained weight loss
- A rapidly growing lump

Some symptoms require emergency care. If you are unsure, call your veterinary clinic and describe what is happening. Early treatment often improves comfort and may prevent a manageable problem from becoming a crisis.

## A Simple Dog Health Checklist

### Every day

- Provide fresh water.
- Feed measured portions.
- Give prescribed medication and preventives as directed.
- Provide exercise and mental enrichment.
- Check appetite, energy, stool, and urination.
- Watch for limping, coughing, pain, or behavior changes.

### Every week

- Brush your dog as appropriate.
- Check ears, paws, skin, and coat.
- Inspect teeth and gums.
- Wash food and water bowls.
- Check your home and yard for hazards.

### Every month

- Check your dog’s weight or body condition.
- Confirm parasite prevention is current.
- Inspect identification tags and microchip records.
- Review medication supplies.
- Update your health notes if anything has changed.

### Every six to twelve months

- Schedule a veterinary wellness examination as recommended.
- Review vaccines and parasite testing.
- Discuss nutrition, weight, dental care, behavior, and exercise.
- Ask whether screening tests are appropriate for your dog’s age and health.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should my dog visit the veterinarian?

Most healthy adult dogs should have at least one wellness examination each year. Puppies, senior dogs, and dogs with chronic illnesses may need more frequent appointments. Your veterinarian will recommend a schedule based on your dog’s individual needs.

### What is the best food for my dog?

The best dog food is one that is complete and balanced for your dog’s life stage and appropriate for their health, size, and activity level. Ask your veterinarian for specific recommendations, especially if your dog has allergies, digestive problems, kidney disease, diabetes, or another medical condition.

### How much exercise does a dog need each day?

Exercise needs vary widely. A young, energetic working breed may need much more activity than a senior companion dog. Begin with manageable activity and adjust based on your dog’s age, breed, fitness, and health.

### How can I tell if my dog is overweight?

You should generally be able to feel your dog’s ribs without pressing hard, and your dog should have a visible waist and abdominal tuck. Your veterinarian can assign a body condition score and recommend a healthy target weight.

### Should I brush my dog’s teeth every day?

Daily brushing is ideal when your dog accepts it, but any consistent, dog-safe dental care is better than none. Use only dog toothpaste and schedule regular oral examinations with your veterinarian.

### Are heartworm and flea prevention really necessary year-round?

Many veterinary professionals recommend year-round parasite protection because exposure can occur in different seasons and even indoors. Your veterinarian can recommend products based on your location, climate, lifestyle, and the parasites common in your area.

### What should I do if my dog eats chocolate or xylitol?

Contact your veterinarian or an animal poison control service immediately. Do not wait for symptoms or attempt to induce vomiting unless directed by a veterinary professional. Be prepared to provide your dog’s weight, the product name, the amount eaten, and the time of exposure.

## Final Thoughts

The best way to keep your dog healthy is to combine everyday care with professional preventive medicine. Feed appropriate portions, provide regular exercise, protect your dog from parasites, maintain dental hygiene, keep vaccinations current, and make your home safe.

Most importantly, learn your dog’s normal habits. When you know how your dog usually eats, sleeps, plays, walks, and behaves, you are more likely to notice meaningful changes early. Consistent care and regular veterinary guidance can help your dog stay healthier, more comfortable, and active throughout every stage of life.
