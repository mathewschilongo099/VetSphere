Pets cannot explain when they feel unwell. Instead, they communicate through changes in behavior, appetite, appearance, movement, and bathroom habits. Learning how to recognize these early warning signs can help you arrange veterinary care before a minor problem becomes a serious emergency.

Some animals, especially cats, are skilled at hiding pain and illness. A cat may appear only slightly quieter than usual even when a significant health problem is developing. Dogs may also disguise discomfort until symptoms become more obvious. The most reliable way to identify illness early is to know your pet’s normal routine and pay attention when something changes.

This guide explains the common signs of a sick animal, what you can safely monitor at home, and when to contact your veterinarian or an emergency animal hospital.

## Know What Is Normal for Your Pet

Early detection begins with establishing a baseline. Observe your pet when they are healthy so you know what is typical for them.

Pay attention to:

- Normal appetite and meal patterns
- Usual water consumption
- Energy level and interest in play
- Sleeping habits
- Breathing pattern while resting
- Normal stool and urine output
- Grooming habits
- Weight and body condition
- Favorite activities and places
- Normal personality and social behavior

A naturally quiet cat may sleep more than an energetic dog, while a senior pet may move more slowly than a young animal. The key concern is not whether your pet matches another animal. It is whether your pet is different from their own normal behavior.

A sudden or gradual change in routine can be an early sign of pain, infection, dental disease, digestive problems, kidney disease, diabetes, or another health condition.

## 1. Watch for Changes in Behavior and Energy

Behavioral changes are often among the first signs of illness in pets. A dog that normally greets you at the door may stop responding. A social cat may begin hiding under furniture or avoid being touched.

Common behavior changes include:

- Sleeping much more than usual
- Losing interest in walks, toys, or play
- Hiding or withdrawing from family members
- Becoming unusually clingy
- Restlessness or difficulty getting comfortable
- New aggression or irritability
- Whining, crying, or unusual vocalization
- Repeatedly changing sleeping locations
- Acting confused or disoriented
- Stopping normal grooming

Lethargy does not always mean your pet is seriously ill, but persistent or severe tiredness deserves attention. It is especially concerning when lethargy occurs with vomiting, diarrhea, poor appetite, weakness, fever, breathing changes, or pain.

Cats often become quiet and withdrawn before they show obvious physical symptoms. Veterinary guidance notes that changes in a cat’s energy, sociability, posture, coat, appetite, litter box use, or breathing may indicate illness. 

## 2. Monitor Appetite and Water Intake

Changes in eating or drinking habits can provide important clues about your pet’s health. A sick animal may eat less, refuse food completely, become unusually selective, or occasionally develop an increased appetite.

Watch for:

- Skipping meals
- Refusing favorite treats
- Chewing on one side of the mouth
- Dropping food
- Difficulty swallowing
- Eating much more than usual
- Drinking significantly more water
- Drinking less than usual
- Sudden weight loss or weight gain

A decreased appetite may be caused by dental pain, nausea, infection, stress, gastrointestinal disease, or organ problems. Increased thirst and urination can occur with conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease, although only a veterinary examination and diagnostic testing can identify the cause.

A cat that has not eaten properly for 24 hours needs immediate veterinary attention because cats can develop serious liver complications when they stop eating.  For dogs, a missed meal may be less urgent if the animal is otherwise bright, comfortable, and behaving normally, but continued appetite loss should still be discussed with a veterinarian.

Do not force-feed a pet that is vomiting, choking, or having difficulty swallowing. Contact your veterinary team for advice instead.

## 3. Check Your Pet’s Weight and Body Condition

Unexplained weight changes often develop gradually, which makes them easy to miss. Run your hands gently over your pet’s ribs, spine, hips, and shoulders during regular handling.

Possible warning signs include:

- Ribs or spine becoming more prominent
- Loss of muscle over the back legs
- A noticeably enlarged abdomen
- Sudden weight gain
- A collar or harness becoming loose
- Reduced muscle tone
- A potbellied appearance in a young animal
- Difficulty maintaining a healthy body condition

Weight loss may accompany dental disease, parasites, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, thyroid disease, cancer, or digestive disorders. Weight gain can also signal reduced mobility, overeating, fluid accumulation, or a metabolic problem.

If possible, weigh your pet regularly. Small changes are easier to identify when you keep a written record rather than relying only on appearance.

## 4. Pay Attention to Vomiting and Diarrhea

Vomiting and diarrhea are common pet health concerns, but they can range from mild stomach upset to a life-threatening emergency.

A single episode of vomiting in an otherwise alert adult pet may sometimes be monitored. However, repeated vomiting, vomiting with weakness, or vomiting accompanied by pain requires veterinary advice.

Contact your veterinarian promptly if your pet has:

- Repeated vomiting
- Blood in vomit
- Black or tarry stool
- Bloody or uncontrollable diarrhea
- Severe abdominal pain
- A swollen or hard abdomen
- Inability to keep water down
- Marked weakness or lethargy
- Signs of dehydration
- Possible toxin or foreign-object exposure

Long-term vomiting or vomiting more than once or twice daily, particularly when combined with blood, abdominal pain, dehydration, fever, weakness, or weight loss, requires a thorough veterinary examination.  

Repeated attempts to vomit without producing anything are especially dangerous in dogs because they may indicate bloat, also called gastric dilatation-volvulus. Restlessness, excessive drooling, dry heaving, and a distended abdomen are emergency symptoms.

Young puppies, kittens, senior pets, and animals with chronic disease can become dehydrated more quickly. Do not wait for symptoms to become severe before calling your veterinarian.

## 5. Look for Changes in Urination and Defecation

Bathroom habits can reveal early signs of urinary, kidney, intestinal, or neurological problems.

Monitor for:

- Straining to urinate or defecate
- Frequent trips to the litter box
- Producing only a few drops of urine
- Blood in urine or stool
- Urinating outside the normal area
- New constipation or diarrhea
- Very small, hard stools
- A sudden increase in urine volume
- A sudden decrease in urine production
- Crying during urination or bowel movements
- Strong or unusual urine odor

Inability to urinate is a medical emergency, particularly in male cats. A cat that repeatedly enters the litter box, strains, cries, or produces little or no urine may have a urinary obstruction. Without rapid treatment, this condition can become life-threatening.

Do not assume that straining is constipation. Urinary obstruction can look similar at first. If you are unsure whether your pet is passing urine, seek veterinary help immediately.

## 6. Recognize Breathing Problems Early

Breathing changes should never be ignored. Watch your pet while resting, rather than immediately after exercise, excitement, or hot weather.

Warning signs include:

- Rapid breathing at rest
- Shallow breathing
- Labored breathing
- Wheezing or noisy breathing
- Persistent coughing
- Extended neck while breathing
- Flared nostrils
- Excessive panting without an obvious reason
- Blue, gray, purple, or very pale gums
- Open-mouth breathing in a cat
- Inability to lie down comfortably

Breathing difficulty is an emergency. Cats should not routinely pant like dogs, and open-mouth breathing in a cat requires immediate veterinary care. Respiratory distress may result from airway disease, heart problems, lung disease, allergic reactions, heatstroke, or trauma.

Keep your pet calm and avoid forcing them to walk if they are struggling to breathe. Call an emergency veterinary clinic while preparing for transport.

## 7. Examine the Eyes, Nose, Mouth, and Gums

A quick visual check can reveal important changes. Healthy eyes are generally clear, moist, and free of significant discharge.

Contact a veterinarian if you notice:

- Redness or swelling
- Squinting
- Cloudiness
- Bulging
- Unequal pupil size
- Excessive tearing
- Yellow, green, or bloody discharge
- A visible third eyelid in a cat
- Pawing at the eye
- Sudden vision problems

Mouth and gum changes may include:

- Bad breath
- Excessive drooling
- Bleeding gums
- Swelling
- Difficulty chewing
- Dropping food
- Pawing at the mouth
- Pale, white, yellow, blue, or gray gums

Gum color can provide clues about circulation and oxygenation. Pale or white gums may occur with anemia, shock, or blood loss, while blue or gray gums can indicate inadequate oxygen. Yellow gums may suggest jaundice or another systemic problem. These changes require prompt veterinary attention.  

Do not put your fingers into the mouth of a conscious, frightened, or choking animal unless a veterinary professional instructs you to do so.

## 8. Inspect the Skin and Coat

Changes in the skin and coat may appear before your pet seems obviously sick. During routine cuddling or brushing, check for:

- New lumps or bumps
- Sores that do not heal
- Hair loss
- Redness or scabs
- Excessive itching
- Fleas or ticks
- Ear odor or discharge
- Greasy or dull fur
- Matted hair
- Sudden dandruff
- Swelling or painful areas

A dull, unkempt coat can indicate that a cat is no longer grooming normally because of pain or illness. Persistent itching may be associated with allergies, parasites, skin infections, or other conditions.

Any new lump should be examined by a veterinarian. You cannot determine whether a growth is harmless simply by feeling it.

## 9. Notice Pain and Mobility Changes

Animals may show pain through subtle changes rather than obvious crying. A pet may avoid stairs, stop jumping, walk stiffly, or become irritable when handled.

Possible pain signs include:

- Limping
- Difficulty standing
- Reluctance to climb stairs
- Trouble jumping onto furniture
- Hunched posture
- Trembling
- Restlessness
- Protecting one area of the body
- Growling or snapping when touched
- Excessive licking of a joint or wound
- Changes in facial expression
- Difficulty getting comfortable

Senior cats with arthritis may stop jumping onto familiar surfaces without showing a noticeable limp. Dogs may move more slowly after rest or avoid activities they once enjoyed.

Never give human pain medication unless your veterinarian specifically prescribes or approves it. Common human medications can be dangerous or fatal to pets.

## 10. Know the Signs of Dehydration

Dehydration can develop after vomiting, diarrhea, fever, heat exposure, kidney disease, or inadequate water intake.

Possible signs include:

- Dry or tacky gums
- Weakness
- Lethargy
- Sunken-looking eyes
- Reduced urination
- Loss of skin elasticity
- Rapid breathing
- Collapse in severe cases

You can gently lift the skin over your pet’s shoulders and release it. In a well-hydrated animal, it should return quickly. Skin that remains tented may indicate dehydration, although this test is less reliable in very young, elderly, thin, or overweight animals. Veterinary sources caution that skin turgor can be affected by age, body condition, and other factors, so it should not replace professional assessment.  

Seek veterinary care if dehydration occurs with vomiting, diarrhea, refusal to drink, weakness, or collapse.

## What to Do if You Notice Early Signs of Illness

If your pet has mild symptoms and is otherwise alert, take the following steps:

1. Write down when the symptoms began.
2. Note changes in appetite, thirst, urination, and stool.
3. Record the number of vomiting or diarrhea episodes.
4. Check for possible access to toxins, medications, plants, garbage, or foreign objects.
5. Take photographs of unusual stool, vomit, swelling, or skin changes.
6. Keep your pet quiet and comfortable.
7. Call your veterinarian for individualized advice.
8. Avoid giving medications or home remedies without professional guidance.

Do not wait for symptoms to become severe if your pet is very young, elderly, pregnant, immunocompromised, or living with a chronic disease.

## When Is a Sick Animal an Emergency?

Seek immediate veterinary care for:

- Difficulty breathing
- Collapse or loss of consciousness
- Seizures
- Severe or uncontrolled bleeding
- Blue, gray, white, or very pale gums
- Inability to urinate
- Repeated dry heaving
- A swollen, hard, painful abdomen
- Severe weakness or inability to stand
- Major trauma
- Sudden paralysis
- Suspected poisoning
- Severe heatstroke
- Repeated vomiting with weakness
- Bloody vomit or stool
- A serious eye injury

If poisoning is possible, remove your pet from the substance and contact a veterinarian or animal poison-control service immediately. Do not induce vomiting unless directed by a veterinary professional. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center can be reached at **(888) 426-4435** in the United States. 

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell if my dog is sick?

Look for changes from your dog’s normal behavior, including reduced energy, appetite loss, vomiting, diarrhea, coughing, limping, unusual thirst, changes in urination, or reluctance to participate in normal activities. Breathing difficulty, collapse, severe pain, and repeated vomiting require emergency care.

### What are the early signs of illness in a cat?

Early signs may include hiding, sleeping more, reduced grooming, appetite or thirst changes, unusual vocalization, weight loss, a hunched posture, vomiting, or changes in litter box use. Cats often hide illness, so even subtle changes should be taken seriously. 

### How long can a pet go without eating?

A pet that refuses food should be monitored closely and discussed with a veterinarian. A cat that has not eaten properly for 24 hours needs immediate veterinary attention. Do not wait if appetite loss occurs with vomiting, lethargy, pain, dehydration, or other symptoms.

### Is one episode of vomiting an emergency?

Not always. A single episode in an otherwise bright, comfortable adult pet may sometimes be monitored. Contact your veterinarian if vomiting repeats, contains blood, is accompanied by pain or weakness, follows possible toxin exposure, or prevents your pet from keeping water down.

### How do I know if my pet is dehydrated?

Dry or tacky gums, weakness, reduced urination, sunken eyes, and skin that remains tented after being gently lifted can suggest dehydration. Because home checks are imperfect, seek veterinary care when dehydration is suspected, especially after vomiting or diarrhea.

### Why is my cat suddenly hiding?

Hiding can occur because of stress, fear, pain, or illness. If it is new, persistent, or accompanied by reduced appetite, vomiting, low energy, breathing changes, or litter box problems, contact your veterinarian.

### When should I take my pet to an emergency vet?

Go to an emergency veterinary clinic for breathing difficulty, collapse, seizures, uncontrolled bleeding, inability to urinate, severe trauma, suspected poisoning, repeated dry heaving, a swollen abdomen, or severe weakness. When uncertain, call your veterinarian or emergency clinic for guidance.

## Conclusion

The best way to spot a sick animal early is to know your pet’s normal behavior and notice small changes. Appetite, thirst, energy, breathing, grooming, weight, mobility, and bathroom habits all provide valuable clues.

Do not dismiss persistent hiding, reduced appetite, unusual thirst, repeated vomiting, changes in urination, or breathing problems as “just getting older.” Early veterinary attention can identify the cause sooner, improve treatment options, and prevent a manageable condition from becoming an emergency. When something feels wrong, trust your observations and contact your veterinarian.
