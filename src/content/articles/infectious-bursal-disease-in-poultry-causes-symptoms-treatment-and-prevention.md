# Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is one of the most important viral diseases affecting young chickens worldwide. It attacks a key immune organ, the bursa of Fabricius, causing immunosuppression, poor performance, and sometimes high mortality, especially in broilers and layers between 3–6 weeks of age.  Because the virus is very hardy in the environment and spreads easily between farms, understanding the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment options, and especially the prevention of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is essential for farmers, students, and poultry professionals.  

This article explains in simple but scientifically accurate language what Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD, also called Gumboro disease) is, how to recognize it, how veterinarians diagnose it, why there is no specific cure, and how to prevent and control it through vaccination, hygiene, and biosecurity. 

---

## What is Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry?

Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is an acute, highly contagious viral disease that mainly affects young chickens, especially from 3 to 6 weeks of age.  It is caused by the Infectious Bursal Disease Virus (IBDV), a double‑stranded RNA virus belonging to the family Birnaviridae, genus Avibirnavirus.  The disease is sometimes called Gumboro disease, infectious bursitis, or infectious avian nephrosis.  

The hallmark of IBD is damage to the bursa of Fabricius, a lymphoid (immune) organ located near the cloaca that is responsible for the development of B lymphocytes (B cells).  When IBDV infects this organ, it destroys immature B cells, causing immunosuppression. This means affected birds become more susceptible to other infections and may respond poorly to vaccinations for other diseases, such as Newcastle disease or infectious bronchitis.  

Clinically, Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry can appear in three broad forms:

1. **Clinical (overt) disease** – obvious illness, depression, diarrhea, and variable mortality, usually in 3–6‑week‑old birds.  
2. **Subclinical disease** – no clear outward signs but severe damage to the bursa in very young chicks, leading to long‑lasting immunosuppression and secondary disease problems later in life.  
3. **Immunosuppressive / variant forms** – milder or atypical presentations that still impair the immune system and cause major economic losses due to poor vaccine response and secondary infections.

## Causes of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry

The primary cause of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is infection with Infectious Bursal Disease Virus (IBDV). The virus exists as several pathotypes (strengths), including classical, variant, and very virulent (vvIBDV) strains.  Very virulent strains can cause mortality rates of 50–60% or higher in some flocks, whereas classical strains usually produce lower mortality but still high morbidity (many birds sick).  

Transmission is mainly **oral**, through ingestion of virus shed in the feces of infected birds and present in contaminated litter, feed, and water.  IBDV is extremely stable in the environment; it can remain infectious in poultry houses for weeks to months, even surviving some common disinfectants and normal cleaning procedures.  This environmental persistence is a major reason why the disease becomes “resident” or enzootic in many poultry areas and houses.  

Key risk factors and “causes” at farm level include:

- **Contaminated premises and equipment** – virus remaining in litter, dust, drinkers, feeders, and building surfaces. 
- **Poor biosecurity** – movement of people, vehicles, crates, and equipment between farms without proper cleaning and disinfection spreads IBDV as fomites. 
- **Inadequate breeder vaccination** – if breeder hens are not properly immunized, their chicks lack sufficient maternal antibody protection and are more vulnerable to early infection. 
- **Stress and co‑infections** – mycotoxins, other immunosuppressive viruses (Marek’s disease, chicken anemia virus, reovirus), and poor management can worsen disease expression and vaccine failure.  

The virus mainly affects chickens, although turkeys, ducks, guinea fowl, and ostriches may be infected subclinically; however, clinically important disease is essentially a problem of chickens.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry

Understanding the main symptoms of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry helps farmers recognize possible outbreaks quickly. Typical clinical signs usually appear suddenly in birds over 3 weeks of age, especially in fast‑growing broilers and young pullets.  

Common clinical signs and symptoms include:

- **Sudden onset of disease in the flock** – many birds become sick within 1–3 days.  
- **Depression and listlessness** – birds are dull, reluctant to move, and often sit hunched. 
- **Ruffled feathers** – especially around the head and body; birds may look “rough.” 
- **Watery diarrhea** – whitish, watery droppings often lead to soiling of feathers around the vent. 
- **Decreased feed and water intake** – sudden drop in consumption is common. 
- **Dehydration and weight loss** – due to reduced feed/water intake and diarrhea. 
- **Vent pecking and picking** – irritated vents and fecal staining can attract pecking from flock mates.  

Mortality typically starts about 24–48 hours after onset of signs, peaks around day 3–4, and then declines, with the whole outbreak usually lasting 5–7 days in a flock.  Mortality varies widely depending on the virulence of the virus and the immune status of the birds; classical strains may cause under 40% mortality, while very virulent strains can cause more than 60% mortality in susceptible flocks.  

Subclinical infection, particularly in chicks less than 3 weeks old, often shows **no obvious external symptoms**, but the virus severely damages the bursa, leading to strong and permanent immunosuppression.  These birds may later show:

- Poor growth and uneven body weights  
- Increased susceptibility to diseases like colibacillosis, gangrenous dermatitis, and inclusion body hepatitis  
- Poor response or failure of vaccinations for other diseases.

## How to Diagnose Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry

Accurate diagnosis of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry requires a combination of flock history, clinical signs, necropsy (post‑mortem) findings, and laboratory tests.  

Key elements of diagnosis:

1. **History and flock data**  
   - Age of birds: most clinical cases occur between 3–6 weeks of age. 
   - Sudden onset, high morbidity, and short course of disease.  
   - History of poor vaccination or high disease pressure in the region.  

2. **Clinical signs**  
   - Depression, ruffled feathers, watery diarrhea, and dehydration in many birds within a short period. 
   - Variable mortality patterns depending on strain and immunity.  

3. **Necropsy (post‑mortem) lesions**  
   On necropsy of affected birds, the most important lesion is in the **bursa of Fabricius**. Typical changes include:  
   - Early stage: enlarged, swollen bursa with edema, hemorrhages, and sometimes a jelly‑like transudate on the surface. 
   - Later stage: bursal atrophy with thin walls, fibrosis, or cystic follicles.  
   Other findings can include hemorrhages in skeletal muscles and sometimes kidney swelling (nephrosis).  

4. **Laboratory confirmation**  
   Veterinarians use several tests to confirm IBDV infection and characterize the strain:  
   - **Histopathology** – microscopic examination of bursa tissue shows destruction of lymphoid cells. 
   - **Virus detection** – molecular methods (RT‑PCR) or virus isolation from bursal tissue to identify IBDV and differentiate strains. 
   - **Serology** – ELISA or agar gel precipitation (AGP) to detect antibodies and monitor vaccination status or exposure.  

A definitive diagnosis is important not only to distinguish IBD from other causes of diarrhea and mortality, but also to design an appropriate vaccination and control program for the farm based on the circulating strains.

## Treatment of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry

For farmers asking **how to treat Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry**, it is important to understand that **there is no specific antiviral treatment** that cures IBD once birds are infected.  Because the virus targets lymphoid tissue and causes structural damage to the bursa, therapy cannot reverse the main lesions.

However, supportive care can reduce losses and improve recovery:

1. **Supportive management**  
   - Ensure easy access to clean, cool water; use electrolytes and vitamins (especially vitamins A, D, E and B‑complex) in drinking water to combat dehydration and stress. 
   - Maintain comfortable temperature and ventilation to reduce additional stress.  
   - Remove severely affected or dead birds promptly to limit contamination and cannibalism.  

2. **Control of secondary infections**  
   - Because IBD causes immunosuppression, birds are more likely to develop bacterial infections like colibacillosis. Veterinarians may prescribe appropriate antibiotics to control secondary bacterial diseases, but antibiotics do not act against the virus itself.  

3. **Flock‑level decisions**  
   - In severe outbreaks with high mortality and heavily contaminated houses, depopulation followed by thorough cleaning and disinfection may be recommended to break the cycle of infection.  

In summary, **treatment of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is mainly supportive**, aimed at minimizing mortality and complications. Long‑term control depends on prevention, especially vaccination and strict biosecurity, rather than curative treatment.

## Prevention and Control of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry

Because there is no curative therapy, **prevention of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry** is critical. Effective control programs combine **vaccination**, **biosecurity**, and **good management** at breeder and broiler levels. 

### 1. Vaccination strategies

Vaccination is the main tool for controlling IBD in modern poultry production.  There are two major levels:

- **Breeder vaccination**  
  Breeder hens are vaccinated with live and/or inactivated IBD vaccines so that they develop strong antibodies, which they then pass to their chicks as maternal antibodies in the egg yolk.  These maternal antibodies protect chicks during the early days and weeks of life until they can be actively vaccinated themselves.

- **Broiler / pullet vaccination**  
  Live attenuated or vector vaccines are used in chicks as maternal antibodies decline, usually between 1–21 days of age depending on the program.  Vaccines can be administered via drinking water, eye drops, or injection. The exact timing must be adjusted according to measured maternal antibody levels in each flock to avoid interference with vaccine “take.”  

Vaccines vary in their “strength” (mild, intermediate, intermediate‑plus, or “hot”). Less attenuated (stronger) vaccines can protect better in high‑challenge areas but may themselves damage the bursa and cause immunosuppression if used incorrectly.  Therefore, designing a vaccination program should be done in consultation with a poultry veterinarian or company specialist.

### 2. Biosecurity and farm hygiene

Since IBDV is highly resistant and tends to persist as a “resident” virus in poultry houses, strict biosecurity is essential.  

Key points include:

- **All‑in/all‑out management** – avoid mixing age groups; completely empty, clean, and disinfect houses between flocks.  
- **Thorough cleaning and disinfection** – remove all litter and organic material, wash all surfaces, use effective disinfectants known to work against IBDV, and allow adequate contact time and drying. 
- **Control of traffic and equipment** – restrict visitors, use farm‑specific boots and clothing, disinfect vehicles and equipment entering and leaving the farm. 
- **Litter management** – reuse of built‑up litter can increase virus pressure; if used, it must be managed carefully with good disinfection and downtime. 

### 3. Good management and health programs

- **Source chicks from reputable hatcheries** with documented breeder vaccination and monitoring of maternal antibody levels. 
- **Minimize stress** from overcrowding, poor ventilation, temperature extremes, and poor nutrition, as stress weakens immunity and worsens disease.  
- **Control other immunosuppressive diseases** such as Marek’s disease and chicken anemia virus, as well as mycotoxins in feed.  

An effective IBD control program blends all these measures: strong breeder immunity, proper chick vaccination schedules, tight biosecurity, and high management standards.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry

### Q: What age of chickens is most at risk for Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry?  
**A:** Clinical Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is most common in chickens between 3 and 6 weeks of age, when the bursa of Fabricius is largest and full of maturing B cells, the main target of the virus.  Chicks infected before 3 weeks often show no obvious signs but may become severely immunosuppressed. 

### Q: Is Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry contagious to humans or other animals?  
**A:** IBD is **not** considered a human health risk and has no public health significance.  The virus mainly causes disease in chickens, although other poultry species can be infected subclinically. It does not infect humans in a way that causes illness.

### Q: How long can the IBD virus survive in a poultry house?  
**A:** IBDV is very stable and can remain infectious in the environment for many weeks or even months, especially in litter, dust, and organic matter.  It is resistant to many common disinfectants, making eradication difficult without thorough cleaning and strict biosecurity. 

### Q: Can vaccination completely prevent Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry?  
**A:** Vaccination greatly reduces the risk of clinical disease and mortality and helps control virus circulation, but it may not completely prevent infection, especially in high‑challenge areas.  Well‑designed breeder and broiler vaccination programs, combined with strong biosecurity, are needed to keep virus pressure low and protect flock performance. 

### Q: How can I tell the difference between IBD and other poultry diseases with diarrhea and mortality?  
**A:** Many diseases can cause diarrhea and mortality in chicken flocks. IBD is suspected when young birds (3–6 weeks) show sudden, high morbidity, watery droppings, depression, and characteristic bursal lesions on necropsy.  Laboratory tests such as histopathology, virus detection (PCR), and serology are required for definitive diagnosis and to distinguish IBD from other conditions like coccidiosis, Newcastle disease, or nephropathogenic infectious bronchitis.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

You should contact a poultry veterinarian promptly if you notice any of the following in your flock:

- Sudden onset of depression, ruffled feathers, and watery diarrhea in many birds within a few days, especially in 3–6‑week‑old chickens.  
- Rapid drop in feed and water consumption, followed by unexpected mortality over several days. 
- Recurring problems of poor growth, uneven body weights, and frequent secondary bacterial diseases despite routine vaccinations, which may indicate subclinical IBD and immunosuppression.  

A veterinarian can perform or arrange:

- Proper necropsies to examine the bursa of Fabricius and other organs.  
- Laboratory tests to confirm IBDV infection and characterize the strain.  
- Review of your vaccination schedule, breeder source, and biosecurity measures to design a tailored control program.  

Early veterinary involvement not only helps limit losses in the current flock but also reduces long‑term impacts on future flocks by improving prevention strategies.

## Conclusion

Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry is a major viral threat to young chickens worldwide, causing immunosuppression, poor performance, and sometimes very high mortality.  The disease is caused by Infectious Bursal Disease Virus, which targets the bursa of Fabricius and destroys B lymphocytes, leaving birds vulnerable to many other infections and vaccine failures.  

There is no specific antiviral treatment for IBD; management focuses on supportive care during outbreaks and, more importantly, on **prevention and control**. Effective prevention of Infectious Bursal Disease in Poultry depends on:

- Strong breeder vaccination to provide maternal antibodies to chicks.  
- Correctly timed vaccination of broilers and pullets using suitable vaccine strains.  
- Rigorous farm biosecurity, cleaning, and disinfection to reduce environmental virus pressure.  
- Good overall management to minimize stress and control other immunosuppressive factors.  

By understanding the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, and control measures described in this article, farmers, students, and poultry professionals can work with veterinarians to design robust IBD prevention programs, safeguard bird health, and protect the economic viability of poultry operations.
