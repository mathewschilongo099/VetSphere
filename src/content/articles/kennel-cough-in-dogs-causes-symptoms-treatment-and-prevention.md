# Kennel Cough in Dogs: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Kennel Cough in Dogs is one of the most common infectious diseases seen in veterinary practice, especially in dogs that mix with other dogs in kennels, shelters, doggy day care, grooming salons, and dog parks.  It is usually mild, but the cough can sound alarming and, in vulnerable animals, it can progress to serious pneumonia.  Understanding the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of Kennel Cough in Dogs helps pet owners act quickly and protect both their own dog and others.

This condition is now more correctly called canine infectious respiratory disease complex (CIRDC) or canine infectious respiratory disease (CIRD), because many different bacteria and viruses can be involved.  However, most owners and many professionals still use the familiar term “kennel cough.” Knowing how this disease spreads and what to watch for is especially important if your dog ever boards, attends group training, or visits crowded dog parks.

---

## What is Kennel Cough in Dogs?

Kennel Cough in Dogs is a highly contagious infection of the upper airways (trachea and bronchi) that causes a characteristic dry, hacking cough.  It affects dogs of all ages and breeds, although young puppies, elderly dogs, and animals with weak immune systems are at greater risk of complications.  

Veterinarians now understand Kennel Cough in Dogs as part of a broader syndrome, canine infectious respiratory disease complex (CIRDC). Multiple organisms can be involved together, including bacteria such as Bordetella bronchiseptica and viruses such as canine parainfluenza virus, canine adenovirus type 2, canine influenza virus, and others.  Because several germs can act at once, the severity of disease can range from a mild cough to life‑threatening pneumonia.

Most cases of Kennel Cough in Dogs are self‑limiting, meaning many otherwise healthy dogs recover over 1–3 weeks with rest and supportive care.  However, some dogs need veterinary treatment, especially if they are very young, very old, immunocompromised, or develop fever, lethargy, difficulty breathing, or loss of appetite.  Early recognition and isolation are also essential to prevent spread to other dogs.

## Causes of Kennel Cough in Dogs

When discussing the causes of Kennel Cough in Dogs, it is important to remember that this is usually a mixed infection. One of the most common bacterial causes is Bordetella bronchiseptica, which is why many people simply call the disease “Bordetella.”  Bordetella damages the lining of the respiratory tract, making it easier for other germs to invade and causing inflammation of the trachea and bronchi.  

Several viruses are frequently involved in Kennel Cough in Dogs. These include canine parainfluenza virus, canine adenovirus type 2, canine distemper virus, canine influenza virus, canine herpesvirus, canine respiratory coronavirus, canine pneumovirus, and others.  These viruses can either cause respiratory disease on their own or set up the airways for a secondary infection with Bordetella or other bacteria.  

Dogs typically get Kennel Cough by inhaling aerosolized droplets or direct contact with respiratory secretions from infected dogs, for example when sniffing, playing, coughing, or sharing water bowls and toys.  Crowded conditions, poor ventilation, high humidity, and stress are major risk factors, which is why kennels, shelters, dog shows, and daycare centers are common sources.  Environmental persistence of some agents and inadequate cleaning between dogs can also contribute to outbreaks.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Kennel Cough in Dogs

The most typical symptom of Kennel Cough in Dogs is a sudden onset of a loud, dry, hacking cough. Many owners describe it as a “goose‑honking” sound or as if the dog has something stuck in its throat.  The cough is often triggered by excitement, pulling on the collar, or pressure on the trachea, and may end with a retch or gag, sometimes bringing up small amounts of foam or mucus.  

Apart from the cough, many dogs with uncomplicated Kennel Cough are bright, alert, and still eating and drinking normally. They may show mild signs such as a runny nose, watery eyes, or a small temperature rise, but they usually do not appear very ill.  In these straightforward cases, symptoms of Kennel Cough in Dogs tend to improve over one to two weeks, although the cough can occasionally linger longer.  

However, more severe disease can occur, especially in puppies, older dogs, brachycephalic (short‑nosed) breeds, and animals with other health problems. Warning signs include lethargy, reduced appetite, high fever, labored or rapid breathing, thick green‑yellow nasal discharge, and a productive (wet) cough that suggests lower respiratory involvement or pneumonia.  These more serious symptoms of Kennel Cough in Dogs need urgent veterinary attention.

## How to Diagnose Kennel Cough in Dogs

In most cases, veterinarians diagnose Kennel Cough in Dogs based on history and physical examination. A typical history includes recent exposure to other dogs (such as boarding, daycare, or dog parks) followed by the sudden development of a characteristic honking cough.  On examination, vets may be able to trigger the cough by gently pressing on the trachea, and they will listen carefully to the lungs and heart.

Because many other diseases can cause coughing, such as heart disease, collapsing trachea, pneumonia, canine influenza, and even foreign bodies, your vet may recommend additional tests in some cases.  Diagnostic options can include chest X‑rays, blood tests, and in selected cases, PCR testing or culture to identify specific viruses or bacteria involved in the canine infectious respiratory disease complex.  

In mild, straightforward cases of Kennel Cough in Dogs in otherwise healthy animals, extensive testing is often not needed. The vet may make a presumptive diagnosis and treat based on clinical signs. However, in severe, persistent, or atypical cases—especially if there is suspicion of pneumonia, distemper, influenza, or another serious disease—more detailed diagnostic work‑up is appropriate to guide treatment and prognosis.

## Treatment of Kennel Cough in Dogs

Understanding how to treat Kennel Cough in Dogs starts with recognizing that many cases are mild and self‑limiting. For otherwise healthy dogs with an uncomplicated dry cough and no signs of systemic illness, vets often recommend rest, stress reduction, and monitoring at home.  Avoiding excitement and using a harness instead of a collar can help reduce coughing spells while the respiratory tract heals.

When medication is needed, treatment of Kennel Cough in Dogs may include cough suppressants (antitussives) to reduce the frequency and severity of coughing, provided the cough is non‑productive and there is no pneumonia.  Anti‑inflammatory drugs may also be used to decrease airway irritation and discomfort, under veterinary supervision.  It is important never to give human cough or cold medicines to dogs unless specifically directed by a veterinarian.

Antibiotics are sometimes prescribed, especially when Bordetella bronchiseptica or secondary bacterial infection is suspected, or when the dog is systemically ill (fever, lethargy, thick nasal discharge, reduced appetite). Common choices include doxycycline and other antibiotics effective against respiratory bacteria.  In severe cases, especially if there is pneumonia or marked breathing difficulty, dogs may need hospitalization, oxygen therapy, nebulization, and intensive care.  Treatment length varies, but most dogs improve significantly within 7–10 days when treated appropriately.  

Supportive care at home is also very important. Keeping the dog in a warm, well‑ventilated, low‑dust environment, offering soft palatable food, and ensuring easy access to fresh water will support recovery. Using a harness instead of a neck collar, avoiding smoke, aerosols, and heavy perfumes, and strictly resting the dog (no running or rough play) help to reduce irritation of the airways while healing occurs.  Dogs should be kept away from other dogs until your veterinarian confirms they are no longer contagious.

## Prevention and Control of Kennel Cough in Dogs

Effective prevention of Kennel Cough in Dogs relies on a combination of vaccination, good management, and sensible hygiene. Vaccines are available against several of the key organisms that cause Kennel Cough, especially Bordetella bronchiseptica and canine parainfluenza virus, often combined with other core vaccines such as distemper and adenovirus type 2.  The “kennel cough vaccine” or Bordetella vaccine can be given as an injection, intranasal drops, or oral dose, depending on the product.  

The Bordetella vaccine is considered a non‑core vaccine, but it is strongly recommended for dogs that are regularly exposed to other dogs in kennels, daycare, grooming salons, training classes, shelters, or dog shows.  Many facilities require proof of current vaccination before accepting a dog.  While vaccination does not prevent every case of Kennel Cough in Dogs, it can significantly reduce the severity and duration of illness and helps limit spread within dog populations.  

Beyond vaccination, prevention and control of Kennel Cough in Dogs includes minimizing exposure to crowded, poorly ventilated indoor spaces and avoiding contact with coughing dogs. Good kennel management—adequate ventilation, regular disinfection, separation of sick animals, and appropriate vaccination policies—greatly reduces outbreaks.  At home, dogs with Kennel Cough should be isolated from other dogs, and bowls, bedding, and surfaces should be cleaned and disinfected; diluted household bleach (1:32) is effective against Bordetella in the environment.  Dogs that have recovered can continue to shed bacteria for weeks, so following veterinary advice on isolation periods is important.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Kennel Cough in Dogs

### Q: Is Kennel Cough in Dogs contagious to other dogs?

**A:** Yes. Kennel Cough in Dogs is highly contagious and spreads easily through droplets from coughing and sneezing, as well as through direct contact with contaminated bowls, toys, and surfaces.  This is why it often occurs where many dogs are kept together, such as kennels, shelters, dog shows, and daycare centers.  Infected dogs should be kept away from other dogs until your veterinarian advises that they are no longer a risk.

### Q: Can humans catch Kennel Cough from dogs?

**A:** Kennel Cough in Dogs is generally considered a disease of dogs, and typical strains of Bordetella bronchiseptica and the common canine respiratory viruses do not usually cause illness in healthy people.  Rare infections have been reported in individuals with severely weakened immune systems, so immunocompromised owners should take extra precautions and may prefer injectable vaccines for their dogs and reduced exposure to large dog groups.  If you are immunocompromised, discuss risk with your physician and veterinarian.

### Q: How long does Kennel Cough in Dogs last?

**A:** In uncomplicated cases, most dogs begin to improve within a few days and recover fully within 7–14 days, although the cough can sometimes linger for several weeks.  Dogs with more severe infections, especially those that develop pneumonia or have underlying health problems, may need longer treatment and recovery time.  Even after symptoms resolve, some dogs can continue to shed bacteria for weeks, so isolation guidelines from your vet should be followed. 

### Q: Does my dog need the Bordetella vaccine?

**A:** The Bordetella vaccine is strongly recommended for dogs that regularly visit kennels, doggy daycare, groomers, training classes, shelters, or dog parks, as these environments increase the risk of exposure.  Many boarding and daycare facilities require proof of a current vaccine. For mostly home‑based dogs with little or no contact with other dogs, your veterinarian will evaluate the individual risk and may or may not recommend routine vaccination.  Vaccination schedules vary, but many facilities prefer boosters every 6–12 months. 

### Q: How can I tell if my dog’s cough is Kennel Cough or something more serious?

**A:** Kennel Cough in Dogs typically causes a dry, honking cough in an otherwise bright, active dog with a normal or only slightly reduced appetite.  More serious problems are suggested by signs such as high fever, lethargy, trouble breathing, blue‑tinged gums, refusal to eat, collapse, or a wet, productive cough with thick nasal discharge.  Because many conditions can cause coughing, including heart disease and pneumonia, any persistent or severe cough should be evaluated by a veterinarian.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

You should contact a veterinarian as soon as you suspect Kennel Cough in Dogs, especially if your dog has recently been in a high‑risk environment such as a kennel, shelter, daycare center, or dog park and has developed a new cough.  Early advice helps you manage the dog properly at home, determine whether a clinic visit is needed, and prevent exposing other dogs. In mild cases, your vet may recommend simple home care and monitoring; in others, examination and treatment may be necessary.

Immediate veterinary attention is essential if your dog shows any warning signs: difficulty breathing, rapid or labored respiration, persistent severe coughing, blue or pale gums, high fever, marked lethargy, refusal to eat, vomiting, or collapse.  These signs can indicate pneumonia or other serious complications of Kennel Cough in Dogs that may require antibiotics, oxygen therapy, or hospitalization.  Puppies, elderly dogs, short‑nosed breeds, pregnant bitches, and dogs with chronic heart or lung disease are at particular risk and should be seen promptly.

Even if your dog’s symptoms seem mild, you should call your veterinarian if the cough lasts longer than two weeks, if it worsens rather than improves, or if new signs such as nasal discharge, eye discharge, or lethargy appear.  Your vet may decide to perform diagnostic tests such as chest X‑rays or laboratory work to rule out other diseases and refine treatment of Kennel Cough in Dogs.  Also remember to ask your vet when it is safe for your dog to return to social activities, as dogs can remain contagious after the cough resolves.

## Conclusion

Kennel Cough in Dogs, or canine infectious respiratory disease complex, is a common and highly contagious upper respiratory infection caused by a mixture of bacteria and viruses, most notably Bordetella bronchiseptica and various respiratory viruses.  While many cases are mild and self‑limiting, the disease can be distressing for both dogs and owners, and may become serious in vulnerable animals.  Recognizing the characteristic signs, especially the loud, honking cough after exposure to other dogs, allows for early veterinary advice and timely management.

Effective management includes accurate diagnosis, appropriate treatment of Kennel Cough in Dogs—ranging from rest and cough suppressants to antibiotics and intensive care in severe cases—and strict isolation to protect other dogs.  Prevention of Kennel Cough in Dogs centers on vaccination against key pathogens such as Bordetella and parainfluenza, good ventilation and hygiene in kennels and daycare facilities, and sensible avoidance of crowded dog environments when outbreaks occur.  

For farmers, students, and pet owners, understanding the causes of Kennel Cough in Dogs, the typical symptoms of Kennel Cough in Dogs, and the options for treatment and prevention helps protect both individual pets and the wider dog community. With informed care and appropriate vaccination, most dogs can avoid serious illness and continue to enjoy safe social interaction with other dogs.
