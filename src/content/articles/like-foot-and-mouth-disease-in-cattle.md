## Introduction

Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) is one of the most important and feared diseases of cattle and other cloven‑hoofed animals worldwide. It is caused by a highly contagious virus that spreads very quickly within and between herds, leading to large economic losses due to reduced milk production, weight loss, infertility, trade restrictions, and the cost of control measures. Although adult cattle often survive, the pain, debility, and long recovery make it a major welfare concern and a serious threat to livestock farming.  

The disease is characterized by fever and the sudden appearance of blisters (vesicles) and sores in the mouth, on the tongue, lips and dental pad, around the feet (especially at the coronary band and between the claws), and on the teats of lactating cows. These lesions are extremely painful and cause drooling, reluctance to eat or move, and a marked drop in milk and body condition. Because of this typical pattern, FMD is sometimes called a “vesicular disease.”  

FMD is also a major concern at national and international level because it spreads across regions and countries through infected animals, animal products, contaminated equipment, and even wind‑borne virus over long distances. Many countries have strict control programs and surveillance because a single outbreak can shut down exports and cost the livestock sector billions. This makes early recognition, rapid reporting, and solid farm biosecurity essential skills for farmers, students, and animal health workers.

## Causes

Foot and Mouth Disease is caused by the Foot‑and‑Mouth Disease Virus (FMDV), a member of the genus Aphthovirus in the Picornaviridae family. It is one of the smallest known viruses but is highly infectious and very resilient in the environment, especially in cool, moist conditions, manure, and organic material. There are seven recognized serotypes (O, A, C, SAT1, SAT2, SAT3, Asia 1) and many subtypes. Infection with one serotype generally does not protect against the others, which complicates vaccination and control programs.  

The virus mainly affects cloven‑hoofed animals such as cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats, and pigs, as well as some wild species like deer and wild boar. Cattle are especially important because they usually show “textbook” signs that make them good sentinel animals, while pigs tend to shed very large amounts of virus and act as amplifiers. Sheep and goats may show milder or less obvious signs, allowing the virus to move silently between flocks and farms.  

FMD spreads by direct and indirect contact. Direct spread occurs when healthy animals inhale or ingest virus from droplets in the air, saliva, milk, semen, or fluid from blisters of infected animals. Indirect spread happens through contaminated equipment, vehicles, clothing, boots, feed, water, and animal products such as meat and milk. The virus can also travel by air over several kilometers under suitable weather conditions. Because animals can shed virus before they show symptoms, and because some may carry virus in the throat after recovery, movement control and strict biosecurity are critical tools in preventing outbreaks.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms

The incubation period (time from infection to visible illness) in cattle is usually 2–14 days. Often the first sign is a sudden fever around 40 °C (104 °F), with the animal becoming dull and off‑feed. Shortly after, affected cattle start to drool excessively, with ropy, foamy saliva hanging from the mouth, and may smack their lips or grind their teeth due to pain. Milk production drops sharply, and affected cows may separate from the herd and lie down more often.  

As the disease progresses, blisters appear on the tongue, dental pad, gums, lips, and sometimes the nostrils. These blisters quickly rupture, leaving raw, red, and painful erosions and ulcers. The animal has difficulty chewing and swallowing, may drop feed from the mouth, and may lose a lot of weight in a short time. Offensive odor from the mouth may be noted on close examination. In adult cattle, death is uncommon, but young calves can die from heart damage (“tiger heart” lesions on post‑mortem) or severe weakness and dehydration.  

Lesions on the feet are equally important. Blisters form at the coronary band (where hair meets hoof), in the interdigital space (between the claws), and on the bulbs of the heel. When they burst, the exposed tissues are extremely painful, leading to lameness, reluctance to walk, and animals spending long periods lying down. Severely lame cattle may refuse to move to feed and water. Teat lesions in lactating cows cause kicking during milking, mastitis, and further loss of milk. Bulls may develop vesicles on the scrotum, and both males and females can suffer temporary fertility problems. Recovery of the mouth and feet lesions may take 1–2 weeks or more, but full restoration of production can take much longer.

## Diagnosis

On farms, the first step in diagnosing FMD is careful observation of clinical signs and patterns within the herd. The combination of acute fever, excessive salivation, and typical blisters or erosions in the mouth and on the feet, especially when several animals are affected at once, should immediately raise suspicion of FMD. Farmers should closely check the tongue, dental pad, gums, lips, coronary band, and spaces between the claws in any animal showing lameness or drooling. Noting when signs first appeared and how quickly they spread through the group is helpful information for veterinarians and authorities.  

However, FMD cannot be reliably distinguished from other vesicular diseases (such as vesicular stomatitis or swine vesicular disease) based on clinical signs alone. For this reason, laboratory confirmation is essential and is considered urgent in any suspected case. Veterinarians collect samples such as pieces of fresh vesicle epithelium, fluid from unruptured blisters, swabs from lesions, whole blood, and serum. These samples must be handled with care using protective clothing and sent only to approved FMD reference laboratories. Tests used include virus isolation, detection of viral antigen or nucleic acid, and measurement of specific antibodies.  

Because of the serious trade and control implications, FMD is usually a notifiable disease. That means that anyone who suspects FMD (farmer, veterinarian, or worker) is legally required to report it to animal health authorities. Once suspicion is raised, movement restrictions, on‑farm biosecurity measures, and tracing of animal movements are typically started even before laboratory results are available, in order to limit possible spread. Rapid recognition, prompt reporting, and quick laboratory confirmation are the cornerstones of successful FMD control.

## Treatment

There is no specific antiviral cure for Foot and Mouth Disease. Because it is caused by a virus, treatment is mainly supportive and aimed at reducing pain, preventing secondary bacterial infections, and keeping the animals comfortable until they recover. Infected animals are usually isolated from the healthy herd in a clean, dry, and well‑bedded area to reduce further spread and to allow easier monitoring and care. Stress should be minimized by gentle handling and avoiding unnecessary movement.  

Pain relief and fever control are important for animal welfare and appetite. Non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as flunixin or meloxicam (used under veterinary guidance) can help reduce pain, inflammation, and high body temperature. Mouth and foot lesions should be kept as clean as possible; washing with mild antiseptic solutions and keeping floors dry helps to reduce infection and promote healing. If flies are a problem, appropriate fly control measures should be used to prevent maggot infestation of open sores.  

Because damaged tissues are open to bacteria, antibiotics may be prescribed to prevent or treat secondary infections such as foot rot or mastitis. Soft, palatable feeds (chopped forage, mash, good quality hay) and easy access to clean, fresh water encourage animals to keep eating and drinking. In hot climates or in animals showing signs of dehydration, oral electrolytes can be added to drinking water. Severely affected or very weak animals may require more intensive nursing care. Decisions about culling, slaughter, or salvage may be guided by national policy, severity of lesions, and economic considerations.

## Prevention and Control

Preventing FMD from entering a farm is much better than trying to control it after introduction. Strong biosecurity is the first line of defense. Farmers should control movements of animals, people, vehicles, and equipment onto the farm. New or returning animals should be quarantined and observed for at least two weeks before mixing with the resident herd. Boots, clothing, and equipment should be cleaned and disinfected between farms and between groups of animals, especially where disease is known to be present in the region. Feed and bedding should be sourced from trusted suppliers, and visitors’ access to animal areas should be restricted.  

Vaccination is a key tool in countries where FMD is endemic or where there is high risk of introduction. Available vaccines are inactivated (killed) virus preparations and must be matched as closely as possible to the circulating serotypes. Because there is little cross‑protection between serotypes and immunity from vaccination may last only 4–6 months, regular booster doses are needed, often every 6–12 months depending on the product and regional guidelines. Vaccination greatly reduces clinical disease and virus shedding, protecting high‑value dairy and breeding animals, but it does not always completely prevent infection or carrier states in cattle. Accurate records of vaccinations, including batch and date, are essential for herd health management.  

At national level, control strategies during an outbreak may include immediate movement restrictions, establishment of protection and surveillance zones (for example, 3 km and 10 km radii around the outbreak), culling of infected and in‑contact animals (“stamping out”), and sometimes ring vaccination around affected farms. Contaminated premises, vehicles, and equipment are thoroughly cleaned and disinfected. Countries free from FMD generally avoid routine vaccination to maintain a clear‑status, relying on rapid stamping out in case of introduction, whereas endemic countries use ongoing vaccination plus improved biosecurity programmes to reduce the burden of disease. Successful control depends on close cooperation among farmers, veterinarians, laboratories, and government authorities.

## Conclusion

Foot and Mouth Disease remains one of the most significant threats to cattle health and livestock production worldwide. Although it is usually not highly fatal in adult cattle, the severe pain, loss of milk and body weight, reproductive problems, and trade disruptions make it an economically devastating disease. Its ability to spread rapidly, even before animals look sick, means that no farm can afford to be complacent.  

For farmers, students, and animal owners, a clear understanding of the causes, clinical signs, diagnosis, and supportive treatment of FMD is vital. Rapid recognition of drooling, mouth ulcers, lameness, and sudden herd‑wide production drops, followed by immediate veterinary consultation and notification of authorities, gives the best chance of limiting damage. While there is no specific cure, good nursing care, pain relief, and protection against secondary infections can help affected cattle recover more quickly and with fewer complications.  

In the long term, strong farm biosecurity, appropriate vaccination where recommended, and adherence to regional and national control measures offer the most effective protection. By combining these approaches and maintaining good communication between farmers and veterinary professionals, it is possible to reduce the impact of Foot and Mouth Disease and safeguard both animal welfare and the sustainability of the livestock industry.
