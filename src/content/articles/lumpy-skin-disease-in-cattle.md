Lumpy skin disease in cattle is a serious viral infection that can affect animal welfare, milk production, fertility, and farm profitability. The disease is best known for the firm, painful lumps that develop across an animal’s skin, but it can also cause fever, swollen lymph nodes, lameness, pneumonia, mastitis, abortion, and long recovery periods.

The good news is that lumpy skin disease is not a human disease. People do not become infected through contact with affected cattle or by consuming milk or meat from cattle infected with lumpy skin disease. However, the disease can create major economic problems for farmers and may spread quickly through a herd, especially where biting insects are abundant.  

Early recognition, veterinary diagnosis, insect control, vaccination, and strict farm biosecurity are the most important tools for managing lumpy skin disease in cattle.

## What Is Lumpy Skin Disease in Cattle?

Lumpy skin disease, often called LSD, is caused by lumpy skin disease virus, or LSDV. The virus belongs to the *Capripoxvirus* genus within the *Poxviridae* family. It primarily affects cattle and, in some situations, water buffalo and certain wild ruminants.  

The disease can range from mild or subclinical infection to severe illness. Some infected cattle may show only fever and enlarged lymph nodes, while others develop numerous skin nodules, respiratory complications, severe weight loss, and permanent hide damage.

Lumpy skin disease has historically been associated with Africa, but it has spread to parts of the Middle East, Asia, the Balkans, and Europe. Because it can cross borders through animal movement and insect transmission, it is considered an important transboundary livestock disease.  

Although death rates are often low, the disease can still cause serious losses through:

- Reduced milk production
- Weight loss and poor growth
- Abortion and delayed breeding
- Temporary or permanent infertility in bulls
- Mastitis and pneumonia
- Damaged hides and lower carcass value
- Treatment and vaccination costs
- Animal movement and trade restrictions
- Death in severe cases

## How Does Lumpy Skin Disease Spread?

Biting insects are the main route of lumpy skin disease transmission. Mosquitoes, stable flies, horseflies, biting flies, and some other arthropods can mechanically carry the virus from an infected animal to a susceptible cow or bull. The virus does not need to multiply inside the insect for transmission to occur.  

Transmission risk is usually higher during warm, humid periods when insect populations increase. Farms located near standing water, wetlands, dense vegetation, or poorly drained areas may face increased exposure to biting insects.

The virus may also spread through:

- Contaminated needles and injection equipment
- Shared grooming tools
- Contaminated vehicles and handling equipment
- Saliva and respiratory secretions
- Skin lesions, scabs, and necrotic tissue
- Semen from infected bulls
- Contaminated hides and animal materials
- Movement of infected or incubating cattle

Direct contact between cattle is generally less efficient than insect transmission, but it may still contribute to spread in heavily affected herds. The virus can remain viable for extended periods in dried skin crusts, necrotic nodules, and contaminated dark or sheltered environments.  

Healthy-looking cattle may also present a risk because not every infected animal develops obvious skin nodules. For this reason, moving apparently healthy cattle from an affected area can introduce the disease into a new herd.

## Lumpy Skin Disease Incubation Period

The incubation period is commonly reported as approximately two to five weeks, although signs may appear sooner in experimentally infected cattle. During this time, an infected animal may look normal while the virus is developing.  

This makes quarantine important whenever you:

- Purchase new cattle
- Bring cattle home from a show or market
- Return animals from communal grazing
- Introduce breeding bulls
- Transport cattle from a region with reported cases

Keep new or returning animals separate from the main herd for the period recommended by your veterinarian or animal health authority. Monitor their temperature, appetite, milk production, and skin carefully.

## Common Symptoms of Lumpy Skin Disease in Cattle

The first signs may be general and easy to confuse with other cattle diseases. Watch for a sudden fever, depression, reduced appetite, watery eyes, nasal discharge, and reluctance to move.

The characteristic skin lumps may appear shortly after the fever begins. They are usually firm, round, raised, painful nodules involving the full thickness of the skin. Nodules may range from small, localized lesions to widespread lumps covering much of the body.  

Common clinical signs include:

- Fever that may exceed 40°C
- Firm skin nodules on the head, neck, back, limbs, udder, genitalia, or perineum
- Enlarged superficial lymph nodes
- Watery eyes and conjunctivitis
- Nasal discharge
- Excessive salivation
- Reduced appetite
- Depression and weakness
- Reduced milk production
- Swelling of the legs, brisket, udder, or genital region
- Lameness and reluctance to walk
- Weight loss and emaciation
- Coughing or difficulty breathing

The nodules may become necrotic in the centre. In some cases, the dead tissue forms hard plugs known as “sitfasts.” These plugs may later separate from the surrounding skin, leaving deep ulcers that heal slowly and often produce permanent scars. 

### Severe complications

Lumpy skin disease can affect more than the skin. Lesions may develop in the mouth, throat, respiratory tract, digestive tract, udder, and reproductive organs.

Possible complications include:

- Secondary bacterial infection of skin wounds
- Fly strike and maggot infestation
- Mastitis
- Pneumonia
- Corneal ulcers or impaired vision
- Severe dehydration
- Difficulty eating because of mouth lesions
- Abortion or premature birth
- Temporary or permanent infertility in bulls
- Reduced fertility and delayed return to oestrus in cows
- Permanent damage to teats, joints, or limbs

Young calves and lactating cows may develop more severe signs. Certain *Bos taurus* breeds, including some Channel Island breeds, may be more susceptible than zebu-type cattle. 

## What Does Lumpy Skin Disease Look Like?

The skin nodules are usually the most recognizable sign. They are often:

- Firm rather than soft
- Clearly separated from normal skin
- Raised and painful
- Covered by short, erect hair at first
- Surrounded by a narrow area of inflammation or bleeding
- Located on sparsely haired areas such as the head, neck, udder, legs, and genitalia

However, not every lump on a cow is caused by lumpy skin disease. Other conditions can produce skin bumps, scabs, or nodules, including insect bites, ringworm, dermatophilosis, bovine herpes mammillitis, abscesses, mange, and other viral or bacterial infections.

Do not rely on appearance alone. A veterinarian should examine the animal and arrange laboratory testing.

## How Is Lumpy Skin Disease Diagnosed?

A veterinarian may suspect LSD based on the animal’s fever, enlarged lymph nodes, characteristic skin nodules, insect exposure, and recent movement history. Laboratory confirmation is important because several other cattle diseases can resemble lumpy skin disease.

Diagnostic methods may include:

- Real-time polymerase chain reaction, or PCR
- Virus isolation
- Testing of skin nodule material
- Blood testing
- Swabs from lesions or secretions
- Histopathological examination of tissue

PCR testing of skin lesions is commonly used to detect LSDV and distinguish it from related capripoxviruses. The veterinarian may also investigate whether the infection is a field strain or related to a vaccine strain where appropriate.  

If you suspect lumpy skin disease, contact your veterinarian and the relevant animal health authority immediately. Do not move cattle, sell animals, share equipment, or transport animals to another property until you receive professional instructions.

## Is There a Treatment for Lumpy Skin Disease?

There is no specific antiviral treatment that cures lumpy skin disease. Management focuses on supportive care, animal welfare, and prevention of secondary complications.  

Your veterinarian may recommend:

- Anti-inflammatory medication for fever and pain
- Antibiotics when bacterial infections are present or considered likely
- Wound cleaning and protective dressings
- Fly control around skin lesions
- Fluids or nutritional support for weak animals
- Treatment for mastitis, pneumonia, or eye disease
- Adequate shade, clean water, and comfortable housing
- Careful monitoring of body temperature and appetite

Never give antibiotics, pain medication, insecticides, or other products without veterinary guidance. Incorrect drug use can cause toxicity, antimicrobial resistance, residues in milk or meat, and problems with withdrawal periods.

Keep affected cattle in a clean, dry area with easy access to feed and water. Soft feed may help animals with painful mouth lesions. Handle them gently because skin nodules, swollen limbs, and ulcers can be extremely painful.

Severely affected animals may require humane euthanasia if they cannot stand, eat, drink, or recover without prolonged suffering. This decision should be made with a veterinarian.

## How to Prevent Lumpy Skin Disease in Cattle

Prevention is more effective than attempting to manage a large outbreak after it begins. A complete prevention plan should combine vaccination, insect control, quarantine, movement restrictions, and daily monitoring.

### 1. Vaccinate according to local guidance

Vaccination is one of the most effective ways to reduce lumpy skin disease outbreaks and limit severe disease. Live attenuated LSDV vaccines generally provide strong protection, while sheep or goat pox vaccines may be used in certain countries and circumstances. Vaccine choice must follow local regulations and veterinary recommendations.  

Important vaccination practices include:

- Vaccinate healthy cattle before the high-risk season when possible.
- Follow the product label and official vaccination schedule.
- Ensure adequate coverage across the entire herd.
- Record animal identification, vaccine name, batch number, and date.
- Discuss booster requirements with your veterinarian.
- Do not assume vaccination prevents every infection.
- Report unexpected or severe vaccine reactions.

Some live vaccines can produce temporary reactions, and vaccine policies differ between countries. Never import or use an unapproved product.

### 2. Control biting insects

Insect control should be part of everyday herd management, especially during periods of high fly and mosquito activity.

Practical steps include:

- Remove standing water where possible.
- Improve drainage around barns and water troughs.
- Keep manure and decaying organic material under control.
- Use veterinarian-approved insecticides or repellents.
- Repair screens and reduce insect access to housing.
- Clean cattle shelters regularly.
- Avoid overcrowding.
- Inspect animals more frequently during peak insect seasons.

Insecticides alone may not stop an outbreak, but reducing vector numbers can lower transmission pressure.

### 3. Quarantine new and returning cattle

Keep new cattle separate from your herd and inspect them every day. Take particular care with animals from markets, shared grazing areas, livestock shows, or regions where LSD has been reported.

During quarantine:

- Use separate feed and water equipment.
- Avoid shared handling facilities.
- Do not share needles.
- Inspect the skin and lymph nodes.
- Monitor temperature and milk production.
- Control flies around the quarantine area.
- Arrange veterinary testing if signs develop.

### 4. Strengthen farm biosecurity

Use dedicated clothing, boots, and equipment when working with sick cattle. Clean and disinfect vehicles, trailers, milking equipment, ropes, halters, and handling facilities.

Dispose of skin crusts, contaminated bedding, and other infectious material according to veterinary and environmental regulations. Because the virus can persist in dried lesions and hides, do not leave infected material exposed where insects or other cattle can contact it.

### 5. Monitor the herd daily

Early detection can reduce the number of animals exposed. During an outbreak, check every animal daily for:

- Fever
- New lumps or skin changes
- Nasal or eye discharge
- Swollen lymph nodes
- Lameness
- Reduced appetite
- Reduced milk yield
- Swelling of the udder, legs, or brisket

Keep written records. A sudden drop in milk production or a cluster of fever cases may be an early warning even before obvious nodules appear.

## Economic Impact of Lumpy Skin Disease

The financial impact of lumpy skin disease can continue long after the outbreak ends. Affected cattle may recover slowly, lose body condition, produce less milk, and require extended care.

Farm losses may include:

- Lower milk sales
- Slower weight gain
- Reduced fertility
- Abortion and calf losses
- Bull infertility
- Veterinary and medication expenses
- Increased labour requirements
- Lower hide quality
- Death or euthanasia
- Restrictions on animal movement and trade

Milk production can fall sharply, particularly when cows have fever, mastitis, udder lesions, or reduced feed intake. Hide damage may remain after recovery because deep ulcers can heal with permanent scarring. 

## Can Humans Catch Lumpy Skin Disease?

No. Lumpy skin disease is considered non-zoonotic, meaning it does not infect humans. People are not considered at risk from handling affected cattle or consuming milk and meat because of LSDV itself.  

Nevertheless, normal farm hygiene remains essential. Wear gloves when handling open wounds, wash your hands after working with cattle, and follow food safety and veterinary instructions. Also remember that other infections or chemicals on a farm may pose human health risks even when LSDV does not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What causes lumpy skin disease in cattle?

Lumpy skin disease is caused by lumpy skin disease virus, a capripoxvirus in the poxvirus family. It mainly affects cattle and is spread primarily by biting insects.

### What are the first signs of lumpy skin disease?

Early signs may include high fever, depression, reduced appetite, watery eyes, nasal discharge, and enlarged lymph nodes. Firm skin nodules often appear soon afterward.

### Can lumpy skin disease kill cattle?

Yes, although mortality is often relatively low, severe disease can be fatal. Death is more likely when cattle develop pneumonia, severe secondary infections, dehydration, profound weight loss, or extensive skin damage.

### Is there a cure for lumpy skin disease?

There is no specific antiviral cure. Treatment is supportive and may include pain relief, wound care, fluids, fly control, and antibiotics for secondary bacterial infections.

### How can I prevent lumpy skin disease in my herd?

Use an approved vaccination program, control biting insects, quarantine new cattle, restrict animal movement during outbreaks, disinfect equipment, avoid sharing needles, and monitor cattle daily.

### Can lumpy skin disease spread directly from cow to cow?

Direct transmission is possible but generally less efficient than transmission by biting insects. The virus may also spread through contaminated equipment, secretions, skin lesions, semen, and animal materials.

### Is milk from an affected cow safe?

Lumpy skin disease is not considered a human disease. However, milk from sick cows may be unsuitable for sale or consumption under local regulations, and affected cows may have mastitis or receive medicines requiring a withdrawal period. Follow your veterinarian’s and food safety authority’s instructions.

### What should I do if I find lumps on a cow?

Separate the animal if possible, avoid moving cattle, do not share equipment, photograph or record the signs, and contact your veterinarian or animal health authority immediately.

## Conclusion

Lumpy skin disease in cattle is a preventable but economically important viral disease. Its most recognizable signs are fever, enlarged lymph nodes, and firm skin nodules, but severe cases may involve mastitis, pneumonia, lameness, abortion, infertility, weight loss, and permanent hide damage.

If you suspect lumpy skin disease, act quickly. Isolate the affected animal, stop unnecessary cattle movement, increase insect control, and seek veterinary confirmation. A well-planned vaccination program combined with quarantine, biosecurity, and daily herd monitoring gives your cattle the best protection against this damaging disease.
