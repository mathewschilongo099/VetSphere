## Hitting Your Calving Age Target

Getting your replacement stock to enter the milking string at the right stage of maturity is one of the most effective ways to protect your farm from unnecessary expenses. When young females enter production late, your feed costs climb steadily before the animal ever generates any income in the parlor. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that the desired age at first calving is generally targeted between 22 and 24 months old, based on the mature size of cows in your herd.

Reaching this specific age window requires consistent attention from weaning onward. USDA APHIS associates heavier body weights at calving with greater milk production and fewer calving difficulties, provided you manage the ration carefully. The key is achieving these heavier weights without pushing the animals into over-conditioning.

## Protecting Rumen Development in Young Calves

![several dairy cows with yellow ear tags inside a barn stall](https://images.pexels.com/photos/14197504/pexels-photo-14197504.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


Feeding young stock takes a different strategy than feeding mature milking cows, especially when pasture is part of the ration. MU Extension points out that no more than one-third of forage dry matter for heifers less than six months old should come from high-moisture forages like pasture. Young rumens simply lack the capacity and development required to process large volumes of washy grass efficiently without proper digestive support.

Because pasture alone cannot meet all structural growth needs during this early window, you also need to include at least minimal amounts of grain in the diet to maintain proper growth. Without this careful balance, skeletal frame development slows down, making it much harder for the animal to catch up later without over-conditioning. Matching the feed to the actual developmental stage of the digestive tract prevents digestive upsets and keeps the frame growing steadily toward your herd goals.

## Feeding Separately to Match Nutritional Demands

![several dairy cows eating feed from a trough in a barn](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1629313471551-ba2052384b9b?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyfHxkYWlyeSUyMGNvd3MlMjBmZWVkaW5nfGVufDF8MHx8fDE3OTU4MjcyOTN8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


One of the most common pitfalls in rearing replacement stock is housing and feeding them alongside the mature milking herd. Kansas State University Extension stresses that replacement heifers need to be fed separately from the rest of the mature cow herd due to higher nutritional demands and smaller sizes. If you let young replacement stock run with older, heavier cows, the older animals will reliably push the younger ones away from the feed bunks.

This competition means dominant cows get more than their share of nutrients while the replacement heifers fall behind. Even if you put out plenty of total feed, hierarchy ensures that younger animals miss out on the specific protein and energy concentrations they need for skeletal growth. Keeping replacement stock in their own designated pen gives them uninterrupted access to their rations and lets you adjust their diet precisely as they grow.

## Tracking Height and Weight Against Targets

Monitoring your feeding program requires more than just guessing by eye. You can evaluate your program success by monitoring the height and weight of heifers and comparing those results against breed averages or individual herd targets, according to Penn State Extension. Regular measurement catches stalls in growth long before breeding age arrives, giving you time to adjust rations or check for health issues.

Exact target body weight numbers for specific breeds are not always straightforward to apply without referencing the full Merck Veterinary Manual table, which can make mapping out precise milestones tricky for individual farms. To help simplify this process, Bovine Vet Online mentions that Penn State University developed a free, customizable, online spreadsheet to track heifer growth and compare your measurements directly against established benchmarks.

## What to Do This Week

Start by defining a clear target age at first calving, generally between 22 and 24 months old, based on the mature frame size of your current herd. Set up a regular routine to measure the height and weight of your replacement stock so you can compare their progress against breed averages. Move your replacement heifers into a separate pen away from the mature cow herd immediately if they are currently sharing space, ensuring they have uninterrupted access to their feed. Check your rations for calves under six months old to confirm that high-moisture forages make up no more than one-third of their forage dry matter, and make sure they are receiving minimal amounts of grain to support skeletal development. If you want to make tracking easier, access the free online spreadsheet tool from Penn State University to evaluate your growth data, and ask your local veterinary authority for advice if your animals are falling behind target measurements.
