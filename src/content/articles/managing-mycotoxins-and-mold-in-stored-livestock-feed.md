## The Hidden Toxins in Your Storage Bins

![several dairy cows with yellow ear tags standing in a barn](https://images.pexels.com/photos/69170/pexels-photo-69170.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


When you pull feed from storage to give to your animals, you expect it to nourish them. But damp conditions and poor storage can turn that feed into a source of hidden danger. Mycotoxins are toxic secondary metabolites produced by various fungi that contaminate livestock feed. They pose serious threats to animal health, productivity, and food safety. If your animals are dropping in condition, losing milk yield, or struggling with fertility, the feed in your bins might be the underlying issue.

Real-world conditions typically involve the simultaneous presence of multiple mycotoxins. When animals consume feed with more than one type, it results in additive or synergistic toxic effects that are often more severe than single toxin exposures. You are rarely dealing with just one isolated problem when mold takes hold.

## Where the Fungi Come From

![several light brown dairy cows standing inside a covered barn](https://images.pexels.com/photos/11357087/pexels-photo-11357087.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


To manage the problem, it helps to know what is growing in the feed. There are exactly 3 major genera of fungi that produce mycotoxins, and you need to watch out for all of them. These genera are Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium. 

Mycotoxins are named on the basis of the fungus that produces them. For example, Aflatoxin uses A for Aspergillus and fla for the species flavus along with the word toxin. Knowing the source helps you understand how the contamination started in the field or during storage.

Beyond what the animals eat, simply handling or standing near spoiled feed creates hazards for the people on the farm too. Mold may cause respiratory issues in livestock and humans. These respiratory issues are caused due to mold spores in the air around suspected moldy feed. Always protect your lungs when clearing out spoiled batches from your storage units.

## Practical Steps to Protect Your Crops and Feed

![multiple dairy cows lined up at a metal barrier eating feed](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1629313471551-ba2052384b9b?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyfHx2YWNjaW5hdGluZyUyMGRhaXJ5JTIwY293c3xlbnwxfDB8fHwxNzk1Mjg1MTMyfDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


Preventing contamination starts long before the feed reaches your storage bins. You can take specific actions on your land to give your feed the best defense possible. Plant species that are able to defend naturally against mycotoxins. Apply proper fertilization to support strong crop growth. Practice weed control to reduce unwanted plants that trap moisture and encourage fungal growth. Use proper crop rotation to break the life cycles of soil-borne fungi.

Despite your best efforts in the field, storage is where things often go wrong. Exact damp storage thresholds and moisture percentages that lead to toxin production in specific developing region storage systems remain difficult to pin down precisely, meaning you must rely on careful observation rather than exact meter readings. If you notice spoilage, act quickly to keep the rest of your stores safe.

## Testing Feed and Handling Binders

If you suspect a batch is bad, do not guess. Suspected moldy feed samples can be submitted to a commercial laboratory for a mold spore count and/or identification. A lab analysis will tell you exactly what you are dealing with so you can make informed decisions about whether to discard the batch or alter how you feed it.

When looking for solutions, you might encounter commercial products marketed to bind these toxins in the gut. Be aware of the regulatory landscape surrounding these products. The FDA regulates the use of binders in the U.S. under provisions of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. No mycotoxin binder has been approved by the FDA for use, making it illegal to market such chemicals as mycotoxin binders in the U.S. If you have questions about treatment protocols or managing affected animals, ask a veterinarian or your local veterinary authority before spending money on unapproved additives.

## What to Do This Week

Walk your storage areas today and check the moisture levels in your current feed supplies. Inspect your crop rotation plans and check your fields for weed pressure that could increase harvest moisture. If you find suspicious feed, bag a sample and arrange to send it to a commercial laboratory for a mold spore count and identification. Talk to your local vet about how to adjust your feeding routines safely if contamination is confirmed.
