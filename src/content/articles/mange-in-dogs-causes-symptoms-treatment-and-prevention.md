# Mange in Dogs: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Mange in Dogs is a common but often misunderstood skin disease that can cause intense itching, hair loss, and serious discomfort for affected animals. It is caused by microscopic mites that live on or in the skin, and can affect dogs of any age, breed, or background.  Because mange can be highly contagious (depending on the type), early recognition, correct diagnosis, and prompt treatment are essential to protect both your dog and other animals in the home.  This article explains what mange is, the causes of Mange in Dogs, symptoms, diagnosis, how to treat Mange in Dogs, and practical prevention and control strategies.

## What is Mange in Dogs?

Mange in Dogs is a skin disease caused by parasitic mites that infest a dog’s skin and hair follicles. These mites are so small that they cannot be seen with the naked eye, but their presence triggers inflammation, itching, and damage to the coat and skin.  Mange is essentially the canine version of mite infestations that, in humans, correspond to conditions like scabies (due to Sarcoptes mites) and demodicosis (due to Demodex mites).  

There are several important types of mange in dogs. The two main forms are sarcoptic mange (also called canine scabies) and demodectic mange (also called demodicosis or red mange).  Sarcoptic mange is caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. canis mites that burrow into the superficial layers of the skin and is highly contagious to other dogs and can also affect people temporarily.  Demodectic mange is caused by Demodex canis mites that live in hair follicles; most dogs carry small numbers of these mites normally, but disease occurs when they multiply excessively, often because the immune system is weakened.  

Some veterinarians and authors also include otodectic mange (ear mite infestations) under the broad umbrella of mange in dogs. Otodectes cynotis mites live in the ear canal and can cause intense irritation, head shaking, and dark waxy discharge.  Regardless of the specific type, all forms of Mange in Dogs can significantly affect a dog’s comfort and can lead to secondary infections if left untreated. 

## Causes of Mange in Dogs

*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*


The primary causes of Mange in Dogs are different species of mites that either burrow in the skin or live in hair follicles and ear canals. Sarcoptic mange is caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. canis mites, which burrow into the skin surface where they feed and lay eggs.  These mites are highly contagious and are usually transmitted by direct contact with an infected dog or by contact with contaminated bedding, grooming tools, or the environment.  Because the mites prefer canine hosts, they may cause short‑term itching in people but do not usually complete their life cycle on humans.  

Demodectic mange is caused by Demodex canis mites that live deep in hair follicles and sebaceous glands.  Nearly all dogs acquire Demodex mites from their mother shortly after birth and normally carry small populations without disease.  Disease appears when the dog’s immune system cannot adequately control the mite numbers. This can happen in puppies with immature immune systems, in malnourished or stressed animals, or in older dogs with underlying diseases such as hormonal disorders or cancer.  In many cases, generalized demodectic mange is considered associated with an inherited or immune‑related weakness, so affected dogs are often advised not to be bred.  

Otodectic mange (ear mites) is caused by Otodectes cynotis mites that live on the surface of the ear canal skin and sometimes on the surrounding head and neck.  These mites are also contagious and are commonly spread between puppies or between dogs and cats living together. Poor general health, crowded living conditions, and lack of parasite control all increase the risk of dogs acquiring mange. 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Mange in Dogs





![Symptoms of Mange in Dogs](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1573828260583-ec780bbe0c1a?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNYW5nZSUyMGluJTIwRG9ncyUyMHNpY2slMjBhbmltYWx8ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk1NzA2Mnww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*


The symptoms of Mange in Dogs vary with the type of mite, the severity of infestation, and the dog’s general health. However, all forms can lead to irritation, hair loss, and changes in the skin. In sarcoptic mange, the hallmark sign is intense itching (pruritus) that can become constant. Dogs scratch, bite, and rub their skin, leading to redness, scabs, and crusts. Hair loss often starts on the ears, elbows, chest, and belly and may spread over the body.  Secondary bacterial infections are common because the damaged skin barrier allows bacteria to invade, leading to pustules, bad odor, and thick crusts.  

Demodectic mange often begins with localized patches of hair loss, usually on the face, around the eyes and mouth, and on the front legs. The skin may appear red, scaly, and slightly itchy, though some dogs show little itch in the early stages.  Because the inflamed skin can look very red, generalized demodectic mange is often called “red mange.”  In more severe or generalized cases, large areas of the body become bald, thickened, and crusted, and secondary bacterial or fungal infections can occur, causing odor, oozing, and discomfort.  

Otodectic mange typically shows as ear‑related symptoms. Dogs may shake their heads frequently, scratch at their ears, and develop dark, crumbly or waxy discharge with an unpleasant odor.  The skin around the ears and on the head may become inflamed and hairless from scratching. In all types of mange, chronic cases can lead to poor overall body condition, weight loss, and restlessness due to constant itching and discomfort. 

## How to Diagnose Mange in Dogs

Correct diagnosis is critical because the symptoms of Mange in Dogs can look similar to allergies, flea infestations, fungal infections, or other skin diseases. A veterinarian will start with a full history and physical examination, noting where the lesions are, how severe the itching is, and whether other pets or people are affected.  The pattern—such as intense general pruritus with crusting in sarcoptic mange versus patchy hair loss with relatively mild itch in localized demodectic mange—can provide useful clues.  

The most common diagnostic test for mange is a skin scraping. The veterinarian uses a dull blade or scalpel to gently scrape the surface of the skin or squeeze and scrape deeper around hair follicles. The collected material is then examined under a microscope for the presence of mites, eggs, or mite feces.  This method can reliably detect Demodex mites in demodectic mange, but sarcoptic mites can be more difficult to find because they are few in number and move around the skin.  As a result, dogs can still have sarcoptic mange even if no mites are seen, and veterinarians may treat presumptively when the clinical signs strongly suggest this disease.  

In some cases, additional tests are needed. Skin biopsies may be taken if the diagnosis is uncertain or if deep follicular disease is suspected.  For dogs with generalized demodectic mange, veterinarians may also run blood tests, hormone profiles, or imaging such as radiographs to look for underlying problems that might suppress the immune system.  For otodectic mange, an ear swab examined under the microscope can reveal the characteristic moving ear mites or their eggs.  Accurate diagnosis guides the choice of treatment and helps monitor progress over time.

## Treatment of Mange in Dogs





![Treatment for Mange in Dogs](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1630438994394-3deff7a591bf?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNYW5nZSUyMGluJTIwRG9ncyUyMHZldGVyaW5hcnl8ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk1NzA2Mnww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*


The treatment of Mange in Dogs depends on the type of mange, the severity of the lesions, and any secondary infections present. Modern veterinary medicine offers several effective antiparasitic drugs that can kill mites and help resolve the disease. Many of these treatments are in the isoxazoline class (such as afoxolaner), given orally as chewable tablets, or as topical spot‑on formulations.  For example, afoxolaner has been shown to be effective against both sarcoptic and demodectic mange in dogs.  Products such as certain monthly chewable preventives have documented efficacy in treating sarcoptic, demodectic, and otodectic mange when used according to veterinary instructions.  

In sarcoptic mange, dogs typically receive systemic antiparasitic treatment for at least two consecutive monthly doses, sometimes longer, until clinical signs resolve and skin scrapings are negative for mites.  Additional medications may be prescribed to relieve itching (such as anti‑inflammatory drugs), treat secondary bacterial or yeast infections (antibiotics, antifungals), and support skin healing with medicated shampoos or topical treatments.  Because sarcoptic mange is highly contagious, environmental hygiene and isolation from other animals are important during treatment.  

Demodectic mange treatment focuses on reducing mite numbers and supporting the dog’s immune system. Localized demodectic mange in young dogs sometimes resolves spontaneously or with minimal topical therapy, whereas generalized cases often require longer‑term systemic antiparasitic medications, sometimes for several months.  Veterinarians may also address underlying factors such as poor nutrition, stress, or concurrent disease and treat secondary skin infections with antibiotics or antifungals as needed.  Regular follow‑up skin scrapings are used to monitor progress, and treatment is usually continued until two or more consecutive scrapings are negative for mites.  

Otodectic mange is treated with appropriate ear mite products—either topical ear drops or systemic medications that are effective against Otodectes mites.  The ears are often gently cleaned to remove debris before treatment. As with other forms of mange, all in‑contact animals may need to be treated to prevent re‑infestation.  With correct veterinary guidance, most cases of Mange in Dogs can be successfully managed, and dogs can return to a comfortable, normal life. 

## Prevention and Control of Mange in Dogs





![Prevention of Mange in Dogs](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1680718349543-2016f3e618cd?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNYW5nZSUyMGluJTIwRG9ncyUyMGZhcm0lMjBwcmV2ZW50aW9ufGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NTcwNjJ8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*


Effective prevention of Mange in Dogs focuses on limiting exposure to mites, maintaining good general health, and using appropriate parasite control. For sarcoptic and otodectic mange, avoiding direct contact with infected animals and not sharing bedding, grooming tools, or living spaces with affected dogs are key strategies.  In kennels, shelters, and multi‑dog households, strict hygiene, regular cleaning, and isolation of suspected cases help stop outbreaks.  

Routine use of broad‑spectrum parasite preventives that are effective against mites, fleas, and ticks can play an important role in the prevention of Mange in Dogs. Several monthly oral or topical products have documented activity against sarcoptic, demodectic, and otodectic mites when used regularly as directed.  These products not only treat active infestations but also reduce the risk of re‑infestation. Dog owners should consult their veterinarian to select the most suitable preventive based on the dog’s age, health status, and lifestyle.  

For demodectic mange, prevention is more about managing risk factors than avoiding exposure, since most dogs carry Demodex mites from early life.  Good nutrition, parasite control, reduction of stress, and regular health checks help keep the immune system strong so it can control mite populations. Because generalized demodectic mange may have a hereditary component, many veterinarians recommend that affected dogs not be bred, to reduce the risk of passing on genetic susceptibility.  Early veterinary attention for any skin problem improves the chances of quick control and prevents progression to severe, generalized mange.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Mange in Dogs

### Q: What are the first symptoms of Mange in Dogs?

**A:** Early symptoms of Mange in Dogs often include localized hair loss, redness, and mild scaling, usually on the face, ears, or front legs in demodectic mange, or intense itching around the ears, elbows, chest, and belly in sarcoptic mange.  As the disease progresses, dogs may develop scabs, crusts, thicker skin, and more widespread hair loss. Otodectic mange typically starts with ear scratching, head shaking, and dark ear discharge. 

### Q: Is Mange in Dogs contagious to humans or other pets?

**A:** Sarcoptic mange is highly contagious to other dogs and can cause temporary itchy skin lesions in humans and other animals that have close contact with infected dogs.  However, the mites do not usually complete their life cycle on non‑canine hosts, so human symptoms are typically self‑limiting once contact is removed.  Demodectic mange is generally not considered contagious between adult dogs, as Demodex mites are normal skin inhabitants acquired early in life.  Otodectic mange can spread easily between dogs and cats living together, so all in‑contact pets may need treatment. 

### Q: How long does it take to treat Mange in Dogs?

**A:** The duration of treatment depends on the type and severity of mange. Sarcoptic mange may show improvement within a few weeks of starting effective antiparasitic therapy, but treatment is usually continued for at least two monthly doses and until skin scrapings are negative.  Localized demodectic mange in young dogs may resolve within several weeks with minimal treatment, while generalized demodectic mange often requires months of therapy and repeated monitoring until mites are eliminated on consecutive skin scrapings. 

### Q: Can Mange in Dogs come back after treatment?

**A:** Yes, mange can recur, especially if underlying problems are not corrected or if the dog is re‑exposed to contagious mites. Dogs with an inherited or immune‑related predisposition to demodectic mange may experience relapses, particularly during times of stress or illness.  Sarcoptic mange can return if the environment is contaminated or if in‑contact animals are not treated, allowing reinfestation.  Ongoing parasite prevention and regular veterinary check‑ups help reduce the risk of recurrence. 

### Q: What is the best way to treat Mange in Dogs at home?

**A:** Mange in Dogs should always be managed under veterinary supervision. While there are many home remedies discussed online, they are often ineffective or even harmful to the dog’s skin. Evidence‑based treatment relies on veterinary‑prescribed antiparasitic medications (such as certain oral isoxazolines or topical products) combined with appropriate management of secondary infections and skin care.  Attempting to treat mange at home without diagnosis can delay proper care, worsen the disease, and increase the risk of spreading it to other animals and people. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Dog owners should contact a veterinarian promptly if they notice persistent itching, hair loss, scabs, or changes in the skin that do not improve quickly. Intense scratching, especially around the ears, elbows, chest, and belly, or sudden development of crusty lesions, should be considered urgent signs that may indicate sarcoptic mange or another serious skin disease.  Likewise, patchy bald spots on the face or legs, red inflamed skin, or foul odor from the coat may signal demodectic mange or secondary infections that require professional care.  

Any ear problems—such as continuous head shaking, scratching at the ears, or dark, smelly ear discharge—warrant veterinary examination to check for otodectic mange, ear infections, or other ear diseases.  Owners should also seek veterinary help if other pets or human family members develop itching or skin rashes after contact with a dog that has suspicious skin signs, as this may point to a contagious condition like sarcoptic mange.  Early veterinary involvement improves the chances of a quick, effective treatment plan and helps prevent spread in multi‑pet households, kennels, or farms. 

## Conclusion

Mange in Dogs is a significant but manageable skin disease caused by microscopic mites that damage the skin and coat. Understanding the different types—sarcoptic, demodectic, and otodectic—helps owners recognize the variety of symptoms, from intense itching and crusting to patchy hair loss and ear irritation.  Prompt veterinary diagnosis, typically using skin scrapings, ear examinations, and sometimes additional tests, is essential to distinguish mange from other skin problems and to guide appropriate therapy.  

Modern antiparasitic medications, given orally or topically, have greatly improved the outlook for dogs with mange, especially when combined with careful management of secondary infections, environmental hygiene, and good overall health care.  Prevention of Mange in Dogs relies on regular parasite control, minimizing contact with infected animals, and supporting a strong immune system through proper nutrition and veterinary care.  With informed owners and timely veterinary support, most dogs with mange can recover fully and return to a comfortable, healthy life.
