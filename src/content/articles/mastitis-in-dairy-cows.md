Mastitis in dairy cows is one of the most important health challenges facing dairy producers. It affects the udder, reduces milk production, lowers milk quality, increases treatment costs, and can compromise cow welfare. In severe cases, mastitis can lead to permanent udder damage, premature culling, or death.

The good news is that a consistent mastitis control program can prevent many infections. Clean milking practices, healthy teat ends, dry bedding, regular somatic cell count monitoring, and prompt veterinary care all play an important role in protecting your herd.

This guide explains what mastitis is, how to recognize it, what causes it, how veterinarians diagnose and treat it, and which daily management practices help prevent it.

## What Is Mastitis in Dairy Cows?

Mastitis is inflammation of one or more mammary glands, usually caused by microorganisms entering the udder through the teat canal. Bacteria are the most common cause, although fungi, yeasts, algae, and other organisms can also produce mastitis.

When pathogens enter the udder, the cow’s immune system responds by sending white blood cells into the mammary tissue and milk. This immune response causes inflammation and can change the appearance, composition, and quantity of milk.

Mastitis is generally classified into two forms:

- **Clinical mastitis**, which causes visible changes in the milk or udder
- **Subclinical mastitis**, which has no obvious outward signs but raises the somatic cell count and may gradually reduce production

Both forms matter. Clinical mastitis is easier to notice, but subclinical mastitis can spread quietly through a herd and create substantial production losses over time.

## Clinical vs. Subclinical Mastitis

### Clinical mastitis

Clinical mastitis causes visible abnormalities in the milk, udder, or cow. Signs may affect only one quarter or may involve the entire animal.

Common signs include:

- Flakes, clots, strings, or pus in the milk
- Watery, yellow, pink, red, or brown milk
- A hot, swollen, firm, or painful udder quarter
- Reduced milk production
- Reluctance to let down milk
- Restlessness during milking
- Fever
- Depression or weakness
- Reduced appetite
- Dehydration
- Diarrhea in severe cases

Clinical cases may be mild, moderate, or severe. A cow with abnormal milk but normal behavior may have mild clinical mastitis. A cow with an abnormal udder and fever, weakness, or loss of appetite requires urgent veterinary attention.

### Subclinical mastitis

Subclinical mastitis does not usually cause visible swelling, pain, or abnormal milk. The cow may look healthy, yet the infected quarter produces less milk and milk quality declines.

The main indicators are:

- Increased somatic cell count
- Reduced milk yield
- Repeated high cell counts
- A higher risk of future clinical mastitis
- Changes in milk composition
- Poorer processing quality

Somatic cell count, or SCC, measures the number of cells—primarily immune cells—in milk. An SCC near or above 200,000 cells per milliliter is commonly used as an indicator of possible intramammary infection, although SCC can also vary with age, stage of lactation, stress, breed, and other factors.  

## What Causes Mastitis in Dairy Cows?

Mastitis occurs when pathogens reach the teat opening, move through the teat canal, and establish an infection in the mammary gland. The risk increases when the teat end is dirty, damaged, poorly disinfected, or exposed to contaminated bedding, manure, water, equipment, or hands.

Mastitis pathogens are commonly divided into two groups.

### Contagious mastitis pathogens

Contagious organisms spread primarily from cow to cow during milking. They may be transferred by:

- Milking machines
- Milker’s hands or gloves
- Reusable towels
- Contaminated teat cups
- Milk droplets
- Poorly maintained equipment

Important contagious pathogens include *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus agalactiae*, and *Mycoplasma* species. These infections can persist in the udder and may produce long-term high SCC.

### Environmental mastitis pathogens

Environmental organisms live in bedding, soil, manure, mud, water, and other areas around the farm. They may enter the teat canal between milkings or during milking.

Common environmental pathogens include coliform bacteria such as *Escherichia coli*, environmental streptococci, and *Klebsiella* species.

Environmental mastitis risk rises when cows are exposed to:

- Wet or dirty bedding
- Muddy lots
- Flooded or poorly drained areas
- Dirty calving pens
- Excessive manure contamination
- Heat stress
- Teat-end damage
- Poor udder hygiene

Good milking hygiene helps control both contagious and environmental mastitis. However, environmental pathogens require additional attention to housing, bedding, calving areas, and cow comfort.  

## Risk Factors for Mastitis

Some cows are more vulnerable to mastitis than others. Important risk factors include:

- Early lactation
- The first weeks after calving
- The period immediately before and after dry-off
- Older age and multiple lactations
- Teat-end injuries or hyperkeratosis
- Pendulous udders
- Poor teat conformation
- High milk production
- Leaking milk
- Incomplete teat closure during the dry period
- Wet or dirty housing
- Poorly functioning milking equipment
- Overmilking
- Incorrect vacuum levels
- Inadequate nutrition
- Heat stress
- Inconsistent milking routines

The dry period is especially important. The udder remains vulnerable to new infections after milking stops and again during the weeks before calving. High-producing cows may take longer to form a natural keratin plug in the teat canal, leaving an entry route for bacteria. 

## How to Detect Mastitis Early

Early detection improves treatment decisions and helps prevent infected cows from exposing the rest of the herd.

### Check milk at every milking

Strip a few streams of milk from each quarter into a strip cup before attaching the milking unit. Look for:

- Clots
- Flakes
- Watery milk
- Blood
- Pus
- Unusual color
- Abnormal texture

Do not strip milk onto the floor because this can spread infectious material and contaminate the environment.

### Examine the udder

Check each quarter for:

- Heat
- Swelling
- Hardness
- Redness
- Pain
- Uneven size
- Firm areas or lumps

A quarter that suddenly becomes enlarged or painful should be evaluated promptly.

### Monitor cow behavior

Watch for reduced appetite, depression, fever, dehydration, reluctance to move, or unusual behavior during milking. Systemic signs may indicate severe mastitis and require emergency veterinary care.

### Use the California Mastitis Test

The California Mastitis Test, or CMT, is a practical cow-side test for identifying quarters with elevated somatic cells. It can help find subclinical infections that are not visible during routine milking.

### Track somatic cell counts

Regular individual-cow SCC records can help you identify:

- Newly infected cows
- Chronic high-SCC cows
- Problem quarters
- Herd-level trends
- Whether prevention changes are working

SCC monitoring is one of the most useful tools for finding subclinical mastitis.  

## Diagnosing Mastitis

A veterinarian may diagnose mastitis using several methods:

1. Physical examination of the cow and udder
2. Inspection of foremilk
3. CMT testing
4. Individual-cow SCC testing
5. Milk culture
6. Antibiotic sensitivity testing
7. Evaluation of herd treatment and production records

Milk culture is particularly useful because it can identify the organism responsible for infection. Culture results may show whether the case involves a contagious pathogen, environmental bacteria, yeast, or another cause.

Culture also helps your veterinarian decide whether antibiotics are appropriate. Not every case of abnormal milk requires the same treatment, and some mild cases may resolve without antimicrobial therapy. Merck Veterinary Manual notes that culture is the only reliable way to determine the cause of many clinical mastitis cases.  

For the best results, collect a milk sample before treatment begins. Use sterile equipment, clean the teat end carefully, and follow your veterinarian’s sampling instructions.

## Treatment of Mastitis in Dairy Cows

Treatment depends on the severity of the disease, the suspected pathogen, the cow’s history, and the results of culture or herd protocols.

### Mild cases

A cow with abnormal milk but no fever or systemic illness may have mild mastitis. Your veterinarian may recommend:

- Collecting a milk sample for culture
- Segregating the cow
- Discarding milk from the affected quarter
- Using an approved intramammary treatment when indicated
- Monitoring temperature, appetite, and behavior
- Rechecking the quarter during treatment

Some mild cases can be monitored while waiting for culture results, particularly when the cow is otherwise healthy. Treatment decisions should follow a written herd protocol rather than guesswork.

### Severe cases

Severe mastitis is an emergency. Contact your veterinarian immediately if the cow shows:

- Fever
- Depression
- Weakness
- Rapid breathing
- Dehydration
- Cold ears or extremities
- Shock
- Loss of appetite
- Inability to stand
- Severe udder inflammation

Severely affected cows may need fluids, electrolytes, anti-inflammatory medication, nursing care, and antimicrobial treatment. Supportive care is often a critical part of managing severe cases, especially when bacterial toxins cause systemic illness. 

### Antibiotic stewardship

Use antibiotics only when appropriate and under veterinary direction. Treatment should be based on the organism involved, the cow’s clinical condition, previous treatment history, and the farm’s written protocol.

Never:

- Use leftover medication without veterinary guidance
- Combine products unless instructed
- Use human antibiotics
- Use homemade intramammary preparations
- Infuse dirty products or equipment
- Ignore the label
- Sell milk before the withdrawal period ends

Milk and meat withdrawal times must be followed exactly. Extra-label drug use requires veterinary oversight and a properly established withdrawal interval. Treatment records should include the cow’s identification, product, dose, route, date, person administering it, and the date milk and meat can safely re-enter the food supply.  

## Dry Cow Therapy and Teat Sealants

Dry cow management is a major part of mastitis control. Approved dry cow therapy can eliminate existing infections and reduce new infections during the early dry period.

Depending on herd history and veterinary advice, a dry-off program may include:

- An approved long-acting intramammary antibiotic
- An internal teat sealant
- Selective treatment based on SCC and culture results
- Blanket treatment of every quarter
- Improved dry cow housing
- Careful observation after dry-off

An internal teat sealant creates a physical barrier that helps prevent bacteria from entering the teat canal. It does not kill bacteria, so it must be used appropriately, especially when infection status is uncertain. Dry cow therapy and teat sealants should be selected with your veterinarian.   

Aseptic technique is essential when administering any intramammary product:

1. Milk the cow completely.
2. Clean and dry the teats.
3. Disinfect each teat end with an approved germicide.
4. Scrub each teat end with a separate alcohol swab.
5. Treat the far teats before the near teats.
6. Use one sterile syringe per quarter.
7. Avoid touching the cannula.
8. Insert only the recommended portion of the cannula.
9. Apply post-treatment teat dip.
10. Clearly identify the treated cow.

Poor preparation can push bacteria directly into the udder and cause a serious new infection.

## How to Prevent Mastitis

Prevention is more effective and usually more economical than repeated treatment.

### Follow a consistent milking routine

Every milker should follow the same steps at every milking:

- Wear clean gloves.
- Check the foremilk.
- Pre-dip teats with an effective germicide.
- Allow the required contact time.
- Wipe teats with individual clean towels.
- Attach the unit only when teats are clean and dry.
- Avoid overmilking.
- Remove the unit correctly.
- Apply postmilking teat dip immediately after removal.

Postmilking teat disinfection is considered one of the most effective practices for reducing mastitis transmission in lactating cows.  

### Keep bedding clean and dry

Replace wet bedding regularly and remove manure from high-traffic areas. Pay special attention to:

- Freestalls
- Calving pens
- Hospital areas
- Holding pens
- Dry cow housing
- Water trough areas

A cow should be able to lie down in a clean, dry, comfortable place.

### Maintain milking equipment

Have the milking system evaluated regularly. Check:

- Vacuum stability
- Pulsator function
- Liners
- Air hoses
- Milk hoses
- Claw pieces
- Reverse flow
- Milking time
- Teat-end condition after milking

Improper vacuum or worn liners can damage teat ends and increase the risk of infection. 

### Separate infected cows

Milk cows with known contagious infections last whenever possible. Clean and disinfect equipment between cows when appropriate, and maintain separate treatment and hospital areas.

### Control flies and mud

Flies can transfer pathogens, particularly around dry cows and heifers. Reduce standing water, keep lots clean, use appropriate fly control, and avoid allowing cattle to congregate in muddy areas.

### Support immune function

Good nutrition supports udder defenses. Work with a qualified nutritionist to provide balanced energy, protein, vitamins, minerals, and adequate water. Prevent excessive body condition loss, especially during the transition period.

## When Should You Call a Veterinarian?

Contact your veterinarian immediately when:

- The cow has a fever
- She is depressed or not eating
- The udder is severely swollen or painful
- Milk is bloody, foul-smelling, or pus-like
- More than one quarter is affected
- The cow cannot stand
- Treatment has failed
- Mastitis keeps returning in the same quarter
- Several cows develop mastitis close together
- Herd SCC rises suddenly
- You suspect *Mycoplasma* or another unusual pathogen

A sudden increase in cases may indicate a milking-equipment problem, contaminated bedding, poor teat-dip technique, or a contagious outbreak.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Mastitis in Dairy Cows

### 1. Can a cow have mastitis if her milk looks normal?

Yes. Subclinical mastitis often produces normal-looking milk. Elevated SCC, CMT results, reduced production, or a positive milk culture may reveal the infection.

### 2. Is mastitis always caused by bacteria?

No. Bacteria are the most common cause, but yeasts, fungi, algae, and other microorganisms can also cause mastitis. Culture is important when treatment fails or unusual infections are suspected.

### 3. What does a high somatic cell count mean?

A high SCC usually indicates inflammation in the udder and may indicate an intramammary infection. A single high result should be interpreted alongside the cow’s stage of lactation, history, CMT results, and culture findings.

### 4. Should every case of mastitis be treated with antibiotics?

No. Treatment depends on the severity of the case, the suspected cause, culture results, and your veterinarian’s protocol. Unnecessary antibiotic use increases costs and contributes to resistance.

### 5. Can mastitis spread from cow to cow?

Yes. Contagious pathogens can spread during milking through hands, equipment, towels, and milk droplets. Separating infected cows and improving milking hygiene can reduce transmission.

### 6. How can you prevent mastitis during the dry period?

Use an evidence-based dry cow program, keep dry cows in clean and dry housing, use approved products correctly, consider an internal teat sealant when appropriate, and monitor cows closely after dry-off and before calving.

### 7. Is a swollen udder always mastitis?

No. Udder edema, injury, teat lesions, or other conditions can cause swelling. However, a hot, painful, firm, or rapidly changing quarter should be evaluated for mastitis.

### 8. How quickly should severe mastitis be treated?

Immediately. A cow with fever, depression, dehydration, weakness, or shock may deteriorate quickly and needs urgent veterinary assessment and supportive care.

## Conclusion

Mastitis in dairy cows is both an animal health problem and a milk-quality challenge. The most effective strategy is not simply treating individual cases—it is preventing pathogens from entering the udder in the first place.

Focus on clean, dry bedding; a consistent milking routine; effective pre- and postmilking teat disinfection; healthy teat ends; well-maintained equipment; regular SCC monitoring; careful dry cow management; and prompt veterinary involvement.

When you combine early detection with strong prevention and responsible treatment, you protect cow welfare, improve milk quality, reduce unnecessary antibiotic use, and support a more productive and profitable dairy herd.
