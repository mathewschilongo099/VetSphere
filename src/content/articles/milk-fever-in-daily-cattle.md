# Milk fever in daily cattle

## Introduction



![milk fever in daily cattle](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxtaWxrJTIwZmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMGRhaWx5JTIwY2F0dGxlJTIwYW5pbWFsfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NDkwODl8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: milk fever in daily cattle — via Unsplash*


Milk fever, also called milk sickness, parturient paresis or periparturient hypocalcemia, is a common and serious metabolic disease of dairy cows that happens around calving. It occurs when the cow’s blood calcium level becomes too low to support normal muscle and nerve function at the start of lactation. High‑yielding, older dairy cows (third lactation and above) are most at risk, and incidence in many herds is around 5–10% each year, with even more cows affected by milder, “subclinical” low calcium.  

The condition is an emergency because calcium is essential for muscle contraction, including the muscles that allow the cow to stand, swallow, and the smooth muscles of the gut and uterus. When calcium drops sharply at calving, cows may become weak, unable to rise, and can die within hours without treatment. In addition, even mild hypocalcemia increases the risk of other fresh‑cow problems like retained placenta, metritis, displaced abomasum, ketosis and mastitis, so milk fever has a large impact on herd health, welfare and farm profitability.  

Understanding why milk fever happens, how to recognize it early, and how to prevent it through correct feeding and management in the dry period is essential knowledge for farmers, students and animal owners. Prompt, appropriate treatment usually results in rapid, dramatic recovery, but delay or incorrect handling can lead to permanent damage or death. 

## Causes



![Causes of milk fever in daily cattle](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxtaWxrJTIwZmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMGRhaWx5JTIwY2F0dGxlJTIwZGlzZWFzZXxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ5MDg5fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*


Milk fever is caused by a sudden drop in blood calcium concentration around calving. During the dry period, a cow’s daily calcium need is relatively modest and is used mainly for fetal growth and normal losses in urine and feces. At calving, calcium demand rises sharply because colostrum and early milk contain large amounts of calcium, and the udder may draw more than 50 g of calcium per day. If the cow’s body cannot quickly increase calcium absorption from the gut and release calcium from bone, blood levels fall to dangerously low values and clinical milk fever appears.  

The cow’s body controls calcium through parathyroid hormone (PTH) and vitamin D. When blood calcium drops, PTH should stimulate bone to release calcium, increase calcium re‑absorption in the kidney, and activate vitamin D to boost calcium absorption in the intestine. In cows that develop milk fever, tissues are less responsive to PTH in the days before calving, so these emergency mechanisms start too slowly. Factors that interfere with PTH response include high dietary potassium and calcium in the dry period, and imbalances in magnesium, which is needed for PTH to work correctly.  

Several risk factors increase the chance of milk fever: high milk yield, older age (especially cows in third or later lactations), certain breeds such as Jerseys and Guernseys, high‑potassium or high‑calcium dry cow diets, over‑conditioned cows, and low magnesium intake. Herds using a lot of potassium‑rich fertilizers on forages, or feeding large amounts of lush grass or some legume forages to dry cows, often have more cases. Organic herds with lower yields and less potassium fertilizer tend to have lower incidence. 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms



![Symptoms of milk fever in daily cattle](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1596733430284-f7437764b1a9?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxtaWxrJTIwZmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMGRhaWx5JTIwY2F0dGxlJTIwc2ljayUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ5MDg5fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*


Most clinical signs are due to poor muscle and nerve function. Signs often develop in three stages, progressing over several hours if untreated. In early (stage I) milk fever, the cow is still standing but shows restlessness, excitability, mild muscle tremors, stiffness, shifting weight, and poor appetite. She may show a slight drop in body temperature and reduced rumen motility. This stage can be subtle and easy to miss in a busy calving pen.  

In stage II, the cow is usually down in sternal recumbency (lying on her chest) and unable or unwilling to rise. Classic features include a dull or depressed attitude, cold ears and extremities, dry muzzle, weak or absent rumen contractions, a slow heart rate but with a weak pulse, and low body temperature. The cow often lies with her head turned toward her flank in an “S‑shaped” curve of the neck, a very typical posture for milk fever. She may grind her teeth or show bloat because the rumen stops moving. Without treatment, this stage commonly lasts from 1 to 12 hours.  

Stage III milk fever is life‑threatening. The cow is completely unable to sit up and lies flat on her side, often unresponsive. Muscle tone is severely reduced, legs are extended, and there may be severe bloat and gas accumulation. The heart rate is very high but weak, breathing is shallow, and the body temperature is often low. If no treatment is given at this point, death may occur within a few hours due to respiratory failure, circulatory collapse, or complications such as bloat or aspiration pneumonia. Some cows that remain down for long periods can develop “downer cow syndrome” from muscle and nerve damage. 

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis on farm is mainly based on history and clinical examination. The typical case is an older, high‑producing dairy cow that becomes weak or recumbent within 24–72 hours of calving. The combination of recent calving, inability to stand, reduced muscle tone, cold extremities, and the characteristic neck posture strongly suggests milk fever. A veterinarian will assess mental status, posture, attempts to rise, muscle tremors, rumen activity, heart rate and rhythm, and rectal temperature.  

Because other diseases can also cause a fresh cow to be down, it is important not to assume that every recumbent cow has milk fever. Differential diagnoses include severe toxic mastitis, metritis, traumatic injuries to the pelvis or hips, nerve damage during calving, severe ketosis, or other metabolic and infectious conditions. Toxic mastitis often causes high fever and obvious udder changes, while metritis usually appears several days after calving with foul discharge. Traumatic injuries and nerve damage produce abnormal limb position or pain responses without the typical metabolic signs of hypocalcemia. A careful physical exam helps to distinguish these conditions.  

Definitive diagnosis can be made by measuring blood calcium concentration, but this is not always practical in the field. Normal plasma calcium in cows is usually about 2.2–2.6 mmol/L (8.8–10.4 mg/100 ml). Clinical milk fever signs typically appear when blood calcium falls to around 0.75–1.5 mmol/L (3–6 mg/100 ml). Many cows at calving have mild reductions without obvious signs, a condition called subclinical hypocalcemia, which can still impair performance and increase disease risk. Monitoring blood calcium in a sample of fresh cows is useful for herd‑level assessment. 

## Treatment



![Treatment for milk fever in daily cattle](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1596733430284-f7437764b1a9?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxtaWxrJTIwZmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMGRhaWx5JTIwY2F0dGxlJTIwdmV0ZXJpbmFyeXxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ5MDg5fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*


Milk fever is a true emergency. The main goal of treatment is to restore blood calcium levels quickly and safely. The standard treatment for a recumbent cow with clear signs of milk fever is slow intravenous (IV) administration of a calcium solution, most commonly calcium borogluconate. A typical dose for an adult dairy cow is about 500 ml of a 23% calcium borogluconate solution given slowly IV while listening to the heart, which provides roughly 10–11 g of calcium. A practical rule is approximately 1 ml per kg body weight.  

The calcium must be given slowly because rapid IV administration can cause dangerous heart arrhythmias and even sudden death, particularly with strong calcium salts like calcium chloride. During infusion, the veterinarian or trained operator monitors heart rate and rhythm and stops or slows if abnormalities occur. In less severe or early cases, calcium may be given subcutaneously or orally as gels or boluses instead of IV. Oral calcium is often used as a preventative supplement around calving or as follow‑up after IV therapy to maintain blood levels as the cow continues to lose calcium in milk.  

Most cows respond dramatically within 30 minutes of IV treatment, sitting up, becoming more alert, burping out gas, and attempting to stand. Supportive care is essential: the cow should be propped into sternal position, protected from slipping, given soft bedding, and turned regularly if she remains down to prevent muscle and nerve damage. Warmth and good nursing, along with fluids and multivitamins in some cases, aid recovery. If a cow does not respond to an initial treatment, re‑examination for other conditions (injuries, severe mastitis, metritis) is vital before repeating calcium therapy. 

## Prevention and Control



![Prevention of milk fever in daily cattle](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1583095117886-ddb019562ee0?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxtaWxrJTIwZmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMGRhaWx5JTIwY2F0dGxlJTIwZmFybSUyMHByZXZlbnRpb258ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk0OTA4OXww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*


Effective prevention focuses on dry‑period nutrition and close‑up cow management. The aim is to prepare the cow’s calcium regulatory system to respond quickly at calving. One key approach is to feed a properly formulated dry cow diet with controlled calcium and potassium levels and adequate magnesium. Diets that are too high in calcium and potassium before calving can blunt the cow’s PTH response; therefore, many programs reduce dietary calcium and use forages and supplements with lower potassium content.  

Another widely used strategy is feeding a negative Dietary Cation–Anion Difference (DCAD) diet in the last 3–4 weeks before calving. This involves adding anionic salts (sources of chloride and sulfur) to slightly acidify the cow’s blood, which improves tissue response to PTH and increases bone calcium mobilization. Urine pH testing in close‑up cows is commonly used to monitor whether the DCAD program is working; a slightly acidic urine indicates effective acidification. When correctly managed, DCAD programs can greatly reduce or almost eliminate clinical milk fever in some herds.  

At the individual cow level, oral calcium supplements (boluses, pastes or gels) given at calving and again 12–24 hours later can help older or high‑risk cows. Good body condition management, avoiding over‑fat dry cows, ensuring adequate magnesium intake, and minimizing stress and overcrowding around calving all support normal feed intake and metabolic adaptation. Regular herd monitoring of clinical cases, subclinical hypocalcemia (through blood testing), and related disorders like retained placenta allows evaluation and adjustment of the prevention program over time. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

A veterinarian should be called immediately whenever a cow near calving is unable to stand, is very weak, or shows the typical signs of milk fever such as a cold body, dullness, muscle tremors, and the head bent towards the flank. Because death can occur within hours in severe cases, waiting “to see if she improves” is dangerous. Rapid IV calcium therapy, properly administered, is often life‑saving, and only a veterinarian or suitably trained person should perform it due to the risk of heart complications if done incorrectly.  

You should also seek veterinary help if a cow treated for suspected milk fever does not improve quickly, remains down after treatment, or relapses within a short time. These situations may indicate underlying injuries, nerve damage, toxic mastitis, metritis, or other metabolic diseases that require additional diagnostics and therapy. A veterinarian can perform a full clinical exam, consider other causes of recumbency, and recommend appropriate nursing care and follow‑up treatments.  

Beyond emergency situations, involve your veterinarian in designing and reviewing a milk fever prevention program for the herd. This includes ration evaluation for dry and close‑up cows, decisions about use of anionic salts and oral calcium supplements, and strategies to monitor subclinical hypocalcemia. Regular professional input helps to reduce the incidence of milk fever, improve fresh‑cow health, and increase long‑term herd productivity and welfare. 

## Conclusion

Milk fever is a common, largely preventable metabolic disease of dairy cows that occurs around calving due to a sudden fall in blood calcium when milk production begins. It mainly affects older, high‑yielding cows and can lead to weakness, recumbency, and death if not treated promptly. Even when not obvious, low calcium around calving predisposes cows to many other costly disorders of early lactation, reducing milk production, fertility and longevity.  

Accurate recognition of early clinical signs, rapid veterinary treatment with carefully administered calcium solutions, and good nursing care mean that most affected cows can recover fully. However, long‑term control depends on sound dry‑period feeding and management, including appropriate mineral balance, DCAD strategies, and targeted use of oral calcium in high‑risk animals. Monitoring herd performance and working closely with a veterinarian to adjust prevention programs over time offers the best protection for cow welfare and farm profitability.
