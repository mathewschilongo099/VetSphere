# Milk Fever in Dairy Cows: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction





![Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTU3MTQ4fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Milk Fever in Dairy Cows — via Unsplash*


Milk Fever in Dairy Cows is one of the most important metabolic diseases affecting modern dairy herds, especially around calving. It is closely linked to low blood calcium (hypocalcemia) and can quickly lead to weakness, collapse, and death if not treated promptly.  Understanding the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows helps farmers, students, and animal owners protect cow health and maintain herd productivity.

This condition is highly relevant to high‑producing dairy cows, particularly older animals in their second or later lactations. The sudden jump in calcium demand at the start of lactation makes these cows vulnerable if their metabolism is not well prepared.  Good transition cow management and nutrition before and after calving are essential for reducing Milk Fever cases in dairy herds. 

---

## What is Milk Fever in Dairy Cows?

Milk Fever in Dairy Cows, also called parturient paresis or post‑parturient hypocalcemia, is a disease of adult cows characterized by abnormally low calcium levels in the blood around calving.  Calcium is vital for normal function of nerves and muscles, so when blood calcium drops too far, cows develop weakness, inability to stand, and potentially life‑threatening complications.  

The problem occurs because at calving, the cow’s body suddenly needs much more calcium to produce colostrum and milk. During the dry period, cows typically need to replace about 30 g of calcium per day, but once lactation starts, mammary glands may drain more than 50 g of calcium per day.  This rapid increase in demand is more than the circulating calcium pool in the bloodstream, so the cow’s body must quickly start mobilizing calcium from bone and increasing absorption from the intestine.  

There are two main forms of hypocalcemia related to Milk Fever in Dairy Cows:

- **Clinical milk fever (overt hypocalcemia)** – cows show obvious signs such as inability to stand, cold ears, and depression. 
- **Subclinical hypocalcemia** – blood calcium is low, but the cow appears almost normal. This form is extremely common and is linked to reduced feed intake, lower milk yield, and increased risk of other fresh cow diseases.  

Both clinical and subclinical forms are economically important. Clinical milk fever has reported herd incidence around 5–8%, while subclinical hypocalcemia may affect 50–60% or more of older dairy cows at calving in some herds.

## Causes of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows





![Causes of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGRpc2Vhc2V8ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk1NzE0OHww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*


The primary cause of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows is the **sudden, dramatic increase in calcium demand at the onset of lactation**. A high‑producing cow may secrete 20–30 g of calcium in colostrum on the first day alone, which is several times the amount normally circulating in her blood.  If her calcium-regulating systems are not primed, blood calcium rapidly falls.

Key underlying causes and risk factors include:

1. **Delayed or inadequate activation of calcium mobilization mechanisms**  
   Calcium balance is controlled mainly by parathyroid hormone (PTH) and vitamin D. These hormones mobilize calcium from bone and increase intestinal absorption. In cows with Milk Fever, tissues often respond poorly to PTH, so they cannot mobilize calcium fast enough when demand increases.  High dietary calcium or high potassium diets in the dry period can blunt this response and increase risk.  

2. **Nutritional factors in the dry period**  
   - High‑calcium dry cow diets can make the cow “lazy” at maintaining calcium balance; her body does not keep the calcium mobilization system active. When calving occurs, she is unprepared. 
   - High‑potassium forages (often lush, heavily manured grass or certain silages) can produce a metabolic alkalosis that reduces the effectiveness of PTH and vitamin D. 
   - Incorrect dietary cation–anion difference (DCAD) in the close‑up ration strongly influences the risk of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows. Acidifying diets (negative DCAD) improve calcium mobilization, while alkaline diets increase risk.  

3. **Animal‑related risk factors**  
   - **Age and lactation number:** Cows in second and later lactations are far more susceptible than first‑calf heifers, as bone turnover is slower and milk yield is higher. 
   - **Breed:** Some dairy breeds such as Jersey and Guernsey are reported to be more prone to hypocalcemia than others. 
   - **High genetic merit for milk production:** Modern “top‑athlete” dairy cows with very high milk yields face particularly intense calcium demands, increasing the risk of both clinical and subclinical milk fever.  

4. **Management and environmental factors**  
   - Sudden diet changes, stress, overcrowding, and poor transition cow facilities can all reduce feed intake pre‑calving and impair the cow’s ability to adapt metabolically. 
   - Cows that are over‑conditioned (fat) at calving are at higher risk for a range of transition diseases, including Milk Fever in Dairy Cows.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows





![Symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1580570598977-4b2412d01bbc?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMHNpY2slMjBhbmltYWx8ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk1NzE0OHww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*


Clinical signs of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows typically follow a progression of stages. Because early detection greatly improves survival, it is important to recognize even mild symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows. 

**Stage 1 (Mild, standing)**  
In early hypocalcemia, cows may still be standing but show subtle changes:

- Restlessness, muscle tremors (especially over the shoulders and flanks)  
- Stiff gait, wobbling, or shifting weight  
- Hypersensitivity, ear twitching, teeth grinding  
- Reduced appetite and decreased rumen motility  

At this stage, signs can easily be missed or confused with other problems, yet timely treatment can prevent progression. 

**Stage 2 (Recumbent but alert)**  
If blood calcium falls further, the cow typically becomes unable to stand:

- Cow is down in sternal recumbency (“sitting up”) with the head often turned into the flank  
- Cold ears and extremities; cool skin temperature  
- Dry muzzle, dilated pupils, weak or rapid pulse  
- Reduced rumen contractions and little or no feces  
- Depression but still conscious and responsive  

This “classic” milk fever stage is where many cases are first noticed. Without treatment, most cows will die. 

**Stage 3 (Severe, life‑threatening)**  
In advanced cases:

- Cow is lying flat out (lateral recumbency) and unresponsive  
- Severely reduced or absent muscle tone; may show bloat due to lack of rumen movement  
- Very weak or irregular heartbeat; body temperature may be subnormal  
- Coma and death if calcium is not administered rapidly  

Without treatment, 60–80% of cows with clinical milk fever die, and mortality can approach 90% in some outbreaks.  

It is important to remember that **subclinical hypocalcemia** produces minimal obvious signs but still leads to reduced feed intake, poor muscle function, increased risk of displaced abomasum, retained placenta, metritis, mastitis, and decreased milk yield.  Monitoring production, appetite, and fresh cow health helps detect these hidden cases.

## How to Diagnose Milk Fever in Dairy Cows

Diagnosis of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows is usually based on history, clinical signs, and response to treatment, but laboratory tests can confirm hypocalcemia and help refine prevention strategies. 

**1. Clinical diagnosis on farm**

Veterinarians and experienced farmers often diagnose clinical milk fever by:

- Recent calving (most cases occur within 24 hours of calving; many others within 48 hours). 
- Typical signs: recumbency, cold ears, muscle weakness, reduced rumen activity as already described. 
- Rapid positive response to calcium therapy: many cows stand within 30 minutes after intravenous calcium administration, confirming the diagnosis.  

Because several diseases (such as calving paralysis, severe mastitis, toxic metritis, ketosis, or injuries) can also cause down cows, a careful examination by a veterinarian is important, especially if the cow does not respond to initial treatment. 

**2. Laboratory confirmation**

Blood tests measuring serum calcium concentrations can definitively show hypocalcemia. Clinical milk fever cases typically have blood calcium levels below about 8.0 mg/dL.  Blood sampling can also help identify underlying issues such as magnesium or phosphorus imbalances and guide herd‑level prevention.

For subclinical hypocalcemia, routine blood calcium testing in a sample of fresh cows (for example 2–10 cows within 24–48 hours after calving) can estimate herd prevalence.  A high rate of low calcium values indicates a herd‑level problem, even if few cows show classic clinical signs.

**3. Monitoring tools for prevention programs**

In herds using acidified close‑up diets, urine pH in pre‑fresh cows is often monitored to judge the effectiveness of the DCAD program. Appropriate urine pH ranges indicate that the cows are in mild metabolic acidosis and better prepared to mobilize calcium at calving.  This type of monitoring is more about prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows than diagnosis of individual cases.

## Treatment of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows





![Treatment for Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMHZldGVyaW5hcnl8ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk1NzE0OHww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*


Knowing how to treat Milk Fever in Dairy Cows quickly and correctly can save many animals and prevent complications. Treatment always centers on restoring blood calcium and supporting the cow until normal muscle and nerve function return. 

**1. Intravenous calcium therapy**

For recumbent (down) cows with clear signs of milk fever, **slow intravenous (IV) administration of calcium salts** is the standard life‑saving treatment:

- Commonly used products: 23% calcium borogluconate or combined “3‑in‑1” or “4‑in‑1” mineral solutions. 
- Typical adult cow dose: about 500 ml of a 23% calcium borogluconate solution (around 10–11 g of calcium), given slowly IV while listening to the heart to monitor for arrhythmias. 
- A practical guide is about 1 ml per kg body weight for 23% calcium borogluconate in periparturient hypocalcemia.  

Correct technique, including aseptic catheter placement and heart auscultation, is crucial. Rapid injection can cause dangerous cardiac disturbances. Calcium chloride solutions raise ionized calcium more quickly but are more likely to cause arrhythmias and are used cautiously.  

Many cows stand up within 20–30 minutes after successful IV treatment. However, some may relapse within 12–24 hours and require repeat therapy. 

**2. Subcutaneous and oral calcium**

In less severe cases (standing cows with mild signs) or after initial IV therapy, **subcutaneous (under the skin) and oral calcium supplementation** help maintain blood levels:

- Subcutaneous administration of calcium solutions in multiple sites allows slower absorption and extended support, but care must be taken to avoid tissue damage and never inject calcium solutions intramuscularly. 
- Oral calcium gels, pastes, or boluses are often used immediately after calving and again 12–24 hours later in at‑risk cows to reduce relapse and support recovery.  

Cows must be alert and have a functioning swallow reflex before oral treatment is given to avoid choking. Down cows that do not respond to initial IV treatment should not be drenched with boluses until a veterinarian reassesses them. 

**3. Supportive care**

Along with calcium, supportive care is vital:

- Make sure the cow is on a dry, comfortable, non‑slippery surface with good bedding to prevent muscle and nerve damage.  
- Turn recumbent cows regularly and pull them into sternal position to reduce risk of bloat and aspiration. 
- Provide easy access to fresh water and palatable feed once the cow is conscious and swallowing.  
- Treat associated conditions such as mastitis, metritis, or injuries as directed by the veterinarian.  

Cows that remain down for long periods may develop secondary nerve damage, muscle injury, pressure sores, and may never rise even after calcium levels normalize. Early treatment and good nursing are essential to avoid these complications.

## Prevention and Control of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows





![Prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGZhcm0lMjBwcmV2ZW50aW9ufGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NTcxNDl8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*


Prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows is far better and more economical than treatment. Modern dairy management focuses strongly on nutritional and management strategies during the dry and transition periods to support calcium metabolism. 

**1. Close‑up ration formulation and DCAD management**

One of the most effective tools is adjusting the dietary cation–anion difference (DCAD) of the close‑up dry cow ration:

- Feeding a **slightly acidifying (negative DCAD) diet** in the last 2–3 weeks before calving promotes a mild metabolic acidosis. This improves the action of PTH and vitamin D, boosting bone calcium mobilization and intestinal absorption. 
- Anionic salts (such as chloride and sulfate salts of calcium, magnesium, or ammonium) are often used to drive DCAD negative, but they must be balanced carefully to maintain palatability and avoid excessive acidosis. 
- Urine pH (e.g., target ranges around 6.0–6.5 for Holsteins on anionic diets) is monitored to ensure the diet is working effectively. 

**2. Control of dietary calcium and potassium pre‑calving**

Two broad nutritional strategies exist:

- **Low‑calcium diet strategy:** Feeding very low dietary calcium during the dry period keeps the cow’s calcium regulatory system active so it is ready at calving. 
- **Acidifying (anionic) diet strategy:** As described above, using negative DCAD diets even when calcium intake is moderate, to enhance calcium metabolism.  

In all cases, high potassium forages (such as intensively fertilized grass) should be avoided in close‑up diets because they increase metabolic alkalosis and interfere with PTH. Forage mineral testing helps select materials with low potassium content for dry cows. 

**3. Adequate magnesium and overall mineral balance**

Magnesium is essential for PTH release and function. Low magnesium status can worsen Milk Fever risk. Well‑formulated close‑up rations always include adequate magnesium along with correct calcium and phosphorus levels. 

**4. Targeted oral calcium supplementation around calving**

Oral calcium gels, pastes, or boluses given at calving and again 12–24 hours later to high‑risk cows (older cows, history of milk fever, high producers) can significantly reduce clinical cases.  This is particularly useful in herds where nutritional programs are still being optimized.

**5. Overall transition cow management**

Good prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows also depends on:

- Comfortable, uncrowded close‑up and fresh cow housing  
- Stable, palatable rations with minimal sudden changes  
- Body condition management to avoid overly fat cows  
- Prompt attention to calving problems and post‑calving diseases  

These measures support feed intake and metabolic health, reducing not only milk fever but also related disorders such as ketosis, displaced abomasum, retained placenta, metritis, and mastitis.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Milk Fever in Dairy Cows

### Q: What is Milk Fever in Dairy Cows?

**A:** Milk Fever in Dairy Cows is a metabolic disease caused by low blood calcium (hypocalcemia) that occurs most commonly around calving. It leads to muscle weakness, inability to stand, and, without treatment, a high risk of death. It is also called parturient paresis or post‑parturient hypocalcemia. 

### Q: What are the main symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows?

**A:** Early symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows include restlessness, muscle tremors, stiff gait, and reduced appetite. As it progresses, cows become unable to stand, with cold ears, weak pulse, and decreased rumen movement. In severe cases, cows lie flat, become comatose, and may die if not treated. 

### Q: How do you treat Milk Fever in Dairy Cows?

**A:** Treatment centers on giving calcium, usually as intravenous calcium borogluconate given slowly while monitoring the heart. Subcutaneous and oral calcium products are often used as follow‑up. Supportive care, including good bedding, frequent turning, and prompt treatment of any secondary problems, is also vital. Many cows stand within 30 minutes of correct IV treatment. 

### Q: Can Milk Fever in Dairy Cows be prevented?

**A:** Yes. Prevention focuses on correct nutrition and management of dry and close‑up cows. Key strategies include using low‑calcium or acidified (negative DCAD) diets before calving, avoiding high‑potassium forages, ensuring adequate magnesium, and giving oral calcium supplements to high‑risk cows at calving. Good transition cow housing and stress reduction are also important. 

### Q: When does Milk Fever usually occur?

**A:** Most cases of clinical Milk Fever in Dairy Cows occur within 24 hours after calving, and many of the remaining cases appear within the next 48 hours. However, mild hypocalcemia can begin shortly before calving and persist for several days after, especially in subclinical form. 

### Q: Which cows are at highest risk of Milk Fever?

**A:** Older cows (second lactation and above), high‑producing animals, and some dairy breeds such as Jerseys are at greatest risk. Cows with a previous history of milk fever, those that are over‑conditioned, and cows on high‑potassium or poorly balanced dry‑cow diets are also more likely to be affected.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Veterinary involvement is critical in managing Milk Fever in Dairy Cows. Although some farmers routinely treat typical cases, calling a veterinarian is strongly recommended in several situations:

- **Cows that do not stand within 1–2 hours after calcium treatment.** Failure to respond suggests either inadequate therapy, relapse, or an alternative diagnosis such as nerve damage, fractures, or severe systemic disease. A veterinarian can reassess, give further treatment, and investigate other causes. 

- **Cows with unclear diagnosis or atypical signs.** Not every down cow has milk fever. Conditions like calving paralysis, severe mastitis, toxic metritis, or metabolic diseases can look similar. A veterinarian can perform a full examination, including blood tests if necessary, to ensure the correct treatment plan. 

- **Herds with repeated or high incidence of Milk Fever.** If many cows are affected, or if there is a pattern of subclinical hypocalcemia with related diseases (retained placenta, displaced abomasum, ketosis), a veterinarian and nutritionist should review rations, forage mineral content, and transition management. Adjustments to DCAD, calcium and magnesium levels, and oral calcium protocols can then be made. 

- **Cows with severe complications.** Recumbent cows for more than 12–24 hours are at high risk of muscle damage, nerve injury, pressure sores, and secondary infections. Veterinary guidance on intensive nursing, pain relief, and prognosis helps protect animal welfare and economic outcomes.  

Regular veterinary input into transition cow management programs can dramatically reduce the frequency and severity of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows and improve overall herd health and profitability.

## Conclusion

Milk Fever in Dairy Cows is a common, serious, yet largely preventable metabolic disease linked to low blood calcium at the onset of lactation. The sudden increase in calcium demand for colostrum and milk production can overwhelm the cow’s ability to maintain blood calcium if her diet and metabolism are not properly prepared.  

Understanding the causes of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows, recognizing early symptoms, and knowing how to diagnose and treat affected animals are crucial skills for farmers, students, and anyone working with dairy cattle. Prompt intravenous calcium therapy, supported by subcutaneous or oral calcium and good nursing care, can save many cows and prevent long‑term complications.  

However, the greatest impact comes from prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows through well‑designed dry cow and transition programs. Careful control of dietary calcium and potassium, use of acidified (negative DCAD) diets when appropriate, adequate magnesium, targeted oral calcium supplementation, and strong overall transition management can significantly reduce both clinical and subclinical hypocalcemia.  

By working closely with veterinarians and nutritionists, dairy producers can protect their cows from Milk Fever, improve fresh cow health, and enhance the productivity and profitability of the entire herd.
