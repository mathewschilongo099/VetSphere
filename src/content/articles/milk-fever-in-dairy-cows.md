# Milk Fever in Dairy Cows

## Introduction



![Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ4MzA3fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Milk Fever in Dairy Cows — via Unsplash*


Milk fever, also called parturient paresis or periparturient hypocalcemia, is a common metabolic disease of dairy cows that occurs around the time of calving. It is caused by a sudden drop in blood calcium levels when milk production starts, and the cow cannot quickly mobilize enough calcium from her bones and diet. This leads to weakness, muscle paralysis, and, if not treated promptly, death. It is most often seen in mature, high‑producing dairy cows, especially in their third or later lactations and in certain breeds such as Jersey and Guernsey.  

Although only about 5–10% of cows in a herd may show obvious (clinical) milk fever each year, a much larger proportion can have subclinical hypocalcemia, where blood calcium is low but signs are mild or hidden. These cows are more likely to suffer from other fresh‑cow diseases such as retained placenta, metritis, displaced abomasum, ketosis, and mastitis. This means milk fever is not only a welfare concern, but also a major cause of economic loss due to treatment costs, deaths, culling, and reduced milk yield and fertility.  

Understanding milk fever is essential for farmers, veterinary students, and advisers. Early recognition and correct treatment save lives, and good prevention programs greatly reduce the number of affected cows. This article explains the causes, signs, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and when to involve a veterinarian, in clear practical language.

## Causes



![Causes of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxsaXZlc3RvY2slMjBkaXNlYXNlJTIwY2F0dGxlfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NDgzMDd8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*


Milk fever is fundamentally a problem of calcium balance. During the dry period (late pregnancy, not lactating), a cow’s daily requirement for calcium is modest, mainly for fetal growth and normal body losses. At calving, when lactation begins, calcium demand rises sharply because large amounts of calcium are drawn into colostrum and milk. Mammary “drain” of calcium can exceed 50 g per day at the onset of lactation, which is several times higher than during the dry period. If the cow’s body cannot increase calcium absorption from the gut and calcium release from bones quickly enough, blood calcium falls and hypocalcemia develops.  

The hormones parathyroid hormone (PTH) and the active form of vitamin D play central roles in maintaining normal blood calcium. Around calving, tissues in some cows are less responsive to PTH, and this delayed response contributes to the development of milk fever. Diets fed before calving can strongly influence this hormonal system. High‑calcium or high‑potassium dry‑cow diets (for example, lush, heavily fertilized grass or alfalfa) can suppress the cow’s natural calcium‑mobilizing mechanisms and make it harder for her to respond when demand suddenly increases at calving.  

Several risk factors further increase the chance of milk fever. Age and production level are important: older cows and high‑yielding cows are at greater risk because their calcium demand is higher and their bone and hormonal systems respond more slowly. Jerseys and Guernseys are more commonly affected than Holsteins. Cows that have had milk fever before are much more likely to get it again. Cows carrying twins, over‑fat cows, and those experiencing difficult calvings or other stressors are also at higher risk. Suboptimal magnesium intake can worsen the situation because low magnesium interferes with PTH action and calcium regulation. 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms



![Symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1700665537604-412e89a285c3?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxzaWNrJTIwYW5pbWFsJTIwdmV0ZXJpbmFyeXxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ4MzA3fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*


Clinical signs of milk fever are usually divided into three stages, reflecting how low the blood calcium has become and how long the condition has been present. Early (Stage I) signs are often mild and easily missed: the cow may be restless, anxious, or hypersensitive to sounds and touch. Fine muscle tremors, especially over the shoulders and flanks, may be seen. The cow may shift weight from one leg to another, show a stiff gait, or have a slightly elevated heart rate. If treatment is given at this stage, progression can often be halted quickly.  

As blood calcium falls further, Stage II develops. The cow becomes weak and goes down into sternal recumbency (sitting on her chest with legs folded under her). She is usually dull but still conscious, with a dry muzzle, cold ears and extremities, and reduced body temperature. Her heart rate is fast but the pulse is weak. She often has decreased gut movement, leading to dry feces or constipation, and she may not pass urine. The cow cannot stand but can usually hold her head up, although she may tuck her head into her flank. Without treatment, this stage can progress within hours.  

In Stage III, the condition is severe and life‑threatening. The cow lies flat on her side, is very depressed or comatose, and cannot maintain her head. Muscles are very flaccid (soft and weak). The heart rate is very fast and may become irregular due to the effects of low calcium on cardiac muscle. Body temperature may fall further, and bloating and severe gut stasis are common. If treatment is delayed at this point, the cow may die within a few hours from circulatory collapse or secondary complications such as aspiration pneumonia. 

## Diagnosis

In most farm situations, diagnosis of milk fever is based on history and clinical signs. The typical case is a mature dairy cow within 0–72 hours after calving that is unable to rise, with cold ears, a weak pulse, and flaccid paralysis, often after a normal or difficult calving. The timing around parturition, age, production level, and herd history all support the diagnosis. A good clinical examination, including heart and lung auscultation, temperature, and checking for bloat or uterine problems, is essential to rule out other causes of recumbency such as calving injuries, severe mastitis, or toxic conditions.  

Definitive diagnosis is made by measuring blood calcium concentration, where clinical milk fever is usually associated with total calcium less than about 7.5 mg/dL (1.88 mmol/L), and often much lower. However, this test is not always available on farm, and treatment should never be delayed while waiting for results in a typical case. Blood tests may also show low phosphorus and sometimes low magnesium, which can help guide treatment, especially in cows that fail to respond to standard calcium therapy.  

At the herd level, monitoring blood calcium in a sample of fresh cows can reveal how common subclinical hypocalcemia is in the herd. Regular assessment of the frequency of clinical milk fever, retained placenta, displaced abomasum, and other fresh‑cow diseases helps evaluate the success of prevention programs. In herds using anionic (negative DCAD) diets before calving, urine pH testing in close‑up cows can be used to check whether the diet is effectively acidifying the cows and promoting better calcium metabolism. 

## Treatment



![Treatment for Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1725409796872-8b41e8eca929?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHx2ZXRlcmluYXJpYW4lMjB0cmVhdGluZyUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ4MzA3fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*


Milk fever is an emergency that requires rapid treatment with calcium. The standard therapy for a recumbent cow with typical signs is slow intravenous administration of calcium salts, most commonly calcium borogluconate. A typical adult dairy cow receives about 500 mL of a 23–25% calcium borogluconate solution, which provides around 10–11 g of elemental calcium. This is usually sufficient to correct the immediate calcium deficit and restore neuromuscular function. The solution must be given slowly, with careful monitoring of the heart using a stethoscope because too rapid administration can cause dangerous arrhythmias.  

In milder cases, or after initial intravenous therapy, calcium can also be given subcutaneously in several sites, using clean technique and avoiding large single deposits that could cause tissue irritation. Oral calcium gels, boluses, or drenches are very useful in standing cows with early signs and as follow‑up treatment after an intravenous dose to maintain calcium levels as milk production continues. However, oral products must not be given to cows that are unconscious or unable to swallow properly due to the risk of aspiration and inhalation pneumonia.  

A properly treated cow with uncomplicated milk fever often shows improvement within minutes: muscle tremors decrease, the pulse becomes stronger and slower, and the cow may attempt to stand within 1–2 hours. If the cow does not rise after appropriate treatment, other problems (such as nerve damage, fractures, severe mastitis, or toxic metritis) should be suspected. Good nursing care is vital: provide deep, non‑slippery bedding, turn the cow regularly to prevent pressure sores and bloat, offer fresh water and good feed, and protect her from cold or heat stress. Prompt veterinary involvement is recommended if the response is poor or if the diagnosis is uncertain. 

## Prevention and Control



![Prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1454179083322-198bb4daae41?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxmYXJtJTIwYW5pbWFsJTIwcHJldmVudGlvbiUyMGJpb3NlY3VyaXR5fGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NDgzMDd8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*


Effective prevention starts with correct dry‑cow nutrition. The aim is to prepare the cow’s body to mobilize calcium efficiently at calving. Controlling dietary cation‑anion difference (DCAD) by feeding anionic salts before calving is a widely used and effective strategy. Diets relatively higher in chloride and sulfur and lower in sodium and potassium create a mild metabolic acidosis that improves the response of tissues to PTH and increases calcium release from bone and absorption from the gut. Monitoring urine pH in close‑up cows helps fine‑tune these diets and ensure they are working as intended.  

Avoiding high‑calcium and high‑potassium forages in the close‑up ration is also important. Lush, heavily fertilized grass and some legumes can contain large amounts of potassium and calcium, which interfere with the adaptation process. Instead, rations should be based on moderate‑potassium forages with carefully balanced minerals. Adequate magnesium supply in the pre‑calving diet is essential because magnesium is required for PTH function and calcium homeostasis. Body condition should be managed so cows are neither too thin nor over‑fat at calving, as over‑conditioned cows are at higher risk of metabolic diseases, including milk fever.  

Targeted oral calcium supplementation at calving can reduce the risk of clinical and subclinical hypocalcemia, especially in high‑risk cows (older, high‑yielding, Jersey, previous milk fever cases, twin pregnancies). Common programs include giving an oral calcium bolus or gel at calving and again 12–24 hours later. Herd‑level monitoring of clinical cases, fresh‑cow diseases, and culling patterns helps evaluate whether the prevention program is effective and where adjustments are needed. Long‑term, breeding for robust cows with lower susceptibility to metabolic disorders (without sacrificing production and fertility) can also contribute to improved herd health. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Veterinary assistance should be sought immediately when a cow is unable to stand around the time of calving, especially if she has cold ears, a weak pulse, or appears dull and weak. Rapid assessment and intravenous calcium treatment can be life‑saving, and the veterinarian can also check for other complications such as uterine tears, internal bleeding, mastitis, or nerve injuries that may be present alongside milk fever. Delays in treatment greatly increase the risk of death and long‑term complications.  

A veterinarian should also be called when the response to initial treatment is poor or incomplete. If a cow does not attempt to stand within a few hours of appropriate calcium therapy, or if she relapses and goes down again, additional investigation is needed. Blood tests may be required to check calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium levels, as well as to assess for other metabolic problems. The veterinarian can adjust the treatment plan, for example by adding phosphorus or magnesium, giving additional calcium in a safe manner, or treating concurrent diseases that are preventing recovery.  

Beyond emergency care, veterinarians play a central role in designing and reviewing prevention programs. They can help evaluate rations (including DCAD and mineral balance), interpret herd monitoring data on milk fever and related disorders, and advise on practical measures such as urine pH testing and targeted calcium supplementation. Regular herd health visits focused on the transition period can significantly reduce the incidence and impact of milk fever, improving both cow welfare and farm profitability. 

## Conclusion

Milk fever is a common and important disease of dairy cows that occurs mainly around calving due to a sudden rise in calcium demand and failure of the cow’s systems to maintain normal blood calcium. It typically affects mature, high‑producing cows and leads to weakness, recumbency, and, without prompt treatment, death. Even when obvious cases are few, subclinical hypocalcemia and its associated disorders can cause considerable hidden losses in health, production, and fertility.  

Successful management of milk fever requires a combination of rapid recognition and treatment of individual cases and well‑planned herd‑level prevention. Intravenous and oral calcium preparations, given correctly and safely, can reverse clinical signs quickly. However, the greatest benefits come from prevention through sound dry‑cow nutrition, appropriate use of anionic diets and magnesium, careful forage selection, and targeted oral calcium at calving for high‑risk cows. Regular veterinary involvement in both emergency care and herd‑health planning is essential.  

By understanding the causes, clinical signs, and risk factors, and by implementing appropriate prevention and treatment strategies, farmers and animal health professionals can greatly reduce the impact of milk fever on dairy herds. This leads to healthier cows, better welfare, improved milk production, and more sustainable, profitable dairy farming.
