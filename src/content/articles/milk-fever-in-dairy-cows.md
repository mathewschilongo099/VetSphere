# Milk Fever in Dairy Cows

## Introduction



![Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ3NDQxfDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Milk Fever in Dairy Cows — via Unsplash*


Milk fever, also called hypocalcemia or parturient paresis, is a common metabolic disease of dairy cows that happens around calving. It occurs when there is not enough calcium in the blood to meet the sudden demand for milk production. This low blood calcium affects the nerves and muscles of the cow and can quickly lead to weakness, collapse, and death if not treated.  

Milk fever is most often seen in high‑producing dairy cows, especially in older cows entering their third or later lactations. In many herds, about 5–10% of cows get clinical milk fever each year, while up to half of all fresh cows may have subclinical (hidden) low calcium without obvious signs.  Even when cows do not show clear signs, subclinical milk fever can increase the risk of other fresh‑cow diseases and reduce milk yield and fertility.  

Because milk fever is common, serious, and closely linked with many other calving problems, every dairy should understand how it develops, how to recognize it early, and how to prevent it through good nutrition and management. Proper control of milk fever improves cow welfare, reduces deaths and culling, and increases farm profitability. 

## Causes



![Causes of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGFuaW1hbCUyMGRpc2Vhc2UlMjBjYXVzZXxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ3NDQxfDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*


The root cause of milk fever is a sudden drop in blood calcium at or just after calving. Normal blood calcium is tightly controlled by the cow’s body using the intestines (absorption from feed), the bones (release and storage), and the kidneys (loss in urine). When calving and milk production begin, the calcium requirement jumps sharply because large amounts of calcium are secreted into colostrum and milk. If the body cannot quickly increase calcium supply, blood levels fall and milk fever develops.  

Older, high‑producing cows are at greatest risk because their milk production is higher and their bone and hormonal systems respond more slowly than those of first‑calf heifers. Certain breeds, such as Jersey and Guernsey cows, have a higher incidence of milk fever than some other breeds.  Cows that have had milk fever in previous lactations, or daughters of affected cows, are also at higher risk and should be managed carefully around calving.  

Nutrition and mineral balance before calving are major contributing factors. Pre‑fresh diets that are high in calcium, potassium, and sodium can suppress the cow’s normal system for mobilizing calcium from bone and absorbing it from the gut. When calving suddenly increases demand, her body is not prepared. Feeding a properly formulated close‑up diet with controlled calcium and a suitable dietary cation–anion difference (DCAD) helps prepare the cow’s metabolism and reduces the risk of hypocalcemia. 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms



![Symptoms of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1580570598977-4b2412d01bbc?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMHNpY2slMjBhbmltYWwlMjBzeW1wdG9tc3xlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTQ3NDQxfDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*


Clinical signs of milk fever are usually divided into three stages based on severity. Early (Stage 1) signs may be subtle and easily missed. The cow may be restless or slightly excited, shuffle her feet, tremble in the muscles of the head and limbs, or show a wobbly, unsteady gait. She may stop eating and show reduced rumen movement. At this stage, she is still standing, and prompt treatment can prevent progression.  

In Stage 2, the cow is down and cannot stand. Typically, she lies in a sternal position (on her chest) with her head turned toward her flank. Her ears, muzzle, and legs feel cold, and her body temperature may be slightly low. The heart rate is fast but weak because the heart muscle is also affected by low calcium. The cow is dull, cannot rise, and has dry feces or may stop defecating and urinating due to paralysis of the gut and bladder muscles, which can also lead to bloat.  

If not treated, the condition may progress to Stage 3, where the cow lies flat on her side, is very depressed or comatose, and may be bloating severely. Muscle tone is lost, including in the tongue, and the cow often cannot swallow. Death can occur within hours from shock, heart failure, or complications such as bloat or aspiration pneumonia. Even cows with early signs can deteriorate quickly, so timely recognition and treatment are critical. 

## Diagnosis

On most farms, diagnosis of milk fever is based on history and clinical signs. The disease typically appears from just before calving to about three days after calving, when milk production starts. A fresh, older dairy cow that is weak, wobbly, or recumbent with cold ears and a characteristic posture (lying on the chest with the head turned into the flank) is very likely to be suffering from milk fever.  

Veterinarians may confirm the diagnosis by measuring blood calcium levels. Clinical milk fever usually occurs when blood calcium drops below about 5 mg/dL, while normal levels are approximately 8.5–10 mg/dL.  Laboratory tests can also check phosphorus and magnesium levels, as these minerals may be abnormal at the same time and can influence the cow’s response to treatment. In some cases, low magnesium (hypomagnesemia) may cause signs similar to or overlapping with milk fever.  

On a herd level, monitoring can help identify subclinical hypocalcemia and evaluate prevention programs. Testing blood calcium in a group of fresh cows, measuring urine pH in close‑up cows on anionic diets, and analyzing mineral content of dry‑cow and pre‑fresh diets are all useful tools. These methods help veterinarians and nutritionists fine‑tune rations and management to reduce the incidence of both clinical and subclinical milk fever. 

## Treatment



![Treatment for Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1596733430284-f7437764b1a9?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMHZldGVyaW5hcnklMjB0cmVhdG1lbnR8ZW58MHwwfHx8MTc4MTk0NzQ0MXww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*


Milk fever is an emergency that requires rapid calcium replacement. The standard treatment for recumbent cows is slow intravenous administration of a calcium solution, usually calcium borogluconate. A common dose is around 500 ml of 23% calcium borogluconate, given by slow IV injection while carefully listening to the heart to avoid dangerous arrhythmias. This provides roughly 10 g of calcium, which is enough to correct the deficit and support ongoing milk losses.  

In milder cases or after IV treatment, additional calcium may be given under the skin (subcutaneously) or orally as a calcium bolus or drench. These routes maintain blood calcium levels after the initial rapid correction from IV injection. A general rule is to give about 1 ml of 23% calcium borogluconate per kg body weight, adjusting according to the veterinarian’s protocol and the cow’s condition.  The cow should be kept sitting up (sternal) if possible, bedded comfortably, and turned regularly to prevent muscle and nerve damage.

Supportive care is also important. Cows may need help to stand using hip lifters or a float tank once they have regained enough strength, but lifting should be gentle and supervised to avoid injury. Providing warm, dry bedding, protecting the cow from cold or heat, preventing bloat, and offering fresh water and palatable feed as soon as she can swallow all improve recovery. Some cases require repeat calcium treatments or additional therapy for complications such as metritis, mastitis, or displaced abomasum, which are more common after milk fever. 

## Prevention and Control



![Prevention of Milk Fever in Dairy Cows](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1570042225831-d98fa7577f1e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxNaWxrJTIwRmV2ZXIlMjBpbiUyMERhaXJ5JTIwQ293cyUyMGFuaW1hbCUyMGZhcm0lMjBwcmV2ZW50aW9ufGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NDc0NDF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*


Effective prevention begins in the dry period, particularly in the last three weeks before calving. The goal is to prepare the cow’s body to mobilize calcium rapidly when lactation starts. One widely used approach is feeding a low‑DCAD (slightly acidifying) diet, using anionic salts and carefully controlling potassium and sodium levels. This mild metabolic acidosis improves the response to parathyroid hormone, increases intestinal calcium absorption, and promotes release of calcium from bone when needed.  

Close‑up diets should avoid excessive calcium, especially in the weeks before calving. Keeping pre‑fresh calcium intake moderate encourages the cow’s regulatory systems to stay active. At the same time, adequate magnesium must be supplied, because magnesium is needed for proper function of the hormones that control calcium balance. Forages and concentrates should be tested for mineral content, and nutritionists should adjust the ration accordingly, with special attention to high‑potassium forages that increase milk fever risk.  

On many farms, oral calcium supplements are given around calving to high‑risk cows, such as older animals, Jerseys, or cows with a past history of milk fever. These boluses or drenches, usually given shortly after calving and again 12–24 hours later, can help reduce the incidence of clinical disease when used as part of a broader prevention plan developed with a veterinarian and nutritionist. Herd‑level monitoring of clinical cases, subclinical hypocalcemia, and related fresh‑cow diseases helps assess how well the prevention program is working and guides adjustments over time. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

A veterinarian should be called immediately whenever a cow around calving is unable to stand, shows signs of severe weakness, or has the typical posture and signs of milk fever. Rapid IV calcium treatment can be life‑saving, and delay can lead to death or long‑term complications. Stage 2 and Stage 3 cows are emergencies that require professional treatment and careful monitoring during and after calcium administration.  

Veterinary input is also important for cows that do not respond quickly to treatment, relapse after initial improvement, or remain down for more than a few hours. Such “downer cows” may have muscle or nerve damage, fractures, or other diseases that need additional diagnosis and care. A veterinarian can also identify and manage concurrent problems such as metritis, mastitis, displaced abomasum, or ketosis, which are more common in cows that have had hypocalcemia.  

Beyond individual cases, farmers should work with a herd veterinarian to develop written protocols for recognizing and treating milk fever, training staff, and monitoring outcomes. Regular review of dry‑cow and transition‑cow nutrition, housing, and overall management with both the veterinarian and nutritionist is essential to keep milk fever and related metabolic disorders under good control. 

## Conclusion

Milk fever is a major metabolic disease of dairy cows that arises from a sudden fall in blood calcium at calving, especially in older, high‑producing animals. It causes muscle weakness, recumbency, and, without quick treatment, can lead to death or serious complications. Subclinical forms, although less obvious, are even more widespread and increase the risk of many other transition‑cow diseases, making hypocalcemia a key “gateway” disorder.  

Accurate recognition of early clinical signs, prompt calcium treatment, and good supportive care greatly improve survival and recovery. However, long‑term success on dairy farms depends mainly on prevention. Well‑designed dry‑cow and close‑up diets, attention to DCAD and mineral balance, targeted use of oral calcium in high‑risk cows, and regular monitoring of herd performance all play essential roles in controlling milk fever.  

By working closely with veterinarians and nutritionists, farmers can reduce the incidence of both clinical and subclinical hypocalcemia, improve cow health and welfare, and enhance the productivity and profitability of the herd. Understanding milk fever and applying sound management around calving is therefore one of the most important responsibilities in modern dairy production.
