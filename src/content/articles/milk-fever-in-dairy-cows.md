Milk fever in dairy cows is a serious metabolic disorder that can develop around calving. Despite its name, milk fever is not an infectious disease and usually does not cause a high body temperature. It is another name for **hypocalcemia**, a condition in which the cow’s blood calcium level falls too low to support normal muscle, nerve, and digestive function.

The risk is highest during the first day after calving, although cases may occur up to two or three days postpartum. The sudden start of colostrum and milk production creates a sharp increase in the cow’s calcium requirements—estimated at about four times the dry-period demand. If calcium absorption and bone mobilization cannot keep pace, the cow may become weak, go down, and eventually die without prompt treatment.  

Understanding the causes, recognizing early milk fever symptoms, and using a well-planned dry cow nutrition program are essential for protecting your herd.

## What Is Milk Fever in Dairy Cows?

Milk fever is clinical hypocalcemia associated with calving and the beginning of lactation. Calcium is needed for:

- Muscle contraction
- Nerve impulses
- Heart function
- Rumen and intestinal movement
- Uterine contractions
- Normal immune function
- Milk and colostrum production

During the dry period, a cow’s calcium requirement is relatively low. After calving, the mammary gland rapidly removes calcium from the bloodstream to produce colostrum and milk. The cow must respond by absorbing more calcium from the intestine and releasing stored calcium from her bones.

Milk fever occurs when these natural adjustments are too slow or inadequate. The result is low blood calcium, reduced muscle function, weakness, and, in severe cases, paralysis and collapse.

The word “fever” can be misleading. Affected cows often have a normal or below-normal temperature, with cold ears and a dry muzzle being common warning signs. 

## When Does Milk Fever Occur?

Most milk fever cases occur:

- During calving
- Within the first 24 hours after calving
- During the first 48 to 72 hours of lactation

The condition is more common in mature cows entering their second or later lactation. Older cows generally have a greater risk because their ability to rapidly mobilize calcium may become less efficient with each lactation.

Jersey cows and other Channel Island breeds are also considered higher-risk animals. One meta-analysis found that Jersey cows had approximately 2.25 times the risk of milk fever compared with Holsteins. 

## Clinical and Subclinical Milk Fever

Milk fever may be **clinical** or **subclinical**.

### Clinical milk fever

Clinical milk fever causes visible signs such as weakness, trembling, cold extremities, difficulty standing, recumbency, and loss of consciousness. This is an emergency requiring immediate veterinary attention.

### Subclinical hypocalcemia

Subclinical hypocalcemia occurs when blood calcium is low but the cow does not show obvious signs. It may be more widespread than clinical milk fever. Some sources report subclinical hypocalcemia in a large proportion of mature dairy cows, even in herds with relatively few visible milk fever cases.  

A cow with subclinical hypocalcemia may:

- Eat less than expected
- Spend more time lying down
- Have reduced rumen activity
- Produce less milk
- Be more vulnerable to ketosis
- Face a higher risk of displaced abomasum
- Develop metritis or retained placenta more easily

Because the signs are subtle, subclinical cases are often identified through herd-level monitoring, blood testing, or an investigation of increased transition-cow disease.

## Milk Fever Symptoms: The Three Stages

Milk fever typically progresses through three stages. Early recognition gives you the best chance of preventing a cow from becoming recumbent.

### Stage 1: Early or standing milk fever

The cow may still be standing, but her behavior and movement change. Look for:

- Restlessness or nervousness
- Reduced appetite
- Mild muscle tremors
- Stiffness
- Shuffling of the hind feet
- Weight shifting
- Weakness in the hind legs
- Increased sensitivity to touch or sound
- A wobbly or uncoordinated gait
- Cold ears
- A dry muzzle

Stage 1 can be easy to miss, especially when staff are not checking freshly calved cows several times per day. Treat unusual weakness or a change in attitude as a warning, particularly when it occurs shortly after calving.

### Stage 2: The cow is down but remains upright

As blood calcium falls further, the cow may lie on her chest with her legs tucked underneath. She may hold her head against her flank or neck.

Common signs include:

- Dullness and lethargy
- Muscle trembling
- Cold ears and limbs
- Rapid heartbeat
- Reduced or absent rumen contractions
- Constipation
- A lowered body temperature
- Inability to rise

This stage is an emergency. The cow should be treated according to a veterinarian-approved milk fever protocol.

### Stage 3: Severe milk fever

In advanced cases, the cow lies flat on her side and may become unconscious or comatose. She may develop bloat because the rumen has stopped moving normally.

Stage 3 signs include:

- Complete inability to stand
- Loss of consciousness
- Severe muscle weakness
- Lateral recumbency
- Reduced reflexes
- Bloat
- Circulatory collapse
- Death if treatment is delayed

A cow lying flat on her side must be moved carefully into a chest-down, or sternal, position if possible. This helps reduce the risk of bloat, regurgitation, aspiration, and pressure-related muscle and nerve damage.

## What Causes Milk Fever?

Milk fever is caused by an imbalance between the cow’s calcium demand and her ability to supply calcium to the blood. Several factors may contribute.

### Sudden calcium loss through milk

The start of lactation places a major burden on calcium metabolism. Colostrum and milk contain substantial calcium, and the demand rises very quickly after calving.

### High-potassium dry cow diets

High-potassium forages can increase milk fever risk. Potassium-rich feeds may create a more alkaline metabolic state, which reduces the cow’s responsiveness to parathyroid hormone. That hormone is important for mobilizing calcium from bone and improving calcium absorption.  

Potentially high-potassium feeds include:

- Heavily fertilized grass
- Manure-fertilized forage
- Some legume hays
- Lush pasture
- Forages grown on soils with high potassium levels

Do not judge a forage only by appearance. Forage and total-ration mineral analysis is essential.

### Inadequate magnesium

Magnesium supports both calcium absorption and calcium mobilization. A magnesium deficiency can make it more difficult for a cow to maintain normal blood calcium around calving. Pre-calving magnesium supplementation is therefore an important part of many milk fever prevention programs. 

### Excessive calcium before calving

Feeding too much calcium during the dry period may reduce the cow’s need to mobilize calcium from her bones. If that mobilization system is not adequately prepared when lactation begins, the cow may struggle to respond to the sudden calcium demand.

However, reducing calcium without professional ration formulation can also create problems. The correct approach depends on the entire diet, forage analysis, DCAD strategy, and herd risk.

### Reduced feed intake

Cows often eat less close to calving. Heat stress, overcrowding, poor bunk access, calving difficulty, sudden ration changes, illness, and uncomfortable housing can reduce dry matter intake.

A cow that consumes less feed receives less calcium, magnesium, energy, and other nutrients at precisely the time her requirements are increasing.

## Which Cows Are at Greatest Risk?

Pay particular attention to:

- Mature cows
- Cows in their second or later lactation
- Jersey cows and other high-risk breeds
- High-producing cows
- Cows with a previous milk fever episode
- Overconditioned cows
- Cows that are excessively thin
- Cows with difficult calvings
- Cows with poor feed intake
- Cows exposed to high-potassium close-up diets
- Cows experiencing heat, transport, or social stress

A previous case of milk fever is especially important. Add these cows to a fresh-cow watch list and discuss targeted calcium supplementation with your veterinarian and nutritionist.

## Emergency Treatment for Milk Fever

Milk fever should always be treated as an emergency. Contact your veterinarian or follow your farm’s written protocol immediately.

Treatment depends on whether the cow is standing.

### If the cow is standing

A standing cow that is alert and swallowing normally is commonly managed with an approved oral calcium bolus. Many herd protocols use a second bolus approximately 12 hours later.

Do not automatically give intravenous calcium to a standing cow with no clinical signs. Rapidly raising blood calcium in a cow that does not need emergency correction may interfere with her natural calcium-regulation response and can lead to lower calcium later. 

### If the cow is down

A recumbent cow generally requires slow intravenous calcium administered by a trained person or veterinarian. Common veterinary protocols use a measured volume of calcium gluconate solution, but the exact product, dose, route, and administration speed must follow veterinary direction and the product label.

Intravenous calcium must be administered slowly because giving it too quickly can cause dangerous heart rhythm disturbances or cardiac arrest. The cow should be monitored during treatment.

Once the cow is standing, alert, and swallowing safely, oral calcium may be used to reduce the risk of relapse. University extension guidance commonly recommends two oral calcium boluses after successful treatment, with the second dose given about 12 hours after the first. 

### Important safety rules

- Do not give an oral bolus or drench to a cow that cannot swallow safely.
- Do not force a down cow to stand.
- Do not administer intravenous calcium rapidly.
- Keep a down cow in a chest-down position.
- Provide deep, dry, non-slip bedding.
- Turn the cow regularly if she remains down.
- Reassess the diagnosis if she does not improve after calcium treatment.

A cow that fails to stand after appropriate calcium therapy may have nerve damage, trauma, toxic mastitis, metritis, hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, ketosis, or another condition requiring veterinary examination.

## How to Prevent Milk Fever

Prevention begins several weeks before calving. A successful program combines nutrition, monitoring, cow comfort, and rapid fresh-cow observation.

### 1. Test forages and formulate the close-up ration

Analyze forages for:

- Calcium
- Magnesium
- Phosphorus
- Potassium
- Sodium
- Chloride
- Sulfur
- Dry matter
- Energy and protein

A mineral supplement cannot reliably correct a ration problem when the mineral content of the forage is unknown.

### 2. Control potassium

Use lower-potassium forages in the close-up dry cow diet when possible. Avoid relying heavily on heavily fertilized or manure-fertilized forage if its potassium content is high.

High-potassium diets are consistently associated with increased milk fever risk because they can reduce the cow’s response to calcium-regulating hormones. 

### 3. Use a negative-DCAD diet when appropriate

A negative dietary cation-anion difference, or DCAD, diet is commonly fed during the final three weeks before calving. It creates a mild metabolic acidosis that helps improve calcium absorption from the intestine and calcium release from bone.

Anionic products must be formulated carefully. Too much can reduce feed intake and contribute to other health problems. Urine pH monitoring helps determine whether the diet is having the intended effect. Suggested urine-pH targets vary by program, but extension guidance commonly places the target around 5.5 to 6.5 for close-up cows receiving anionic diets.  

Never add anionic salts without guidance from a qualified dairy nutritionist.

### 4. Provide adequate magnesium

Magnesium should be included at an appropriate level in the pre-calving and early-lactation diets. Because magnesium is continuously used and does not simply build up as a long-term reserve, supplementation needs to continue according to the herd nutrition plan. 

### 5. Maintain feed and water access

Reduce competition at the feed bunk and provide adequate resting space. Fresh, clean water should be available at all times.

Practical steps include:

- Keep feed fresh and accessible
- Push up feed frequently
- Avoid sudden ration changes
- Reduce overcrowding
- Provide comfortable, dry bedding
- Protect cows from heat and cold stress
- Monitor feed intake before and after calving

### 6. Use targeted oral calcium

Many farms provide oral calcium to higher-risk cows after calving. Common candidates include older cows, high producers, cows with a previous history of milk fever, and cows in herds with frequent hypocalcemia.

The exact product and timing should be part of a written veterinary protocol. Calcium boluses should be given only to cows that are alert and able to swallow.

## Milk Fever and Other Fresh-Cow Diseases

Milk fever does not occur in isolation. Low blood calcium can reduce rumen movement and muscle function, increasing the risk of:

- Retained placenta
- Metritis
- Ketosis
- Displaced abomasum
- Mastitis
- Poor feed intake
- Reduced milk production
- Longer time to reproductive recovery

A down cow does not always have milk fever. Trauma from calving, nerve damage, fractures, toxic mastitis, severe metritis, grass tetany, and other metabolic problems can produce similar signs.

If the cow does not respond promptly to appropriate calcium treatment, stop assuming the problem is simple milk fever and call your veterinarian.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can milk fever occur before calving?

Yes. Most cases occur after calving, but hypocalcemia can occasionally develop during calving or shortly before parturition.

### Does milk fever cause a high temperature?

Usually not. Many affected cows have a normal or low temperature, cold ears, and a dry muzzle. The word “fever” refers to the traditional name rather than a true infectious fever. 

### Can first-calf heifers get milk fever?

They can, but mature cows are generally at greater risk. Heifers usually have a more effective calcium-regulation response and produce less milk than older cows.

### Should every freshly calved cow receive intravenous calcium?

No. Intravenous calcium is intended for appropriate clinical cases and must be administered carefully. Giving IV calcium to a standing cow without clinical signs may create complications and does not replace a well-designed prevention program. 

### Can I give an oral calcium bolus to a down cow?

Not if the cow cannot swallow safely. A recumbent cow may aspirate the bolus or liquid. Down cows require immediate veterinary guidance and commonly need slow intravenous calcium.

### How quickly should a cow respond to calcium?

A cow with uncomplicated milk fever may become brighter and stand during or soon after appropriate calcium treatment. Failure to respond suggests another problem, additional metabolic abnormalities, or secondary muscle and nerve injury.

### Can milk fever happen again after treatment?

Yes. Relapse can occur because the underlying calcium-regulation problem may continue after the initial blood calcium increase. Veterinary protocols often include oral calcium after successful treatment and another dose later to reduce relapse risk.

### Is subclinical hypocalcemia important if the cow looks normal?

Yes. Subclinical hypocalcemia may reduce feed intake, rumen function, milk production, immunity, and reproductive performance. Herd-level prevention is important even when visible milk fever cases are uncommon.

## Conclusion

Milk fever in dairy cows is a preventable but potentially fatal transition-cow disorder. It develops when the cow cannot supply enough calcium to meet the sudden demands of colostrum and milk production.

Your best defenses are early observation, accurate forage analysis, balanced close-up nutrition, adequate magnesium, potassium control, carefully managed DCAD diets, and a clear treatment protocol. Watch mature and previously affected cows closely during the first 72 hours after calving, and treat weakness or recumbency as an emergency.

When prevention, nutrition, and veterinary care work together, you can reduce milk fever cases, improve fresh-cow health, and protect the productivity and welfare of your dairy herd.
