# Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry: A Complete Guide for Farmers and Pet Owners

Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry is one of the most common and costly infectious problems seen in chickens, turkeys and other farmed birds worldwide. It affects mainly the respiratory system and joints, causing chronic respiratory disease, lameness, reduced egg production and higher mortality, especially when other infections are present.  For small‑scale keepers and commercial farmers alike, understanding how to prevent, recognize and manage mycoplasma disease in poultry is essential to protect flock health and farm profits.

This practical veterinary guide explains what mycoplasma bacteria in chickens are, key clinical signs, how infection spreads, diagnostic options, and the treatment of mycoplasma infection in poultry. It also covers real‑world tips for prevention, biosecurity, vaccination and antibiotics for mycoplasma in chickens, in a clear, step‑by‑step way.

## What is Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry?



![Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1621270749227-8037bb2a9b3a?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxMnx8TXljb3BsYXNtYSUyMEluZmVjdGlvbiUyMGluJTIwUG91bHRyeSUyMGFuaW1hbHxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgyMjA1OTg2fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry — via Unsplash*


Mycoplasmas are a special group of bacteria that lack a normal cell wall, making them very small, fragile in the environment, and naturally resistant to some common antibiotics such as penicillins.  In poultry, more than 20 species of mycoplasma have been described, but only a few cause significant disease: mainly Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), Mycoplasma synoviae (MS), M. meleagridis and M. iowae.  Together, these pathogens can severely impact broiler, breeder and layer production.

Mycoplasma gallisepticum infection in poultry is usually associated with chronic respiratory disease (CRD) in chickens and infectious sinusitis in turkeys. It causes catarrhal rhinitis, tracheitis, pneumonia, sinusitis and airsacculitis, often complicated by secondary bacteria such as E. coli.  Mycoplasma synoviae infection in poultry is classically linked with infectious synovitis (joint inflammation) and airsacculitis, but can also contribute to respiratory signs and poor shell quality in eggs.  

Mycoplasma infection in chickens can be subclinical (no obvious signs) or clinical (visible disease). Birds may carry the bacteria for long periods as inapparent carriers, shedding organisms in respiratory secretions or passing them through the egg to chicks (vertical, transovarian transmission), especially for M. gallisepticum and M. synoviae.  These chronic, carrier states are a major reason mycoplasma infections in chickens are so hard to eradicate from a farm once established.

## Why Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry Matters for Farmers and Pet Owners

From an economic standpoint, mycoplasma disease in poultry causes large and ongoing losses due to poor growth, worse feed conversion, carcass condemnation at slaughter, drops in egg production and increased medication and culling costs. In commercial operations, reductions in egg production alone have been estimated at significant annual losses, and severe outbreaks of MG in broilers have led to hundreds of thousands of dollars lost in a single incident.  These numbers highlight why every producer needs a clear guide to Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry and robust control programs.

For smallholders and backyard chicken keepers, mycoplasma infection in chickens is often one of the most frequently encountered respiratory problems. MG is considered the main mycoplasma pathogen of backyard flocks, presenting with “foamy eyes”, sneezing, nasal discharge, swollen eyelids and breathing difficulty.  Birds may survive but remain chronically infected, spreading the organism to new additions or neighboring flocks. This is why mixing birds from different sources without quarantine is a high‑risk practice.

Pet owners and hobby keepers may not face the same financial losses as large farms, but they are often emotionally attached to their birds and may invest heavily in repeated treatments. Mycoplasma infections are also frustrating because antibiotics usually improve clinical signs and reduce spread but do not completely eliminate the organism, leaving carriers in the group.  Understanding realistic goals—control versus eradication—is crucial when deciding how to treat mycoplasma in poultry and when to cull chronic carriers.

## Key Facts About Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry

First, it is important to know the main species involved:

- **Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG)** – the most pathogenic avian mycoplasma; primarily a respiratory pathogen. Causes chronic respiratory disease in chickens and infectious sinusitis in turkeys with signs such as nasal discharge, coughing, swollen sinuses, conjunctivitis and airsacculitis. 
- **Mycoplasma synoviae (MS)** – often targets joints and the respiratory tract. Typical signs include swollen, hot joints (especially hocks), lameness, and sometimes respiratory signs or thin, deformed eggshells at the broad end in layers. 
- **Other species (M. meleagridis, M. iowae)** – mainly important in turkeys and breeding stock, associated with skeletal deformities or reduced hatchability.  

Second, the main **mycoplasma symptoms in poultry** include:

- Nasal discharge, sneezing, coughing, rales (rattly breathing) and gasping  
- Swollen eyelids, foamy or watery eyes, swollen infraorbital sinuses  
- Reduced feed intake and poor weight gain in broilers  
- Drop in egg production and reduced egg quality in layers and breeders  
- Swollen joints, lameness, reluctance to walk (especially with M. synoviae)  
- Increased deaths when other infections (E. coli, viruses) are present  

Third, mycoplasma infection in poultry is **highly contagious** and spreads in two main ways:

- **Vertical (through the egg)** – infected breeder hens can pass MG or MS directly to chicks via the egg (transovarian transmission), creating infected flocks from day‑old birds. 
- **Horizontal (bird‑to‑bird)** – via respiratory droplets, contact with contaminated equipment, people, vehicles, and even wild birds and rodents that act as mechanical carriers.  

Control programs focus heavily on preventing introduction into breeder flocks and maintaining “Mycoplasma‑free” status through biosecurity, monitoring and, where appropriate, vaccination.

## Practical Guide to Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry

This section provides a step‑by‑step, practical guide to Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry, covering causes, clinical recognition, diagnosis, treatment and prevention.

### Causes of Mycoplasma in Poultry

The primary causes of mycoplasma in poultry are infection with MG or MS strains introduced into a flock via:

- Infected replacement birds or hatching eggs that are not certified mycoplasma‑free  
- Contact with neighboring infected flocks, live‑bird markets or shows  
- Poor biosecurity allowing introduction via people, equipment, or wild birds and rodents  

Stress factors strongly influence whether infected birds develop disease. Cold weather, poor ventilation, overcrowding, dusty litter, concurrent infections (e.g. infectious bronchitis, Newcastle disease, E. coli) and some live virus vaccinations can all worsen clinical signs of mycoplasma disease in poultry. 

### Recognizing Mycoplasma Symptoms in Poultry

Early detection is vital. Important points for how to detect mycoplasma infection in chickens and turkeys include:

- Watch for chronic, slowly spreading respiratory disease with sneezing, nasal discharge and swollen sinuses. MG is a classical cause of chronic respiratory disease in chickens. 
- Check eyes for foamy or watery discharge and eyelid swelling, especially in backyard birds. 
- In MS cases, monitor for lameness, birds sitting more, and swollen hock or foot joints that feel hot and contain yellow, viscous exudate. 
- Record egg production and shell quality. Thin, misshapen shells at the broad end may suggest mycoplasma synoviae infection in poultry layers.  

Because these signs are not specific and overlap with many other respiratory diseases, diagnosis should be confirmed by laboratory testing.

### Diagnosis: How Vets Confirm Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry

Veterinarians use a combination of flock history, clinical signs, post‑mortem lesions and laboratory tests. Key diagnostic tools include:

- **Serology** – rapid serum plate agglutination tests and ELISA detect antibodies against MG and MS in blood samples. They are useful for flock screening but may give false positives or cross‑reactions, so positive samples often require confirmation. 
- **Hemagglutination‑inhibition (HI) tests** – used as confirmatory tests for MG and MS antibodies. 
- **Culture and identification** – isolation of mycoplasma from tracheal, sinus, air sac or joint samples in special media containing serum and growth factors. MG and MS are fastidious and slow‑growing, so this method requires expertise. 
- **PCR testing** – detection of specific mycoplasma DNA from tracheal swabs, synovial fluid or other samples. PCR is highly sensitive and is now widely used for rapid diagnosis and flock monitoring.  

Routine serological surveillance is a core pillar of national and industry programs such as the National Poultry Improvement Plan, which coordinates control and serology‑based surveillance for MG and MS in breeder flocks. 

### Treatment of Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry

When it comes to how to treat mycoplasma in poultry, it is important to understand that **antibiotics can control but rarely eradicate** infection. Antimicrobials decrease clinical signs, improve performance, and reduce vertical transmission, but birds often remain carriers.  

Common antibiotics for mycoplasma in chickens include:

- **Macrolides** – tylosin and tilmicosin are widely used and show good activity against MG and MS with limited resistance in many surveys. 
- **Pleuromutilins** – tiamulin is effective but may have side effects like reduced growth and can be costly in some systems. 
- **Tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones** – chlortetracycline, enrofloxacin and others are also used under veterinary direction.  

Field reports note drugs such as tylosin (Tylan), enrofloxacin (Baytril) and erythromycin (Gallmycin) being used for mycoplasma gallisepticum treatment and mycoplasma synoviae antibiotic treatment in chickens, always under veterinary prescription.  

Key treatment principles:

- Always consult a veterinarian before starting antibiotics; misuse promotes resistance and may be illegal without veterinary oversight. 
- Follow correct dose, route (water, feed, injection), and duration as prescribed.  
- Remember that some antibiotics may interfere with immune response to vaccines. 
- Severely affected flocks may need combined approaches: antimicrobial therapy, supportive care (good nutrition, vitamins), and environmental management (improved ventilation, reduced stocking density). 

### Prevention, Biosecurity and Vaccination

Because mycoplasma infections are persistent and hard to clear, **prevention is better than cure**. Effective tips for Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry control include:

1. **Source Mycoplasma‑free stock**  
   - Purchase chicks and hatching eggs only from MG‑ and MS‑negative breeder flocks certified under recognized programs.  

2. **Strong biosecurity**  
   - Keep single‑age farms where possible; depopulate and thoroughly disinfect between flocks.  
   - Control visitor access, use dedicated clothing and footwear, and implement strict cleaning and disinfection of equipment and vehicles.  
   - Control wild birds and rodents, which can carry mycoplasma into your flock.  

3. **Monitoring and surveillance**  
   - Set up a regular testing program using serology and/or PCR, especially in breeders and long‑lived layers.  

4. **Vaccination**  
   - In high‑challenge regions or where eradication is not feasible, live or inactivated MG and MS vaccines may be used to reduce clinical signs and economic losses. 
   - Vaccines do not completely prevent infection but can lower shedding and improve bird performance when integrated with good management.

5. **Culling chronic carriers**  
   - In some systems, depopulation of infected breeder flocks is still used for eradication, as this is the surest way to stop vertical transmission.  

For small backyard flocks, simple measures such as quarantining all new birds for at least 2–3 weeks, avoiding mixing birds from multiple sources, and maintaining clean, well‑ventilated housing can greatly reduce the risk of mycoplasma infection in chickens.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid



![Common mistakes](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1558603374-cf93af771676?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw3fHxmYXJtJTIwYW5pbWFsJTIwaGVhbHRoeXxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgyMjA1OTg2fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


*Photo: Farm management — via Unsplash*


Despite good intentions, many keepers make errors that allow mycoplasma infections in chickens to persist or spread.

One common mistake is **relying only on antibiotics without improving management or biosecurity**. While birds may look better after treatment, continuing overcrowding, poor ventilation, and contact with infected birds mean the problem quickly returns. Antimicrobials should be just one part of a wider plan that includes environmental improvements and disease monitoring.  

Another frequent problem is **buying or mixing birds from different, unknown sources**—auctions, live markets, rescue groups—without quarantine. This is a major route for bringing mycoplasma infection in poultry into previously clean flocks, particularly in backyard and hobby settings.  New birds should always be quarantined and ideally test‑negative before joining the main group.

A further error is **assuming that recovery means the flock is now free of mycoplasma**. In reality, treated or clinically recovered birds often remain carriers and can infect new arrivals or younger stock.  Similarly, skipping regular testing in breeder or layer flocks can allow low‑level infections to persist unnoticed, leading to infected chicks and widespread distribution of mycoplasma‑positive birds.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry

### Q: What is mycoplasma infection in chickens?

**A:** Mycoplasma infection in chickens is a disease caused mainly by Mycoplasma gallisepticum and Mycoplasma synoviae bacteria. MG primarily affects the respiratory system, causing chronic respiratory disease, while MS often causes joint inflammation (infectious synovitis), respiratory signs and sometimes poor eggshell quality. Both can reduce growth, egg production and flock health. 

### Q: What are the most common mycoplasma symptoms in poultry?

**A:** Typical signs include sneezing, nasal discharge, coughing, rattly breathing, swollen eyelids and sinuses, watery or foamy eyes, reduced feed intake, poor weight gain, drops in egg production, and, with M. synoviae, swollen, painful joints and lameness. Birds may also be more prone to secondary infections and may die in higher numbers during outbreaks. 

### Q: How do chickens get mycoplasma disease?

**A:** Chickens can be infected vertically, from infected breeder hens through the egg, or horizontally, from direct contact with infected birds, contaminated equipment, people, vehicles, and wild birds or rodents acting as carriers. Risk is higher where biosecurity is weak and where birds from multiple sources are mixed without quarantine. 

### Q: What is the best treatment of mycoplasma infection in poultry?

**A:** Treatment typically involves prescription antibiotics such as tylosin, tilmicosin, tiamulin, tetracyclines or fluoroquinolones, chosen by a veterinarian based on local regulations and susceptibility patterns. These drugs usually reduce clinical signs and transmission but do not fully eliminate the bacteria, so birds often remain carriers. Supportive care and improved housing conditions are essential to achieve good outcomes. 

### Q: Can I prevent mycoplasma infection in poultry without antibiotics?

**A:** Yes, strong prevention is possible using non‑antibiotic measures: starting with mycoplasma‑free chicks or hatching eggs, maintaining strict biosecurity, preventing contact with other flocks and wild birds, using good ventilation and stocking density, and conducting regular serological or PCR monitoring. In higher‑risk situations, MG and MS vaccination can reduce disease impact as part of a comprehensive control program. 

### Q: Is it better to cull or to treat mycoplasma infections in chickens?

**A:** In commercial breeder operations, depopulation of infected flocks and repopulation with mycoplasma‑free stock is often considered the most reliable method to eradicate infection and prevent vertical transmission.  In backyard or small flocks, owners may choose to treat and manage carriers, accepting that the infection will likely remain in the group and that new birds may be at risk.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

Any time you suspect mycoplasma disease in poultry—especially if multiple birds show chronic respiratory signs, swollen joints or a sudden drop in egg production—you should involve a veterinarian. Early veterinary input allows proper sampling and testing (serology, PCR or culture) to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other important diseases like infectious bronchitis, Newcastle disease or avian influenza.  

A veterinarian can advise on the most appropriate antibiotics for mycoplasma in chickens in your region, taking into account local resistance patterns, regulations and withdrawal periods for meat and eggs. They can also assess flock management, housing and biosecurity and provide tailored tips for Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry control on your specific farm, including whether vaccination or culling is advisable.  

For commercial farms, veterinarians play a key role in designing monitoring programs, interpreting serology, and liaising with hatcheries and breeding companies to maintain Mycoplasma‑free status. For backyard owners, they can help balance animal welfare, practical constraints and realistic disease control goals, and explain clearly how to Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry management in small mixed‑age flocks.

## Conclusion

Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry is a chronic, contagious disease complex that can severely affect chickens, turkeys and other birds, causing respiratory disease, joint problems, production losses and increased mortality. The main pathogens, Mycoplasma gallisepticum and Mycoplasma synoviae, are widespread and difficult to eradicate because they can be transmitted through eggs and persist in carrier birds even after treatment.  

Effective control depends on a combination of strategies: starting with Mycoplasma‑free breeding stock, applying strict biosecurity, using regular serological and molecular monitoring, considering vaccination where appropriate, and using antibiotics judiciously under veterinary guidance.  Farmers, students and pet owners who follow a structured guide to Mycoplasma Infection in Poultry—focusing on prevention, early diagnosis and integrated control—can significantly reduce the impact of mycoplasma infections in chickens and safeguard the health and productivity of their flocks.
