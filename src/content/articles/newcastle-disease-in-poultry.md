Newcastle disease in poultry is one of the most serious infectious diseases affecting chickens and other birds. It can spread rapidly through a flock, reduce egg production, cause severe illness, and kill large numbers of unvaccinated birds. In severe outbreaks, some birds may die suddenly before showing obvious symptoms.   

Whether you keep a backyard flock, raise village chickens, or manage a commercial poultry farm, early recognition and prevention are essential. Newcastle disease has no specific cure, so your best protection comes from vaccination, daily biosecurity, careful monitoring, and rapid veterinary action.

## What Is Newcastle Disease in Poultry?

Newcastle disease is a contagious viral disease caused by virulent strains of avian paramyxovirus type 1, also called Newcastle disease virus, or NDV. It can affect chickens, turkeys, pigeons, quail, guinea fowl, pheasants, parrots, waterfowl, and many wild bird species. Chickens are among the most susceptible domestic poultry.   

The disease may affect several body systems, including the:

- Respiratory system
- Nervous system
- Digestive system
- Reproductive system

The severity of Newcastle disease depends on the strain of virus, the bird’s age, vaccination status, immune health, and species. Young chickens and unvaccinated flocks are particularly vulnerable. Low-virulence strains may cause mild respiratory disease, while virulent strains can produce severe illness and high mortality.   

Newcastle disease is sometimes described using three traditional categories:

- **Lentogenic strains:** Low-virulence strains that may cause mild or unnoticed disease. Some are used in vaccines.
- **Mesogenic strains:** Moderately virulent strains that may cause respiratory and neurological disease.
- **Velogenic strains:** Highly virulent strains that can cause severe respiratory, digestive, or neurological disease and widespread death.

The term “virulent Newcastle disease” is often used for strains capable of causing severe disease and major flock losses.

## How Does Newcastle Disease Spread?

Newcastle disease spreads through direct and indirect contact with infected birds. Infected birds shed the virus in respiratory secretions, saliva, feces, and other body materials. They may spread the virus during the incubation period, while visibly sick, and for a period after recovery.  

Common routes of transmission include:

- Breathing contaminated dust or aerosol
- Contact with nasal or mouth discharge
- Eating contaminated feed
- Drinking contaminated water
- Contact with infected droppings
- Shared feeders, drinkers, crates, cages, and equipment
- Contaminated clothing, footwear, hands, and vehicles
- Movement of infected live birds
- Contact with wild birds, pigeons, or waterfowl
- Contaminated egg trays, litter, manure, and carcasses

Wild birds and waterfowl may carry some Newcastle disease viruses without obvious illness. This makes outdoor access and contact with wild birds an important risk, particularly for backyard and free-range poultry.   

The virus can survive for extended periods in suitable environmental conditions, especially in feces and frozen materials. Poor cleaning, crowded housing, wet litter, rodents, and frequent movement of birds can make spread more likely. 

## Newcastle Disease Incubation Period

The incubation period is the time between exposure to the virus and the appearance of clinical signs. In poultry, signs may develop as early as two days after exposure, although four to six days is common. In some cases, signs may not appear for up to 15 days.  

Because Newcastle disease can spread before symptoms become obvious, a flock may already be infected when the first sick bird is noticed. This is why you should respond immediately to unusual deaths, respiratory problems, neurological signs, or a sudden decline in egg production.

## Signs and Symptoms of Newcastle Disease in Poultry

Newcastle disease does not have one unique symptom. Its signs can resemble avian influenza, infectious bronchitis, infectious laryngotracheitis, fowl cholera, mycoplasmosis, and other poultry diseases. Laboratory testing is required for confirmation.  

Symptoms may include the following.

### Respiratory Signs

Respiratory symptoms are common, especially with low- and moderately virulent strains. Watch for:

- Sneezing
- Coughing
- Gasping
- Difficulty breathing
- Rattling or noisy breathing
- Nasal discharge
- Mucus around the beak
- Open-mouth breathing
- Conjunctivitis
- Swelling around the eyes

Affected birds may sit quietly, keep their feathers fluffed, or separate themselves from the flock.

### Neurological Signs

Some infections primarily affect the nervous system. Neurological symptoms may include:

- Tremors
- Loss of balance
- Circling
- Weakness
- Drooping wings
- Paralysis of the legs or wings
- Stiffness
- Twisted neck
- Head pulled backward
- Difficulty standing or walking
- Seizure-like movements

A twisted neck, known as torticollis, is an important warning sign, but it is not exclusive to Newcastle disease. Other infections, injuries, toxins, and nutritional problems can produce similar signs.

### Digestive Signs

Digestive symptoms may include:

- Greenish, watery diarrhea
- Loss of appetite
- Reduced water consumption
- Dehydration
- Weight loss
- Weakness

Severe strains may cause internal hemorrhages, intestinal damage, and other lesions. However, not every infected bird will show visible lesions during a postmortem examination.

### Reproductive Signs

In laying hens, Newcastle disease may cause:

- Sudden reduction in egg production
- Temporary or complete cessation of laying
- Thin-shelled eggs
- Soft-shelled eggs
- Misshapen eggs
- Rough or abnormal shells
- Watery egg whites

In a well-vaccinated flock, reduced egg production or abnormal eggs may be the only noticeable signs. Vaccinated birds can still become infected and shed virus even when they appear relatively healthy. 

### Sudden Death

Sudden death may be the first sign of severe Newcastle disease. Some virulent infections cause rapid flock-wide illness, while others produce a mixture of respiratory, digestive, and neurological symptoms.

In unvaccinated flocks, morbidity and mortality can approach 100 percent with highly virulent virus strains. The actual outcome varies by strain, bird age, immunity, species, and other health conditions.  

## What Should You Do If You Suspect Newcastle Disease?

Treat suspected Newcastle disease as an emergency. Do not wait several days to see whether the birds improve.

Take these steps:

1. **Stop moving birds.** Do not sell, trade, transport, or lend birds from the affected flock.
2. **Restrict visitors.** Keep unnecessary people away from poultry housing.
3. **Separate visibly sick birds if advised by your veterinarian.** Avoid creating additional contamination through excessive handling.
4. **Use dedicated clothing and footwear.** Do not wear poultry-area boots into other flocks.
5. **Clean and disinfect equipment.** Pay attention to feeders, drinkers, crates, tools, and transport vehicles.
6. **Contact a veterinarian or animal health authority immediately.**
7. **Follow official sampling, quarantine, testing, and control instructions.**

Do not send live or dead birds to markets, shows, auctions, or neighboring farms. Do not dispose of carcasses casually, because contaminated carcasses, litter, and manure can spread the virus.

Newcastle disease is reportable or notifiable in many jurisdictions. Confirmed virulent disease can result in movement restrictions, surveillance, quarantine, depopulation, and trade controls.  

## How Is Newcastle Disease Diagnosed?

Clinical signs alone cannot reliably confirm Newcastle disease. Several poultry illnesses cause coughing, diarrhea, nervous signs, egg-production losses, and sudden death. Avian influenza is one of the most important diseases that must be ruled out.  

A veterinarian or animal health authority may collect:

- Oropharyngeal or tracheal swabs
- Cloacal swabs
- Fresh fecal samples
- Tissue samples
- Blood or serum samples
- Carcass samples when appropriate

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, or real-time RT-PCR, is commonly used to detect viral RNA and help confirm infection. Virus isolation and antibody testing may also be used.   

Avoid performing an informal backyard postmortem and moving tissues between locations. Improper handling can spread contamination and may compromise official testing.

## Is There a Treatment for Newcastle Disease?

There is no medication that cures virulent Newcastle disease or stops the virus from multiplying. Antiviral treatment is not recommended for controlling the disease.  

Antibiotics do not kill Newcastle disease virus. In selected cases, a veterinarian may prescribe antimicrobials to manage secondary bacterial infections, particularly in less severe infections. Supportive care may include:

- Easy access to clean drinking water
- Appropriate complete feed
- Electrolytes when recommended
- Warm, dry, low-stress housing
- Protection from extreme temperatures
- Veterinary treatment for secondary infections

Supportive care must not replace reporting, testing, isolation, or official control measures. Recovered birds may continue to shed virus, and moving them to another flock can create a new outbreak.

## Newcastle Disease Vaccination

Vaccination is one of the most important tools for controlling Newcastle disease in poultry. Vaccines reduce clinical disease, mortality, and production losses, but they do not always create complete “sterile immunity.” In other words, a vaccinated bird may still become infected and shed virus, particularly if immunity is incomplete.   

Available vaccine types include:

- **Live lentogenic vaccines**, often administered by spray, drinking water, eye drop, or nostril application
- **Inactivated vaccines**, usually administered by injection
- **Recombinant or vectored vaccines**, available for some poultry production systems
- **Thermostable vaccines**, useful in areas where maintaining refrigeration is difficult

Your vaccination program should be designed with a poultry veterinarian. It may depend on:

- Local disease risk
- Bird species
- Flock size
- Production purpose
- Maternal antibodies
- Existing diseases
- Vaccine availability
- Local regulations
- Exposure from wild birds
- Whether birds are raised indoors or outdoors

Vaccinate healthy birds according to the product label. Pay close attention to storage temperature, expiry dates, dilution instructions, dose, administration method, and booster timing. Poor vaccine handling, missed boosters, incorrect dosing, and uneven administration can reduce flock protection.  

A vaccine program should never be viewed as permission to relax biosecurity. Vaccinated poultry can still carry and spread Newcastle disease.

## Biosecurity Checklist for Poultry Owners

Biosecurity means preventing disease from entering your flock and limiting spread if exposure occurs. Use the following daily checklist:

### Control Bird Movement

- Quarantine new birds before introducing them to your flock.
- Buy poultry only from reputable sources.
- Keep new, sick, and returning show birds separate.
- Avoid mixing birds from different farms or markets.
- Do not share birds, crates, transport cages, or equipment.

### Limit Wild Bird Contact

- Repair holes in poultry housing.
- Cover feed and water containers.
- Prevent wild birds from eating poultry feed.
- Keep domestic birds away from ponds and areas used by waterfowl.
- Remove spilled feed promptly.

### Improve Cleaning and Disinfection

- Clean manure and organic material before applying disinfectant.
- Disinfect feeders, drinkers, tools, crates, and footwear.
- Keep litter dry and replace heavily contaminated bedding.
- Follow the correct concentration and contact time for disinfectants.
- Dispose of dead birds safely and according to local rules.

### Manage People and Vehicles

- Limit visitors to poultry areas.
- Provide clean or disposable footwear.
- Wash hands before and after handling birds.
- Use farm-specific clothing.
- Clean and disinfect vehicles that enter poultry areas.
- Work with young and healthy birds before older or sick birds.

### Monitor Your Flock Daily

Record changes in:

- Feed and water consumption
- Mortality
- Egg production
- Eggshell quality
- Respiratory signs
- Droppings
- Activity and behavior
- Neurological symptoms

A written health record can help your veterinarian identify the speed and pattern of an outbreak.

## Can Newcastle Disease Affect People?

People who have close contact with infected birds or large amounts of vaccine virus may occasionally develop temporary conjunctivitis, or eye inflammation. Mild influenza-like symptoms may also occur. Human infection is uncommon and is not known to spread from person to person.   

There is no evidence that people contract Newcastle disease from eating properly cooked poultry products. Nevertheless, poultry workers, veterinarians, vaccination teams, and outbreak responders should use appropriate personal protective equipment and wash their hands thoroughly. 

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main cause of Newcastle disease in poultry?

Newcastle disease is caused by infection with virulent avian paramyxovirus type 1, also known as Newcastle disease virus. The virus spreads through infected birds, droppings, respiratory secretions, contaminated equipment, clothing, footwear, feed, water, and vehicles. 

### What are the first signs of Newcastle disease?

Early signs may include depression, reduced appetite, reduced activity, sneezing, coughing, gasping, nasal discharge, watery diarrhea, or a sudden decline in egg production. Sudden death may occur in severe infections. 

### Can vaccinated chickens still get Newcastle disease?

Yes. Vaccination usually reduces severe illness and death, but it may not completely prevent infection or virus shedding. Correct storage, dosing, administration, and booster timing are essential for the best protection.  

### Is there a cure for Newcastle disease?

No specific cure is available. Antibiotics do not treat the virus, although a veterinarian may use them for secondary bacterial infections in selected cases. Control relies on testing, biosecurity, vaccination, and official disease-control measures. 

### Should I isolate a chicken with a twisted neck?

A bird with a twisted neck should be separated from the flock when safe to do so, but this sign alone does not prove Newcastle disease. Contact a veterinarian promptly because neurological signs can have several causes.

### How often should poultry receive Newcastle disease vaccine?

The schedule depends on the vaccine, bird type, local disease pressure, and veterinary recommendations. Some village poultry programs use repeat vaccination every three to four months, while commercial programs may use different schedules and combinations of live and inactivated vaccines. 

### Is Newcastle disease dangerous to humans?

Human infection is rare and generally causes temporary conjunctivitis or mild flu-like illness after heavy exposure. Properly cooked poultry products are not considered a food-safety concern.

## Conclusion

Newcastle disease in poultry can spread quickly and cause devastating losses, especially in young, stressed, or unvaccinated birds. Respiratory distress, green diarrhea, nervous signs, abnormal eggs, sudden death, and falling egg production should always be taken seriously.

Because there is no specific treatment, prevention must be your priority. Build a reliable vaccination program with veterinary guidance, protect feed and water from contamination, control wild bird contact, quarantine new poultry, disinfect equipment, and report unusual sickness or deaths immediately. Strong biosecurity combined with accurate vaccination is the most effective way to protect your flock from Newcastle disease.
