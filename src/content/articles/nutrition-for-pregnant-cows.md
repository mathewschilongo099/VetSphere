Nutrition for pregnant cows is one of the most important parts of successful cattle management. The right feeding program supports fetal growth, protects the cow’s body condition, improves calf vigor, and helps the cow return to reproduction after calving.

A pregnant cow does not need the same diet throughout gestation. Her requirements change as the fetus grows, forage quality changes, weather conditions become more challenging, and calving approaches. The final trimester is especially important because approximately two-thirds of fetal growth occurs during the last third of pregnancy.  

Whether you manage beef cows, dairy cows, or replacement heifers, the goal is not simply to provide “more feed.” Your goal is to provide the right balance of energy, protein, fiber, minerals, vitamins, and water at every stage of pregnancy.

## Why Nutrition Matters During Pregnancy

A pregnant cow must supply nutrients for several purposes at once:

- Maintaining her own body functions
- Supporting the placenta and developing fetus
- Growing her uterus and mammary tissue
- Producing colostrum before calving
- Maintaining an appropriate body condition
- Preparing for lactation and future reproduction

When feed quality or intake is inadequate, the cow may mobilize her own fat and muscle reserves. This can cause weight loss, reduced immunity, weak calves, poor colostrum production, difficult recovery after calving, and delayed rebreeding.

Late-pregnancy undernutrition can also increase the risk of pregnancy toxemia. This metabolic disorder occurs when energy intake cannot meet the demands of the rapidly growing fetus and placenta. Signs may include rapid weight loss, reduced appetite, depression, weakness, and eventually recumbency.  

Overfeeding is also a concern. Excessive energy intake can make cows overconditioned, increase calving problems, and raise the risk of metabolic disease after calving. The best nutrition program maintains a healthy, moderate body condition rather than causing excessive weight gain.

## Nutritional Needs by Stage of Pregnancy

### Early Gestation

During early pregnancy, a mature cow’s nutrient requirements are often close to maintenance requirements, particularly if she is not lactating. However, this does not mean nutrition can be ignored.

Early gestation is an important period for embryo survival and placental development. Severe nutritional stress, illness, heat stress, or abrupt feed changes can negatively affect reproductive performance.

During this stage:

- Maintain a steady body condition.
- Provide adequate pasture or hay.
- Avoid sudden changes in forage or supplements.
- Monitor thin cows separately from cows in good condition.
- Ensure constant access to clean water and minerals.

For a mature cow in good condition, average-quality pasture or hay may be sufficient during early pregnancy. However, first-calf heifers and cows that are still lactating may need additional energy and protein because they have more than one major nutritional demand.

### Mid-Gestation

Mid-gestation is often the period when nutrient requirements are lowest for a mature, dry beef cow. This makes it a useful time to feed lower-cost forage, provided the forage is available and digestible enough to maintain body condition.

Do not assume that poor-quality forage is adequate simply because the cow appears full. Mature, weathered forage may contain too little protein to support rumen microbes. When protein is deficient, fiber digestion and forage intake can decline.

During mid-gestation, assess:

- Forage protein concentration
- Total digestible nutrients
- Hay availability
- Cow body condition
- Weather and grazing conditions
- Whether cows are mature or still growing

A forage analysis is more reliable than judging hay by color or appearance. Two loads of hay can look similar while providing very different levels of crude protein, energy, minerals, and fiber.

### Late Gestation

Late gestation is the most demanding stage of pregnancy for many beef cows. Fetal growth accelerates, the uterus occupies more abdominal space, and the cow’s intake capacity may fall as calving approaches.

For beef cows, nutrient requirements during the last 60 days of pregnancy may increase from approximately 48% to 54% total digestible nutrients and from 7% to 9% crude protein, depending on the animal and production system.  

A mature cow may consume approximately 2% to 2.5% of her body weight in dry matter when forage intake is not restricted by poor quality or limited availability.  For example, a 1,200-pound cow may consume roughly 24 to 30 pounds of dry matter daily. The as-fed weight will be higher if the feed contains moisture.

Late-pregnancy feeding should focus on:

- Maintaining body condition
- Supplying enough energy for fetal growth
- Providing adequate protein for the rumen and fetus
- Preventing excessive fat accumulation
- Maintaining mineral and vitamin status
- Preparing the cow for calving and lactation

Pregnant dairy cows require careful transition-cow management during the final weeks before calving. Dry matter intake commonly decreases during the last two to three weeks of gestation, making it necessary to formulate a more nutrient-dense ration without overfeeding energy. 

## Energy: The Foundation of a Pregnancy Diet

Energy is often the first limiting nutrient in pregnant cows. It comes primarily from digestible carbohydrates, including digestible fiber, starch, and other energy-producing nutrients.

Energy requirements are affected by:

- Cow size
- Age and parity
- Pregnancy stage
- Fetal size or number
- Lactation
- Activity level
- Cold, wind, snow, mud, or heat
- Body condition
- Forage quality

A thin cow needs enough energy to support the fetus while rebuilding some body reserves. A cow in excessive condition needs a controlled diet to avoid becoming obese.

Good energy sources may include:

- High-quality grass hay
- Legume hay
- Corn silage
- Small grains
- Properly balanced commercial supplements
- Distillers grains
- Soybean products
- Other locally available feeds

Introduce grain or high-energy supplements gradually. Sudden increases in starch can disrupt rumen fermentation and increase the risk of acidosis. Any concentrate should be used to correct a specific nutrient deficiency, not simply added without evaluating the whole ration.

## Protein Requirements for Pregnant Cows

Protein supports fetal tissue, placental growth, uterine development, mammary tissue, immune function, and rumen microbial activity.

Protein becomes especially important when cows consume mature grass, dormant range, straw, crop residues, or weathered hay. Low-protein forage can reduce rumen microbial growth and make it harder for the cow to digest fiber.

Useful protein sources include:

- Alfalfa or other legume hay
- Soybean meal
- Canola meal
- Cottonseed meal
- Distillers grains
- Commercial protein tubs or pellets
- Approved nonprotein nitrogen products

In late pregnancy, a mature cow consuming low-quality forage may need a protein supplement to maintain forage digestion and meet her requirements. One extension example notes that a 1,200-pound mature cow in late gestation may require approximately 9 to 11 pounds of total digestible nutrients and about 2 pounds of crude protein daily, although actual needs vary with body weight, forage, pregnancy stage, and environment.  

Do not select a supplement based only on its crude protein percentage. Consider:

- Protein degradability
- Energy content
- Intake rate
- Mineral concentration
- Cost per pound of nutrient
- Compatibility with the forage
- Whether cows will consume it consistently

First-calf heifers deserve special attention because they are still growing while supporting a pregnancy. They may also have lower social status and lose access to feed when housed with mature cows. Separating heifers from mature cows several weeks before calving can help ensure they receive adequate nutrients. 

## Minerals for Pregnant Cows

Minerals are needed in smaller quantities than energy or protein, but deficiencies can have serious consequences for both cow and calf.

Important minerals include:

### Calcium and Phosphorus

Calcium supports bones, muscles, nerves, milk production, and colostrum production. Phosphorus is involved in bone development, energy metabolism, and reproduction.

The correct balance depends on the entire ration. Avoid adding phosphorus automatically without testing the forage and reviewing the mineral program.

### Magnesium

Magnesium is particularly important when cows graze lush, rapidly growing pasture. Under certain conditions, high potassium and nitrogen levels can reduce magnesium availability and contribute to grass tetany risk.

### Selenium

Selenium supports antioxidant defenses and immune function. Deficiency has been associated with weak calves, white muscle disease, poor immune response, and retained placenta. However, selenium can be toxic at excessive levels, so follow the product label and local veterinary guidance.

### Copper and Zinc

Copper and zinc support immune function, skin and hoof health, reproduction, and fetal development. High levels of iron, sulfur, or molybdenum can interfere with copper availability. This is why a mineral program should be based on forage testing and regional conditions rather than guesswork.

### Iodine and Manganese

Iodine is necessary for thyroid hormone production. Deficiency may contribute to weak or hairless calves, stillbirths, and impaired fertility. Manganese supports bone formation, metabolism, and reproductive function. Deficiency has been associated with skeletal abnormalities and reproductive problems.  

Use a complete cattle mineral formulated for pregnant animals. Provide it in a location where all cows can access it, and monitor actual consumption. Free-choice mineral intake can vary widely, so check the feeder regularly and compare consumption with the label recommendation.

## Vitamins and Colostrum Support

Vitamins A, D, and E are especially important in pregnant cows.

Vitamin A supports vision, immune function, reproduction, fetal development, and calf health. Cows grazing dormant forage or consuming large amounts of stored feed may require supplemental vitamin A because carotene levels decline as forage matures and is stored.

Vitamin D supports calcium and phosphorus metabolism. Vitamin E works with selenium to support antioxidant protection and immune function.

The calf is born with limited vitamin A reserves and depends heavily on colostrum and milk for early vitamin support. Low vitamin A intake by the dam can contribute to weak or abnormal calves, reduced fertility, and retained placentas.  

In dairy cows, the final weeks before calving are also important for colostrum production. Colostrum contains substantially more protein, fat, vitamins, and minerals than normal milk and provides the calf with essential immune protection. 

## Water: The Often-Forgotten Nutrient

Water is essential for digestion, feed intake, circulation, fetal development, and milk production. Pregnant cows should have access to clean, palatable water at all times.

Check water sources for:

- Adequate flow
- Ice during winter
- Mud and manure contamination
- Algae
- Excessive salts
- Unpleasant taste or odor

Poor-quality water can reduce intake. In dairy systems, water with total dissolved solids above approximately 5,000 to 6,000 mg/L may be unsuitable for pregnant or lactating cows, depending on the specific mineral profile and management conditions. 

## Body Condition Scoring Pregnant Cows

Body condition scoring helps you evaluate whether your feeding program is working. It is more useful than body weight alone because body weight includes the fetus, uterus, gut fill, and water.

For beef cows, a body condition score of approximately 5 to 6 on a 1-to-9 scale at calving is commonly considered a practical target. Cows below this range may have difficulty maintaining reproductive performance, while excessively fat cows may face greater calving and metabolic risks.  

Score cows at several points:

1. At weaning
2. At pregnancy diagnosis
3. Approximately 90 days before calving
4. About 30 days before calving
5. At calving
6. Before breeding

Separate cows into feeding groups when possible:

- Thin cows
- Mature cows in moderate condition
- Overconditioned cows
- First-calf heifers
- Older or compromised cows

This allows you to target supplementation instead of feeding every animal the same way.

## A Practical Feeding Plan

Use this step-by-step approach to improve nutrition for pregnant cows:

### 1. Identify the animals

Record breed, age, weight, parity, pregnancy stage, lactation status, and body condition score.

### 2. Test the forage

Analyze hay, silage, pasture, or crop residues for dry matter, crude protein, energy, fiber, calcium, phosphorus, and other relevant minerals.

### 3. Estimate intake

Remember that ration calculations use dry matter, while feed is purchased and delivered on an as-fed basis. Mixing up these values can lead to underfeeding or overfeeding.

### 4. Match nutrients to pregnancy stage

Use a lower-cost maintenance diet during mid-gestation when appropriate, then increase nutrient density as fetal growth accelerates.

### 5. Add supplements strategically

Choose protein, energy, mineral, or vitamin supplements based on the actual deficiency. Do not assume that the most expensive product is the most effective.

### 6. Monitor intake and body condition

Observe cows daily. Watch for sorting, aggressive competition, empty bunks, refusal of feed, or cows that are not visiting the feeder.

### 7. Prepare for calving

During the final three weeks, protect cows from sudden ration changes, overcrowding, mud, cold stress, and poor access to feed or water.

## Common Nutrition Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid these frequent management problems:

- Waiting until calving is near to correct thin cows
- Feeding all cows the same ration
- Relying on forage appearance instead of forage testing
- Ignoring first-calf heifers
- Overfeeding grain to “increase calf size”
- Providing mineral but never checking consumption
- Allowing water sources to become dirty or frozen
- Making abrupt feed changes
- Feeding moldy, spoiled, or poorly stored forage
- Letting cows become excessively fat
- Failing to account for wind, cold, snow, mud, or long walking distances

Underfeeding late-pregnant cows can compromise calf vigor and colostrum production, while excessive body condition can increase calving and postpartum health problems. A balanced, monitored plan is safer than extreme feeding in either direction. 

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best feed for a pregnant cow?

There is no single best feed for every pregnant cow. High-quality forage should form the foundation of the diet, with protein, energy, minerals, and vitamins added according to forage analysis, body condition, pregnancy stage, and production type.

### Should pregnant cows receive grain?

Some cows benefit from grain or another energy supplement, particularly if forage is low in energy or cows are losing condition. Introduce grain gradually and formulate the complete ration to avoid rumen upset and excessive weight gain.

### How much should a pregnant cow eat?

Many mature cows consume approximately 2% to 2.5% of body weight in dry matter, but intake varies with body size, forage quality, pregnancy stage, temperature, and whether the cow is lactating. 

### Do pregnant cows need a mineral supplement?

Most pregnant cows benefit from a complete mineral and vitamin program, especially when grazing dormant pasture or eating stored forage. The exact product should be selected using forage analysis and local veterinary or nutrition advice.

### Can poor nutrition cause weak calves?

Yes. Inadequate energy, protein, minerals, or vitamins during pregnancy can contribute to poor fetal development, weak calves, reduced thermoregulation, poor colostrum production, and inadequate immune protection.

### Should first-calf heifers be fed separately?

When possible, yes. First-calf heifers are still growing and may be pushed away from feed by mature cows. Separate feeding helps them receive adequate nutrients during late pregnancy and supports future reproductive performance.

### Can overfeeding cause calving problems?

Overfeeding cannot usually push calf birth weight beyond its genetic potential, but excessive cow condition can increase calving difficulty and postpartum metabolic problems. Aim for a moderate body condition rather than fat cows.

## Conclusion

Good nutrition for pregnant cows is a year-round management process, not a last-minute adjustment before calving. The strongest program combines forage testing, body condition scoring, targeted supplementation, complete mineral support, clean water, and close observation.

Focus on maintaining a healthy body condition early, meeting rising energy and protein needs during late gestation, and protecting feed intake as calving approaches. When you match the diet to the cow’s stage of pregnancy and individual needs, you improve the chances of a healthy calving, a vigorous calf, quality colostrum, and a cow that is ready for her next production cycle.
