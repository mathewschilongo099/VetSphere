Parvovirus in dogs, commonly called **parvo**, is one of the most serious and contagious diseases a dog can develop. It can cause sudden vomiting, severe diarrhea, dehydration, immune suppression, and life-threatening complications—especially in puppies that have not completed their vaccination series.

The good news is that canine parvovirus is preventable. Vaccination, careful hygiene, and prompt veterinary treatment can dramatically improve your dog’s chance of staying healthy or recovering from infection. If your dog is vomiting, unusually tired, refusing food, or developing diarrhea, contact your veterinarian immediately rather than waiting for symptoms to worsen.

## What Is Parvovirus in Dogs?

Canine parvovirus is a highly contagious virus that primarily attacks rapidly dividing cells in a dog’s body. The intestinal lining and bone marrow are especially vulnerable, which explains why infected dogs often develop severe gastrointestinal illness and a weakened immune system.  

Parvo most commonly affects puppies between 6 and 20 weeks of age, although unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated adult dogs can also become infected. Certain breeds may be more susceptible to severe disease, but any dog can contract parvovirus.  

The disease is often referred to as **parvoviral enteritis** because it causes inflammation and damage in the intestines. In very young puppies, the virus may also affect the heart muscle, although this form is less common today than gastrointestinal parvo.

## How Do Dogs Get Parvo?

Parvovirus spreads primarily through contact with infected feces. A dog does not need to directly interact with a sick animal to become infected. The virus can be carried on:

- Contaminated soil, grass, sidewalks, and floors
- Food and water bowls
- Kennels, crates, and bedding
- Leashes, collars, toys, and grooming tools
- Human hands, shoes, and clothing
- The fur or paws of an exposed dog

A dog can become infected after sniffing, licking, or consuming a tiny amount of contaminated material. Infected dogs may shed the virus before obvious symptoms appear, which makes prevention challenging. Dogs can continue shedding parvovirus for a period after clinical signs improve.  

Parvovirus is unusually hardy. It can survive in the environment for months and, under favorable conditions, possibly longer. Many ordinary household cleaners do not kill it, so thorough cleaning with an appropriate disinfectant is essential. 

## Which Dogs Are Most at Risk?

Although every dog should be protected against parvovirus, the following dogs face the greatest risk:

- Puppies younger than 6 months
- Dogs that have received no vaccines
- Puppies that have received only one vaccine dose
- Dogs with incomplete or unknown vaccination histories
- Dogs recently adopted from shelters or crowded facilities
- Dogs exposed to contaminated parks, kennels, or yards
- Dogs with weakened immune systems
- Dogs living in areas with known parvovirus outbreaks

Puppies are particularly vulnerable because maternal antibodies received through their mother’s milk gradually decline. Those antibodies may protect against infection but can also interfere with early vaccination. This creates a “window of susceptibility” during which a puppy may be vulnerable despite having already received a vaccine. 

## Parvovirus Symptoms in Dogs

The first signs of parvo can look mild and nonspecific. A puppy may simply seem quieter than usual or lose interest in food. The illness can then progress rapidly over the next 24 to 48 hours.

Common symptoms of parvovirus in dogs include:

- Extreme tiredness or weakness
- Loss of appetite
- Fever or abnormally low body temperature
- Vomiting
- Watery or bloody diarrhea
- Foul-smelling stool
- Abdominal pain or bloating
- Dehydration
- Drooling or nausea
- Pale gums
- Weight loss
- Collapse

The diarrhea may be bloody, but it is not always. Some dogs with parvo develop severe vomiting and watery diarrhea without visible blood. A lack of blood does not rule out infection.

Symptoms typically appear several days after exposure. The usual incubation period is about 3 to 7 days, although signs can develop anywhere from approximately 2 to 14 days after infection. 

## When Is Parvo an Emergency?

Parvovirus should always be treated as a veterinary emergency, particularly in puppies. Vomiting and diarrhea can rapidly cause dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, low blood sugar, and shock.

Seek immediate veterinary care if your dog has:

- Repeated vomiting
- Bloody or persistent diarrhea
- Severe lethargy
- Refusal to drink
- Pale, gray, or sticky gums
- Collapse or difficulty standing
- A painful or swollen abdomen
- Signs of dehydration
- A known exposure to parvovirus

Do not wait to see whether symptoms improve overnight. Dogs with parvo can deteriorate quickly, and early treatment improves the likelihood of recovery.

## How Is Canine Parvovirus Diagnosed?

Your veterinarian will consider your dog’s age, vaccination history, symptoms, recent exposure, and physical examination findings. A fecal antigen test is commonly used because it can provide results quickly in a veterinary clinic.

Additional tests may include:

- Complete blood count
- Blood chemistry testing
- Electrolyte testing
- Blood glucose measurement
- Fecal PCR testing
- Abdominal imaging when complications are suspected

A fecal test can occasionally be negative early in the disease or later when viral shedding decreases. If your dog’s symptoms strongly suggest parvo, your veterinarian may recommend repeating the test or performing PCR testing.  

Bloodwork is also useful because parvovirus can damage bone marrow and reduce white blood cell counts. Low white blood cells may increase the risk of secondary bacterial infections and help your veterinarian assess disease severity.

## Treatment for Parvovirus in Dogs

There is no simple home remedy that eliminates canine parvovirus. Treatment focuses on supporting your dog’s body while the immune system fights the infection. Severe cases often require hospitalization and isolation.

Veterinary treatment may include:

### Intravenous fluids

IV fluids help correct dehydration, restore circulation, and replace fluid lost through vomiting and diarrhea. Electrolytes such as potassium may also need to be supplemented.

Dogs with mild dehydration may sometimes receive subcutaneous fluids as part of an outpatient treatment plan, but moderate or severe dehydration generally requires IV therapy and close monitoring.

### Anti-vomiting medication

Antiemetic medications help control vomiting and nausea. Once vomiting is controlled, your dog may be able to receive nutrition and maintain hydration more effectively.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics do not kill parvovirus. However, the damaged intestinal lining and low white blood cell count can allow bacteria to enter the bloodstream. Veterinarians may prescribe antibiotics to reduce the risk of secondary bacterial infection or sepsis. 

### Nutritional support

Veterinarians increasingly emphasize early nutritional support when it is safe. Prolonged fasting can weaken an already compromised puppy and may delay intestinal healing. Some dogs may need assisted feeding or a feeding tube if they cannot eat on their own.

Never force food or water into a dog that is actively vomiting. Follow your veterinarian’s instructions about feeding amounts, timing, and diet.

### Pain control and nursing care

Parvo can cause significant abdominal discomfort. Veterinary teams may provide appropriate pain relief, warmth, clean bedding, and frequent monitoring of body temperature, blood glucose, hydration, and electrolyte levels.

### Monoclonal antibody treatment

A canine parvovirus monoclonal antibody treatment is now available in some locations and may be used alongside standard supportive care. It is designed to target the virus and may help reduce disease severity, particularly when administered early. Ask your veterinarian whether this treatment is appropriate and available for your dog. 

## Can Parvo Be Treated at Home?

Some dogs may be candidates for outpatient treatment if they are stable, can be monitored closely, and their owners can follow a strict veterinary plan. Outpatient care may include scheduled fluid therapy, medications, nutrition, and daily reassessments.

However, home treatment is not appropriate for every dog. Hospitalization is more likely to be recommended when a dog has:

- Severe dehydration
- Uncontrolled vomiting
- Bloody diarrhea
- Low blood sugar
- Abnormal electrolytes
- A very low white blood cell count
- Signs of shock or sepsis
- Extreme weakness or collapse

Never attempt to manage parvo with over-the-counter human medications. Some medications can worsen intestinal damage or be dangerous for puppies.

## What Is the Survival Rate for Dogs With Parvo?

A parvo diagnosis is serious, but many dogs recover with prompt and aggressive veterinary treatment. Reported survival rates are commonly highest for dogs receiving intensive hospital care, with some veterinary sources reporting survival above 90% in appropriately treated cases.  

Prognosis depends on several factors, including:

- The dog’s age and size
- How quickly treatment begins
- Degree of dehydration
- White blood cell count
- Blood glucose and electrolyte levels
- Presence of sepsis or other complications
- Ability to tolerate nutrition
- Access to continuous monitoring

The first several days are often the most critical. Dogs that survive the early acute stage commonly begin improving within a few days, although diarrhea, weakness, and weight loss may continue for a while.

Potential complications include sepsis, intestinal blockage from intussusception, low blood sugar, pneumonia, electrolyte abnormalities, and blood-clotting problems. This is why veterinary monitoring is so important.

## How Long Is a Dog With Parvo Contagious?

A dog with parvo may be contagious before symptoms are obvious and can continue shedding virus after appearing to recover. Veterinary guidance commonly recommends strict isolation during treatment and for up to two weeks after recovery, although the exact period may vary.  

Keep an infected dog completely separate from other dogs. Use different bowls, bedding, toys, cleaning supplies, and outdoor areas whenever possible.

Wash your hands after handling the sick dog, and change clothing and footwear before interacting with healthy puppies or unvaccinated dogs. Although people do not become infected with canine parvovirus, they can mechanically carry the virus to other dogs. 

## How to Clean Your Home After Parvo

Cleaning must begin by removing feces, vomit, dirt, and other organic material. Disinfectants work poorly when organic debris remains on the surface.

For hard, washable indoor surfaces, your veterinarian may recommend appropriately diluted household bleach or a veterinary disinfectant labeled effective against parvovirus. One commonly cited bleach dilution is approximately 1 part bleach to 30 or 32 parts water, with adequate contact time after cleaning.  

Important cleaning steps include:

1. Remove all feces and organic material.
2. Wash the surface with detergent and water.
3. Apply an effective parvovirus disinfectant.
4. Allow the product to remain wet for the label-recommended contact time.
5. Rinse when appropriate and allow the area to dry.
6. Wash or discard contaminated bedding, toys, and soft items.
7. Clean floors, crates, door handles, bowls, and frequently touched surfaces.

Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaning products. Follow the product label and keep pets away from wet disinfectants.

Outdoor areas are much harder to disinfect. Pick up feces immediately, prevent unvaccinated puppies from entering contaminated areas, and ask your veterinarian how long the space should remain restricted.

## Preventing Parvovirus in Dogs

Vaccination is the most effective way to prevent parvo. Canine parvovirus is considered a core vaccine recommended for dogs regardless of lifestyle.  

A typical puppy vaccination plan begins at approximately 6 to 8 weeks of age. Vaccines are generally repeated every 2 to 4 weeks until at least 16 weeks of age. In high-risk situations, your veterinarian may recommend continuing the series to 18 or 20 weeks.  

Adult dogs generally receive a booster within one year after the initial puppy series, followed by additional boosters according to veterinary guidelines and the vaccine product’s instructions.

Until your puppy has completed the core vaccine series:

- Avoid dog parks and heavily trafficked dog areas.
- Do not allow your puppy to sniff unknown feces.
- Avoid contact with sick or unvaccinated dogs.
- Choose puppy classes that require vaccines and use strong hygiene practices.
- Ask your veterinarian about safe, controlled socialization.
- Keep your puppy away from shelter or kennel environments unless necessary.
- Maintain regular veterinary appointments.

Socialization is still important for a young puppy, but it should occur in controlled, low-risk settings approved by your veterinarian.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Parvo in Dogs

### Can adult dogs get parvo?

Yes. Adult dogs can develop parvovirus, especially if they are unvaccinated, incompletely vaccinated, or have an unknown vaccination history. Properly vaccinated adult dogs are much better protected, but no vaccine provides an absolute guarantee.

### Can humans catch parvo from dogs?

No. Canine parvovirus does not infect people. It is different from human parvovirus B19, which causes a human illness commonly known as fifth disease. However, people can carry canine parvovirus on their hands, shoes, or clothing and spread it to other dogs. 

### Is bloody diarrhea always a sign of parvo?

No. Parvo may cause bloody diarrhea, but some infected dogs have watery diarrhea without visible blood. Other illnesses, including parasites, poisoning, intestinal obstruction, and bacterial infections, can cause similar symptoms. Veterinary testing is needed.

### Can a vaccinated puppy still get parvo?

It is possible, particularly before the puppy completes the vaccination series. Maternal antibodies may interfere with early vaccine doses, which is why puppies receive repeated vaccinations until at least 16 weeks of age. 

### How long does parvo last in dogs?

The most severe illness often occurs over several days. Dogs that respond to treatment may begin improving within 3 to 4 days and recover over approximately a week, although weakness and digestive changes can last longer. Recovery time varies by dog and disease severity.

### Should I give my dog food or water if I suspect parvo?

Do not force food or water if your dog is repeatedly vomiting. Contact a veterinarian immediately for instructions. A dog with parvo may need professional fluid therapy, anti-nausea medication, and a carefully managed nutrition plan.

### Can a dog get parvo twice?

Dogs that recover generally develop long-lasting, possibly lifelong immunity. However, a dog that appears to have recovered from vomiting and diarrhea may still spread virus for a period, so isolation and sanitation remain important.

## Final Thoughts

Parvovirus in dogs is dangerous, fast-moving, and highly contagious, but it is also largely preventable. Keeping your dog’s vaccinations current, avoiding contaminated environments, and practicing careful hygiene are the best ways to reduce the risk.

If your dog develops vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, or loss of appetite—especially a puppy—treat the situation as urgent. Call your veterinarian before visiting the clinic so staff can prepare for possible parvovirus exposure. Early diagnosis, aggressive supportive care, and strict isolation give your dog the strongest chance of recovery while protecting other dogs in your household and community.
