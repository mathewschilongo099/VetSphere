# Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is one of the most serious viral diseases affecting small ruminants worldwide. It is highly contagious, spreads rapidly through herds, and can cause very high death rates if not controlled. Goats often show more severe illness than sheep, and outbreaks can wipe out most of the goat population in a village or flock, especially in Africa and Asia.  Because goats are a major source of meat, milk, and income for smallholder farmers, this disease has a direct impact on family livelihoods and food security.  

Understanding the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and especially the prevention of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is essential for farmers, veterinary students, and animal health workers. International organizations such as FAO and WOAH (formerly OIE) are working toward global eradication of the disease by 2030, mainly through vaccination and improved surveillance.  Good farm-level knowledge and rapid action are key parts of this global effort.

## What is Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats?

Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), also known as “goat plague” or “sheep and goat plague,” is an acute or subacute viral disease of goats and sheep caused by the PPR virus (PPRV), a Morbillivirus in the family Paramyxoviridae.  The virus is closely related to rinderpest virus, measles virus, and canine distemper virus.  PPR is listed as a notifiable transboundary animal disease by WOAH because of its high contagiousness, severe economic impact, and ability to spread across borders.  

Goats and sheep are both susceptible to PPR, but goats generally show more severe disease and higher death rates.  The infection mainly affects the respiratory and digestive systems, causing fever, mouth lesions, nasal and eye discharges, severe diarrhea, and pneumonia.  Without control, morbidity in affected herds can approach 100%, and mortality can reach 50–70% or more, especially in naïve populations where animals have never been exposed or vaccinated.  

PPR is widespread in many parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, including regions where small ruminants are the main livestock species.  It is considered one of the most damaging small ruminant diseases because it reduces productivity (milk, growth, fertility), increases deaths and abortions, and leads to major costs for disease control and trade losses. 

## Causes of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats





![Causes of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1524024973431-2ad916746881?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxQZXN0ZSUyMGRlcyUyMFBldGl0cyUyMFJ1bWluYW50cyUyMGluJTIwR29hdHMlMjBkaXNlYXNlfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NTM5NjF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Causes — via Unsplash*


The primary cause of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is infection with the PPR virus, transmitted mainly by close contact between infected and susceptible animals. The virus is excreted in high amounts in nasal secretions, saliva, tears, and feces of sick animals.  Transmission usually occurs through aerosols (small droplets released during coughing and sneezing) and direct contact, especially when goats are housed or grazed in close groups.  

Indirect spread also plays an important role on farms and in markets. The virus can contaminate bedding, feed troughs, water points, and clothing or equipment, and then infect other goats when they come into contact with these contaminated “fomites.”  Movement of infected animals—especially trading or communal grazing—rapidly introduces PPR to new villages, districts, and even countries.

Risk factors that increase the chance of PPR outbreaks in goats include:

- High animal density and overcrowded housing  
- Frequent movement of animals (markets, fairs, transhumance)  
- Mixing of flocks from different areas  
- Poor biosecurity and quarantine practices  
- Lack of vaccination or incomplete vaccination coverage  

Because goats are often owned by poorer smallholder farmers in harsh climates, they are frequently moved in search of pasture and water, which creates ideal conditions for further spread of the virus. 

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats





![Symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1593750439808-958d28558592?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxQZXN0ZSUyMGRlcyUyMFBldGl0cyUyMFJ1bWluYW50cyUyMGluJTIwR29hdHMlMjBzaWNrJTIwYW5pbWFsfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NTM5NjF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Clinical Signs — via Unsplash*


The disease usually starts suddenly, after an incubation period of about 3–10 days, with high fever (pyrexia).  Early symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats may be mild and easily overlooked, but they progress rapidly to a characteristic clinical picture.

Typical symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats include:

- High fever  
- Depression, loss of appetite, and weakness  
- Watery eye discharge (tearing) and nasal discharge that later becomes thick and purulent  
- Conjunctivitis (red, inflamed eyes)  
- Coughing and difficult breathing as pneumonia develops  

Lesions in the mouth are a classic feature. Goats develop erosive and necrotizing stomatitis—erosions and ulcers on the gums, lips, dental pad, and inner cheeks. These lesions are painful, so goats salivate excessively, grind their teeth, and refuse to eat or drink.  This quickly leads to weight loss and dehydration.

The virus also attacks the gastrointestinal tract, causing enteritis and severe diarrhea, which may be watery and foul-smelling and sometimes contain mucus or blood.  Diarrhea, combined with reduced water intake and persistent fever, often leads to severe dehydration and death, especially in young or stressed animals. Concurrent pneumonia (bronchopneumonia) causes labored breathing, nasal sounds, and can lead to sudden death in advanced stages.  

In pregnant goats, PPR infection can result in abortions and stillbirths.  In peracute cases, goats may die before showing full mouth or intestinal lesions, which can make clinical recognition more difficult. Some animals may survive but remain weak and stunted. Endemic areas also report subclinical or milder infections, particularly in partly immune flocks, but these animals can still shed virus and help maintain disease in the population. 

## How to Diagnose Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats

In the field, presumptive diagnosis is usually based on the history and the combination of clinical signs: sudden onset of fever, eye and nasal discharges, erosive mouth lesions, severe diarrhea, and pneumonia in multiple goats within a short time.  High morbidity and mortality in unvaccinated flocks, especially with goat-to-goat spread, strongly suggest PPR.

However, other diseases can produce similar symptoms, such as rinderpest (historically), contagious caprine pleuropneumonia, bluetongue, foot-and-mouth disease, and severe parasitism or bacterial enteritis. For this reason, laboratory tests are necessary to confirm the diagnosis, especially during outbreaks, for trade, and for eradication programs.  

Common laboratory methods used to diagnose Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats include:

- **RT-PCR (reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction):** Detects PPR viral RNA from swabs (nasal, ocular, oral), blood, or tissues. Real-time RT-PCR assays targeting the N or M gene are highly sensitive and specific and are now standard tests in many reference labs. 
- **ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay):** Antigen-capture ELISA detects viral antigen in samples, while antibody ELISA detects antibodies in serum for surveillance and post-vaccination monitoring. 
- **LAMP (loop-mediated isothermal amplification):** A newer, field-friendly molecular test that can detect PPR viral nucleic acid rapidly without complex equipment.  

In addition, pathologists may use post-mortem examination to look for typical lesions: erosions in the mouth and digestive tract, congestion of the lungs, and enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes, together with histopathology changes typical of Morbillivirus infections.  For national programs, diagnosis also follows guidance from WOAH’s Manual of Diagnostic Tests for Terrestrial Animals.

## Treatment of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats





![Treatment for Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1524024973431-2ad916746881?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxQZXN0ZSUyMGRlcyUyMFBldGl0cyUyMFJ1bWluYW50cyUyMGluJTIwR29hdHMlMjB2ZXRlcmluYXJ5fGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODE5NTM5NjF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Treatment — via Unsplash*


There is **no specific antiviral drug** that kills the PPR virus in goats. Treatment of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is therefore mainly **supportive care**, aimed at reducing suffering, preventing secondary bacterial infections, and improving the chances of survival while the animal’s immune system fights the virus.  

Supportive measures commonly used by veterinarians include:

- **Fluid therapy:** Oral or parenteral fluids and electrolytes to correct dehydration caused by diarrhea and reduced water intake.  
- **Broad-spectrum antibiotics:** To control or prevent secondary bacterial pneumonia and septicemia that frequently complicate PPR.  
- **Anti-inflammatory and antipyretic drugs:** To reduce fever and pain, improve comfort, and encourage food intake.  
- **Nutritional support:** Offering soft, palatable feeds and encouraging water consumption; sometimes using vitamin and mineral supplements to support recovery.  

Despite intensive care, case fatality in affected, unvaccinated goats can still be high, particularly in young animals, pregnant does, or severely debilitated goats. Because the virus is highly contagious, isolation of sick animals from healthy ones during treatment is essential to reduce spread within the flock. Quarantine of affected groups for at least several weeks after the last case is recommended.  

From a herd health point of view, the economic importance of PPR means that **prevention and vaccination** are much more effective and cost‑efficient than relying on treatment once an outbreak has started. 

## Prevention and Control of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats





![Prevention of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1524024973431-2ad916746881?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxQZXN0ZSUyMGRlcyUyMFBldGl0cyUyMFJ1bWluYW50cyUyMGluJTIwR29hdHMlMjBmYXJtJTIwcHJldmVudGlvbnxlbnwwfDB8fHwxNzgxOTUzOTYxfDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)




*Photo: Prevention — via Unsplash*


Prevention of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is based on a combination of **vaccination**, **biosecurity**, and **movement control**. Because PPR is highly contagious and causes large losses, international agencies have set a goal to globally eradicate the disease by 2030 through these measures. 

### Vaccination

Highly effective live attenuated vaccines against PPR virus are available and widely used in endemic countries. These homologous PPR vaccines provide strong, often lifelong immunity after a single dose in susceptible sheep and goats.  They are the core tool in national and regional control programs and are being used in mass vaccination campaigns to reduce virus circulation and protect at-risk populations.  

Key vaccination points:

- Target all susceptible goats and sheep in an area, especially young stock over the minimum vaccination age recommended by manufacturers.  
- Aim for high coverage (often 70–80% or more of the population) to achieve herd immunity and stop virus spread. 
- Repeat vaccination according to national guidelines, vaccine stability, and local risk (for example, annual or strategic campaigns in high‑risk zones).  
- Avoid using old rinderpest vaccines: after global rinderpest eradication, heterologous rinderpest vaccines are no longer recommended due to interference with surveillance and the absence of need. 

### Biosecurity and Management

Good farm management practices significantly lower the risk of introducing or spreading PPR in goats:

- Quarantine newly purchased or returning goats for at least 2–3 weeks before mixing with the main flock.  
- Avoid mixing your flock with unknown or unvaccinated animals at markets, watering points, and communal grazing when possible.  
- Isolate sick animals immediately and manage them last during daily routines to reduce contamination.  
- Clean and disinfect housing, troughs, and equipment regularly, especially following illness or deaths.  
- Dispose of carcasses safely (burial or incineration) and avoid feeding carcasses or offal to dogs or other animals. 

### Movement Control and Surveillance

At the regional and national level, control of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats requires:

- Rapid reporting of suspected cases to veterinary authorities.  
- Temporary movement restrictions around outbreak areas.  
- Tracing and monitoring of animal movements from affected markets or farms.  
- Active surveillance, including sampling and laboratory testing, to detect and map virus circulation.  

WOAH and FAO have developed a staged pathway—assessment, control, eradication, and post‑eradication—to guide countries from endemic status toward PPR‑free status through coordinated vaccination, surveillance, and biosecurity measures. 

## Frequently Asked Questions About Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats

### Q: What is Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats?

**A:** Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is a highly contagious viral disease caused by the PPR virus, affecting mainly goats and sheep. It produces high fever, eye and nasal discharges, mouth ulcers, severe diarrhea, and pneumonia, and can cause very high death rates in unvaccinated flocks. 

### Q: How does Peste des Petits Ruminants spread between goats?

**A:** The disease spreads mainly through close contact with infected animals. The virus is shed in nasal secretions, saliva, eye discharge, and feces, and is transmitted via aerosols from coughing and sneezing, direct contact, and contaminated bedding, feed, and water. Movement of infected goats through markets or shared grazing also spreads the virus to new areas. 

### Q: What are the main symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats?

**A:** Common symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats include high fever, depression, loss of appetite, watery then thick eye and nose discharge, erosive mouth lesions, excessive salivation, severe diarrhea, dehydration, coughing, and difficult breathing due to pneumonia. In pregnant goats, abortions may occur. 

### Q: How to treat Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats?

**A:** There is no specific antiviral treatment. Management focuses on supportive care: fluids and electrolytes to correct dehydration, broad‑spectrum antibiotics to prevent or treat secondary bacterial infections, anti‑inflammatory drugs to reduce fever and pain, and good nursing care, including soft feed and clean water. Even with treatment, many severely affected goats may die, so prevention by vaccination is crucial. 

### Q: How can I prevent Peste des Petits Ruminants in my goats?

**A:** Prevention of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats relies on regular vaccination of all susceptible goats and sheep, maintaining high flock coverage, strict quarantine of new animals, avoiding contact with unknown or sick flocks, immediate isolation of sick goats, proper cleaning and disinfection, and quick reporting of suspected cases to veterinarians or authorities. These steps help protect your herd and support the wider eradication effort. 

### Q: Is Peste des Petits Ruminants dangerous to humans?

**A:** There is no evidence that Peste des Petits Ruminants infects humans. It is a major threat to animal health and livelihoods, but it is not regarded as a zoonotic disease like some other viral infections. 

## When to Call a Veterinarian

You should contact a veterinarian immediately if you observe sudden onset of fever, eye and nasal discharge, mouth lesions, and diarrhea affecting several goats in your flock within a few days. Rapid spread and high sickness rates in goats, especially in unvaccinated herds, strongly suggest PPR or another serious transboundary disease that needs urgent attention.  

A veterinarian will perform a thorough clinical examination, collect appropriate samples such as nasal and ocular swabs or blood, and arrange laboratory testing (PCR, ELISA, or other methods) to confirm Peste des Petits Ruminants or rule out other conditions. Early confirmation allows veterinary services to implement movement control, targeted vaccination, and other measures to protect surrounding farms.  

You should also call a veterinarian to discuss vaccination plans if PPR is present in your region or if you plan to buy or move goats across districts or borders. Proper vaccination schedules and record‑keeping not only protect your herd but also support national control and eradication programs. Veterinarians can also advise on biosecurity, quarantine, and improved management to reduce future disease risks. 

## Conclusion

Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats is a devastating but preventable viral disease of small ruminants. Caused by a Morbillivirus closely related to rinderpest and measles, it spreads quickly through close contact and contaminated environments, leading to high sickness and death rates, especially in goats.  The typical combination of fever, ocular and nasal discharges, painful mouth ulcers, severe diarrhea, and pneumonia in multiple animals should raise immediate suspicion on any farm.

Because there is no specific antiviral treatment, supportive care and good nursing are the only therapeutic options for sick goats, and many may still die.  The real solution lies in prevention of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats through widespread use of effective PPR vaccines, strong biosecurity practices, rapid diagnosis, and coordinated surveillance and movement control.  

Global strategies led by FAO and WOAH aim to eradicate PPR by 2030, relying on the same principles that successfully eliminated rinderpest from cattle.  At the farm level, farmers, technicians, and veterinarians all play crucial roles: recognizing early symptoms of Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats, reporting suspected outbreaks, vaccinating goats and sheep regularly, and improving management and hygiene. With these combined efforts, PPR can move from being a major threat to smallholder goat production to a disease of the past.
