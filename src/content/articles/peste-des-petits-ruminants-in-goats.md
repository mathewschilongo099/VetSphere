Peste des petits ruminants in goats is a serious, highly contagious viral disease that can spread rapidly through a herd. Often called **PPR**, **goat plague**, or **sheep and goat plague**, the disease causes fever, painful mouth lesions, eye and nose discharge, diarrhea, pneumonia, dehydration, and death.

PPR can affect sheep and several wild ruminant species, but goats often develop particularly severe disease. In some outbreaks, almost every susceptible goat may become ill, and mortality can be extremely high. Young goats are especially vulnerable.  

If you suspect peste des petits ruminants in your goats, treat it as an emergency. Immediately isolate sick animals, stop animal movement, and contact a veterinarian or government veterinary authority.

## What Is Peste des Petits Ruminants in Goats?

Peste des petits ruminants is an acute viral infection caused by **peste des petits ruminants virus**, or PPRV. The virus belongs to the *Morbillivirus* group and is related to—but distinct from—the virus that caused rinderpest. PPR mainly damages the respiratory and digestive systems.  

The disease is found across parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. It can cause major losses for smallholder farmers because affected goats may die quickly, while surviving animals may suffer weight loss, reduced milk production, abortion, and prolonged recovery.

PPR is also an internationally notifiable animal disease. This means suspected cases must be reported according to local regulations. The disease does not infect humans, but people can accidentally spread contaminated material between groups of goats through clothing, equipment, vehicles, and hands. 

## How Does PPR Spread Between Goats?

PPR spreads mainly through close contact with infected animals. The virus is present in secretions and excretions, including discharge from the eyes, nose, and mouth, as well as feces and respiratory droplets.

Common routes of transmission include:

- Breathing in droplets from coughing or sneezing goats
- Direct contact with infected goats
- Shared feed and water containers
- Contaminated clothing, footwear, vehicles, and equipment
- Transporting goats from infected markets or areas
- Mixing newly purchased goats with the existing herd
- Contact with nomadic or migratory flocks
- Contact with susceptible wild ruminants

Crowded markets, livestock transport, communal grazing areas, and poorly ventilated sheds increase the risk of transmission. The virus generally does not survive for long outside the host, but contaminated materials can contribute to short-distance spread.  

Goats may begin showing signs within approximately **2 to 6 days after infection**, although the exact incubation period can vary. 

## Early Signs of PPR in Goats

The first signs of PPR may look similar to other infections. Careful observation is important, especially when several goats become sick at the same time.

Early symptoms may include:

- Sudden high fever
- Depression and weakness
- Loss of appetite
- Reluctance to move
- Rough or dull hair coat
- Dry muzzle
- Reddened eyes and mouth tissues
- Watery discharge from the eyes and nose

Fever may reach approximately **40°C to 41°C**. A goat may appear sleepy, separate itself from the herd, or stand with its head lowered. The watery discharge often becomes thicker and yellowish as the disease progresses and secondary bacterial infection develops. 

## Mouth Lesions and Excessive Salivation

One of the most important signs of PPR in goats is painful inflammation and erosion inside the mouth.

Small gray or red areas may first appear on the gums, lips, dental pad, tongue, palate, and inside the cheeks. These lesions can enlarge, join together, and develop into shallow ulcers covered by dead tissue. The goat may drool excessively, resist having its mouth opened, or stop eating because chewing is painful.

You may notice:

- Swollen or cracked lips
- Foul-smelling breath
- Excessive salivation
- Gray, white, or yellow material in the mouth
- Redness and ulcers on the gums
- Difficulty eating or drinking
- Weight loss and weakness

Mouth lesions can be easy to miss during the early stages. However, they are especially significant when combined with fever, nasal discharge, diarrhea, or breathing problems. 

## Diarrhea, Dehydration, and Weight Loss

Diarrhea commonly develops a few days after the fever begins. It may be watery, foul-smelling, and occasionally blood-stained. In severe cases, feces may contain pieces of damaged intestinal tissue.

Diarrhea can quickly cause dehydration, particularly in young goats. Watch for:

- Sunken eyes
- Dry gums
- Weakness or inability to stand
- Reduced skin elasticity
- Soiled hindquarters
- Rapid weight loss
- Reduced urination
- Cold extremities in advanced disease

Dehydration and pneumonia are major causes of death in goats with PPR. Some animals die within approximately 5 to 10 days after the onset of fever, while others recover after a prolonged period of weakness and poor body condition. 

## Respiratory Signs and Pneumonia

PPR may cause respiratory disease that becomes more severe as the infection progresses. Affected goats may cough, breathe rapidly, or struggle to breathe.

Severe respiratory signs include:

- Persistent coughing
- Fast breathing
- Noisy breathing
- Abdominal effort when breathing
- Extended head and neck
- Open-mouth breathing
- Frothy or thick nasal discharge
- Difficulty lying down

Secondary bacterial pneumonia may develop because PPR damages the respiratory tract and weakens the immune system. In advanced cases, pneumonia may be the immediate cause of death.  

Pregnant goats may abort, and goats that survive may experience a long recovery period. Milk production can fall sharply during illness.

## How Severe Is PPR in Goats?

The severity of PPR depends on the goat’s age, immune status, breed, nutritional condition, vaccination history, and the strain of virus involved.

Young goats and unvaccinated animals are generally at greatest risk. In severe outbreaks, morbidity may approach 100%, while mortality can range from moderate to extremely high. Some official animal health sources report mortality rates of 50% to 100% in affected populations.  

The disease may be:

- **Peracute:** Sudden fever, severe depression, and rapid death before classic signs develop
- **Acute:** Fever followed by mouth lesions, discharge, diarrhea, pneumonia, and death
- **Subacute:** Milder illness with intermittent diarrhea, nasal discharge, and gradual recovery
- **Subclinical:** Infection with few obvious signs but possible spread or internal lesions

A herd may not show identical symptoms in every goat. Some animals may have severe diarrhea, while others primarily show pneumonia or oral lesions.

## Is PPR the Same as Orf or Goat Pneumonia?

No. Several diseases can resemble PPR, so laboratory confirmation is important.

### Orf

Orf, also called contagious ecthyma, commonly causes scabs and nodules around the lips and mouth. Unlike typical PPR, uncomplicated orf generally does not cause severe diarrhea, pneumonia, or widespread oral necrosis. Orf can infect people, while PPR is not known to infect humans. 

### Contagious caprine pleuropneumonia

Contagious caprine pleuropneumonia, or CCPP, causes fever, coughing, and difficult breathing in goats. However, mouth erosions and severe diarrhea are not typical features of CCPP. 

### Pneumonic pasteurellosis

Pasteurellosis is primarily a respiratory disease. Goats with pasteurellosis generally do not have the combination of severe mouth lesions and diarrhea that suggests PPR. 

### Foot-and-mouth disease

Foot-and-mouth disease can cause mouth lesions, but lameness and foot lesions are more characteristic. Severe respiratory disease and diarrhea are not typical features of FMD in the same way they are in PPR.  

Because these diseases require different control measures, do not rely on visual diagnosis alone.

## How Is PPR Diagnosed?

A veterinarian may suspect PPR based on the pattern of disease in the herd, clinical signs, age of affected goats, recent animal movements, and mortality rate. However, a field diagnosis is only provisional.

Definitive diagnosis usually involves laboratory testing such as:

- Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR
- Antigen detection tests
- Immunocapture enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
- Virus isolation
- Serological testing for antibodies

Samples may include nasal, ocular, oral, or rectal swabs, blood, serum, and tissues collected after death. Testing is important because antibodies may result from vaccination as well as natural infection.  

Do not open carcasses or collect samples without veterinary guidance. Improper handling can spread infection and may expose people to contaminated material, even though PPR itself is not considered a human disease.

## Is There a Treatment for PPR in Goats?

There is **no specific antiviral treatment that cures PPR**. Treatment is supportive and should be directed by a veterinarian.  

Supportive care may include:

- Providing clean water frequently
- Offering soft, palatable feed
- Correcting dehydration with appropriate fluids
- Keeping goats warm, dry, and protected from drafts
- Cleaning discharge from the eyes and nostrils
- Treating secondary bacterial infections when indicated
- Controlling parasitic or other concurrent infections
- Separating weak goats from aggressive herd mates

Antibiotics do not kill PPRV. A veterinarian may prescribe them only when secondary bacterial pneumonia or another bacterial complication is suspected. Avoid using leftover antibiotics or human medicines without professional advice.

Sick goats should be monitored frequently. A goat that cannot drink, has severe breathing difficulty, collapses, or develops profound dehydration requires urgent veterinary attention.

## What to Do if You Suspect PPR

Take immediate action:

1. **Isolate sick goats** from healthy animals.
2. **Stop buying, selling, moving, or transporting goats.**
3. **Contact a veterinarian or animal health authority immediately.**
4. **Do not take sick goats to a market or communal grazing area.**
5. **Use separate equipment, clothing, and footwear** for the isolation area.
6. **Clean and disinfect housing, tools, troughs, and vehicles.**
7. **Record the date signs began and the number of sick and dead goats.**
8. **Identify recent animal purchases, visitors, markets, and movements.**
9. **Dispose of carcasses according to veterinary and local regulations.**
10. **Follow official testing, quarantine, vaccination, and movement-control instructions.**

Do not wait for several goats to die before reporting. Early notification can help authorities contain an outbreak and protect nearby herds.

## Preventing PPR in Goats

Vaccination is the most important long-term method for preventing PPR in goats. Live attenuated PPR vaccines are widely used in endemic areas and can provide strong protection. Vaccine availability, timing, age recommendations, and booster requirements vary by country, so follow your veterinarian’s local vaccination program.  

Good prevention also includes:

- Vaccinating the whole at-risk goat population when recommended
- Prioritizing young goats and high-risk herds
- Quarantining newly purchased goats for at least 2 to 3 weeks
- Buying animals only from reliable, traceable sources
- Avoiding contact with unknown or migratory flocks
- Disinfecting vehicles and equipment
- Preventing overcrowding
- Improving ventilation
- Limiting visitors in animal areas
- Keeping accurate health and vaccination records
- Reporting unusual illness or deaths promptly

Vaccination is not a substitute for biosecurity. A vaccinated herd can still face risks if infected animals are introduced before immunity develops or if vaccine storage and administration are inadequate.

## Frequently Asked Questions About PPR in Goats

### Can humans catch peste des petits ruminants from goats?

No. PPRV is an animal virus, and there are no known reports of human infection. Nevertheless, people can carry contaminated material between goat groups, so protective clothing, handwashing, cleaning, and disinfection remain important. 

### Can PPR affect sheep as well as goats?

Yes. PPR primarily affects goats and sheep. Both species can develop severe disease, although goats may show more dramatic clinical signs in some outbreaks. 

### What is the first sign of PPR in a goat?

A sudden fever with depression and loss of appetite is often among the first signs. Watery eye and nose discharge may follow, then mouth lesions, diarrhea, and respiratory problems. 

### Can a goat recover from PPR?

Some goats recover, particularly in milder or subacute cases. Recovery may take weeks, and animals can remain weak, thin, or prone to secondary infections. Severe disease may result in death within days. 

### Do antibiotics cure PPR?

No. Antibiotics do not treat the virus. A veterinarian may use them to manage secondary bacterial infections, such as pneumonia, but they cannot replace vaccination, isolation, reporting, and movement control.

### How can I protect newly purchased goats?

Purchase goats from a trusted source, check their vaccination history, and quarantine them separately from your herd for at least 2 to 3 weeks. Monitor their temperature, appetite, breathing, feces, eyes, nose, and mouth during quarantine.

### Should I vaccinate goats during an outbreak?

Outbreak vaccination may be recommended by veterinary authorities, sometimes in a targeted or “ring vaccination” program around affected herds. Do not make an outbreak vaccination plan independently; follow official instructions regarding which animals should be vaccinated and when.

## Conclusion

Peste des petits ruminants in goats is a fast-spreading and potentially fatal disease. Fever, eye and nose discharge, painful mouth erosions, foul diarrhea, coughing, pneumonia, dehydration, and sudden deaths should always raise suspicion—especially when several goats become sick within a short period.

There is no specific cure for PPR, so prevention is essential. Vaccination, quarantine, responsible animal purchasing, movement control, hygiene, and rapid reporting provide the strongest protection for your herd. If you see symptoms consistent with PPR, isolate affected goats and contact a veterinarian immediately.
