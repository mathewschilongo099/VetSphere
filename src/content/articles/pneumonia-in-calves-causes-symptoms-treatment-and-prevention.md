# Pneumonia in Calves: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Pneumonia in Calves is one of the most common and costly health problems in beef and dairy herds worldwide. It is a serious respiratory disease that affects the lungs and airways of young animals, reducing growth, increasing treatment costs, and causing death in severe cases.  For farmers, students, and animal owners, understanding the symptoms of Pneumonia in Calves, how to treat Pneumonia in Calves, and how to prevent it is essential for good herd health and profitability.

Calf pneumonia is usually not caused by a single factor. Instead, it results from a combination of infectious agents (viruses and bacteria), poor housing, stress, and inadequate immunity.  Early recognition and prompt treatment greatly improve survival and reduce long‑term lung damage, while well‑planned prevention programs can significantly reduce the number of sick calves each year.  

This article explains what Pneumonia in Calves is, the main causes and risk factors, clinical signs, diagnosis, treatment options, and practical prevention and control strategies for your farm.

## What is Pneumonia in Calves?

Pneumonia in Calves is an inflammation and infection of the lower respiratory tract, including the bronchi and lung tissue. The disease interferes with normal gas exchange, making it harder for the calf to breathe and to get sufficient oxygen into the bloodstream.  This leads to reduced growth rates, poor feed conversion, and increased risk of death.

Calf pneumonia is often part of what veterinarians call bovine respiratory disease (BRD) complex. It frequently involves a combination of viral pathogens (such as bovine respiratory syncytial virus – BRSV, infectious bovine rhinotracheitis – IBR, bovine viral diarrhea – BVD) and bacterial pathogens (including Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni, and Mycoplasma bovis).  These agents damage the respiratory tract and lungs, trigger inflammation, and can cause permanent scarring.

Pneumonia can occur in both pre‑weaning and post‑weaning calves. Suckling calves may develop pneumonia due to inadequate colostrum intake, poor environment, and viral infections, while weaned or recently transported calves are at particular risk from stress, mixing with unfamiliar animals, and exposure to new pathogens.  Regardless of age, early detection and decisive action are key to avoid chronic disease and lifetime stunting.

## Causes of Pneumonia in Calves

Understanding the causes of Pneumonia in Calves helps you target both treatment and prevention. The disease is multifactorial and usually involves three main components: infectious agents, the calf’s immunity, and environmental or management stressors. 

### Infectious agents

Common viral causes include BRSV, IBR, BVD, and parainfluenza‑3 (PI3). These viruses damage the cells lining the airways and interfere with local immune defenses, making it easier for bacteria to invade.  Viral infections often start the problem and are then followed by bacterial pneumonia.

Frequently involved bacteria include Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni, and Mycoplasma bovis.  Mannheimia and Pasteurella can produce toxins that cause rapid and severe lung damage, leading to high fevers and acute respiratory distress. Mycoplasma bovis is particularly associated with chronic, difficult‑to‑cure pneumonia, often accompanied by joint infections (arthritis).  

In rare cases, lungworm (Dictyocaulus viviparus) infections can trigger pneumonia in young calves, and these animals will not respond to antibiotic treatment.  This is why proper diagnosis and parasite control are important in any respiratory disease program.

### Calf immunity and colostrum management

The main underlying cause of many outbreaks is poor calf immunity. Calves are born with little natural protection and depend on high‑quality colostrum within the first hours of life to gain antibodies against respiratory pathogens.  Failure of passive transfer (inadequate colostrum) increases the risk of pneumonia, diarrhea, and other diseases.

Stressors such as poor nutrition, sudden diet changes, weaning, dehorning, and castration weaken the immune system and make calves more susceptible to respiratory infections.  Calves that are underfed or exposed to other diseases also have less energy to fight infections.

### Environmental and management risk factors

Environmental conditions often determine whether pathogens actually cause disease. Important risk factors include:

- Overcrowding in calf pens or sheds  
- Poor ventilation, high humidity, and accumulation of ammonia and dust  
- Draughts and chilling, or large temperature swings between day and night  
- Wet, dirty bedding and poor hygiene  
- Mixing calves of different ages and sources  

These conditions increase pathogen load in the air, stress calves, and damage the natural defenses of the respiratory tract.  In many herds, recurrent pneumonia reflects chronic problems with housing design and stock management rather than just “bad luck.”

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Pneumonia in Calves

Recognizing the symptoms of Pneumonia in Calves early is vital. Many calves develop fever and lung changes before obvious coughing or breathing difficulties appear, so close observation is essential. 

### Early and general signs

Initial signs are often mild and non‑specific. They can include:

- Reduced appetite or slower drinking  
- Mild depression or separation from the group  
- Slight drooping of ears or head carriage changes  
- Clear nasal discharge or watery eyes  

An elevated rectal temperature (fever) is often the first measurable sign, occurring 12–72 hours before more obvious clinical signs.  Monitoring calf temperatures in high‑risk groups helps detect pneumonia earlier.

### Respiratory signs

As the disease progresses, more typical respiratory symptoms appear:

- Increased respiratory rate (fast breathing)  
- Laboured or difficult breathing, sometimes with visible abdominal effort  
- Coughing, which may be frequent or intermittent  
- Nasal discharge that can change from clear to thick, mucopurulent (cloudy, yellow, or green)  
- Abnormal lung sounds on auscultation (crackles, wheezes) for the veterinarian  

Common clinical features listed by veterinary and industry sources include high temperature (often above 39.5–40°C or 103–104°F), coughing, nasal discharge, and lethargy.  Severely affected calves may lie down, be reluctant to rise, and show obvious respiratory distress, including open‑mouth breathing or frothy nasal discharge. 

### Chronic and severe cases

If pneumonia is not treated promptly or effectively, calves can develop chronic disease:

- Persistent cough for weeks  
- Poor body condition and stunted growth  
- Recurrent fever episodes  
- Nasal discharge that never fully clears  

Mycoplasma bovis pneumonia often presents as chronic respiratory signs, sometimes combined with swollen joints and lameness due to arthritis.  Calves that suffer repeated or severe bouts of pneumonia can end up permanently stunted, even if they appear outwardly healthy after recovery. 

## How to Diagnose Pneumonia in Calves

Accurate diagnosis of Pneumonia in Calves is essential for effective treatment and for designing a long‑term prevention plan. Diagnosis is based on a combination of history, clinical examination, and, where appropriate, laboratory tests. 

### Clinical examination

A veterinarian will first evaluate the calf’s general appearance, behaviour, body condition, and vital signs (temperature, heart rate, and respiratory rate). Fever, increased breathing rate, and depression strongly suggest systemic illness.  Using a stethoscope, the vet will listen to the lungs (auscultation). Crackling or harsh lung sounds and areas of reduced airflow support a diagnosis of pneumonia.  

In many cases, a pattern of multiple affected calves in the same group, with similar clinical signs, strongly indicates a respiratory disease outbreak such as calf pneumonia. 

### Laboratory and ancillary tests

To identify the specific pathogens involved and to guide targeted treatment, vets may use:

- Nasal swabs or deep nasopharyngeal swabs for PCR or culture of viruses and bacteria  
- Blood tests to assess inflammation and general health  
- Bronchoalveolar lavage samples (lung fluid) in special cases, especially for Mycoplasma bovis diagnosis 
- Lung ultrasound to detect early or subclinical pneumonia lesions before obvious signs appear  

Post‑mortem examination of calves that have died can also help identify the pattern and causes of pneumonia on a farm, revealing areas of lung consolidation, abscesses, or pleurisy. 

### Differentiating from other diseases

Not all coughing or nasal discharge is pneumonia. Your veterinarian will consider other causes such as:

- Viral respiratory infections without significant lung involvement  
- Lungworm infestation (particularly in grazing calves) 
- Heart problems or congenital defects  
- Non‑respiratory illnesses that cause fever and depression  

Because antibiotics are only effective against bacteria, incorrectly assuming all respiratory disease is bacterial pneumonia can lead to unnecessary and ineffective treatments.  Proper diagnosis ensures responsible antibiotic use and better outcomes.

## Treatment of Pneumonia in Calves

How to treat Pneumonia in Calves depends on the severity of disease, the likely pathogens, and the individual farm situation. However, some key principles apply in most cases: early treatment, appropriate drugs, correct dosing, and supportive care. 

### Antibiotic therapy

When bacteria are involved, timely antibiotic treatment can be life‑saving. Commonly used antibiotics in calf pneumonia include agents such as oxytetracycline, florfenicol, tulathromycin, and other products licensed for bovine respiratory disease.  The choice of drug, dose, and duration must always follow veterinary advice and label instructions.

Early initiation of antibiotic therapy greatly improves the chance of full recovery, while delayed or short courses increase the risk of chronic lung damage and relapse.  Many guidelines stress that treatment must be of adequate duration; stopping too soon, just because the calf looks better, can allow infection to re‑emerge.  

Antimicrobial resistance is a growing concern, so antibiotics should not be used indiscriminately or for minor, viral‑only infections.  Responsible use means basing treatment on veterinary diagnosis and, where possible, pathogen identification.

### Anti‑inflammatory and supportive therapy

Non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are often given alongside antibiotics to reduce fever, pain, and lung inflammation. This improves calf comfort, encourages feeding, and may help speed recovery.  

Supportive care is equally important:

- Isolate sick calves to limit disease spread and reduce competition  
- Provide warm, dry, well‑bedded housing with good ventilation but no draughts  
- Ensure easy access to high‑quality milk or milk replacer, plus clean water  
- Offer palatable starter concentrates and good forage to maintain energy intake  

Monitoring the calf’s temperature, respiratory rate, appetite, and general demeanour helps assess response to treatment.  Severely affected animals may need more intensive care depending on veterinary advice.

### Group treatment and metaphylaxis

In outbreaks where many calves are affected, it may be appropriate to treat all animals in the group, especially those showing early signs or fever, not just the obviously sick ones.  This approach (metaphylaxis) aims to prevent progression of subclinical cases and to reduce spread of infection.

Metaphylactic antibiotic use must be balanced against the need to reduce overall antibiotic consumption and should only be undertaken under veterinary guidance, particularly in high‑risk situations such as newly arrived, stressed feeder calves or where Mycoplasma pneumonia is common. 

## Prevention and Control of Pneumonia in Calves

The prevention of Pneumonia in Calves is far more effective and economical than relying solely on treatment. Good control programs address colostrum and nutrition, housing and ventilation, vaccination, and general herd management. 

### Colostrum, nutrition, and general health

Ensuring every calf receives an adequate volume of high‑quality colostrum as soon as possible after birth is the foundation of pneumonia prevention. This supports strong passive immunity against respiratory pathogens.  Routine monitoring of colostrum management and passive transfer status (serum total protein) can help identify problems early.

Balanced nutrition, including appropriate energy, protein, vitamins, and minerals, supports immune function and growth. Avoid sudden diet changes, starvation, or poor‑quality feed, as these weaken the calf and increase susceptibility to disease. 

### Housing and environmental management

Improving the environment is often the single most powerful step in the prevention of Pneumonia in Calves:

- Provide good ventilation to remove moisture, dust, and airborne pathogens without causing draughts. 
- Avoid overcrowding; follow recommended stocking densities for calf pens and hutches. 
- Keep housing dry with ample clean bedding to reduce chilling and ammonia buildup.  
- Separate calves by age group and avoid mixing animals from multiple sources without quarantine.  

Research and field experience show that well‑designed ventilation systems in calf barns markedly reduce pneumonia incidence by lowering the concentration of respiratory pathogens in the air.  Regular cleaning and disinfection of pens, feeding equipment, and bottles are also critical.

### Vaccination

Vaccination is an important tool in the prevention of Pneumonia in Calves, although it is not a complete solution. Vaccines are available against several key respiratory pathogens, including BRSV, IBR, BVD, Mannheimia haemolytica, and Pasteurella multocida.  A veterinarian‑designed vaccination schedule should be tailored to the specific risks and management system of each farm.

Young calves may respond less strongly to some vaccines than older animals, but carefully timed protocols (sometimes involving vaccination of the dam) can still reduce the severity and frequency of disease.  Vaccination must always be combined with improvements in housing and management, rather than used as a substitute.

### Biosecurity and herd‑level control

To prevent the introduction and spread of respiratory pathogens:

- Quarantine new or purchased calves before mixing with home‑reared animals. 
- Control visitors and vehicle movements and use proper hygiene when handling sick and healthy calves.  
- Implement all‑in/all‑out systems where possible to allow thorough cleaning and rest of facilities between groups.  

Regular review of pneumonia cases, mortality rates, and treatment records, together with post‑mortem findings and laboratory results, allows you and your veterinarian to refine your prevention strategy over time. 

## Frequently Asked Questions About Pneumonia in Calves

### Q: What are the earliest symptoms of Pneumonia in Calves?

**A:** Early symptoms often include a slight drop in appetite, reduced activity, mild nasal discharge, ear or head drooping, and fever (rectal temperature above about 39.5°C).  These may appear 1–3 days before obvious coughing or breathing problems.

### Q: How do you treat Pneumonia in Calves on the farm?

**A:** Treatment usually involves prompt antibiotic therapy when bacterial pneumonia is suspected, combined with anti‑inflammatory drugs for pain and fever, plus good nursing care and isolation of sick calves.  Always consult your veterinarian for drug choice, dosage, and treatment length.

### Q: Can Pneumonia in Calves be cured completely?

**A:** Many calves recover fully if pneumonia is detected and treated early and for a sufficient duration.  However, delayed or repeated episodes can cause permanent lung damage, leading to chronic poor performance and stunting, even if the calf appears clinically normal later. 

### Q: How can I prevent Pneumonia in Calves in my herd?

**A:** Effective prevention focuses on strong colostrum management, good nutrition, dry and well‑ventilated housing, avoiding overcrowding, and reducing stress at weaning or transport.  Vaccination against major respiratory viruses and bacteria, planned with your veterinarian, can further reduce the risk. 

### Q: When is pneumonia in calves an emergency?

**A:** Pneumonia is an emergency when calves show severe breathing difficulty, open‑mouth breathing, frothy nasal discharge, refusal to stand, or appear very depressed with high fever despite treatment.  Such cases require immediate veterinary attention and intensive care.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

You should contact a veterinarian as soon as you suspect Pneumonia in Calves. Early diagnosis greatly improves the chances of successful treatment and reduces lung damage and mortality.  At the first signs of fever, rapid breathing, coughing, or abnormal behaviour, it is better to seek advice than to “wait and see.”

Veterinary input is essential for:

- Confirming the diagnosis and ruling out other causes such as lungworm  
- Selecting the most appropriate antibiotic and anti‑inflammatory drugs, and setting the correct treatment duration  
- Deciding whether group treatment or metaphylaxis is appropriate in an outbreak 
- Designing and updating a vaccination program tailored to the herd’s specific pathogens and risk factors  

Your veterinarian can also help review housing, ventilation, and management practices to strengthen your overall prevention and control plan.  Regular collaboration and good record‑keeping are key to reducing the long‑term impact of Pneumonia in Calves on herd performance and farm profitability.

## Conclusion

Pneumonia in Calves is a major health and welfare issue that affects both dairy and beef operations, causing deaths, poor growth, and significant economic losses.  The disease usually arises from a complex interaction between viruses, bacteria, calf immunity, and environmental stressors, rather than from a single cause.

Recognizing the symptoms of Pneumonia in Calves early—particularly fever, increased respiratory rate, coughing, and depression—and seeking veterinary guidance on how to treat Pneumonia in Calves are critical steps for successful recovery.  At the same time, long‑term prevention relies on strong colostrum and nutrition programs, good housing and ventilation, vaccination, and careful management of stress and biosecurity.  

By combining timely treatment with a robust prevention and control strategy, farmers can greatly reduce the incidence and severity of Pneumonia in Calves, safeguard animal welfare, and improve the productivity and sustainability of their herds.
