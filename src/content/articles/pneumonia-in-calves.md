Pneumonia in calves is one of the most important health problems affecting dairy, beef, and veal operations. It can begin with a subtle cough or reduced appetite, then progress quickly to fever, labored breathing, permanent lung damage, or death.

Calf pneumonia is often called **bovine respiratory disease (BRD)**, **enzootic pneumonia**, or, in some situations, **shipping fever**. It is not caused by one organism or one management mistake. Instead, pneumonia usually develops when infectious pathogens, environmental conditions, stress, and reduced immunity combine to overwhelm a calf’s respiratory defenses.  

The good news is that you can reduce the risk substantially. Strong colostrum management, clean and well-ventilated housing, appropriate vaccination, good nutrition, low-stress handling, and early treatment all play an important role.

## What Is Pneumonia in Calves?

Pneumonia is inflammation and infection of the lungs and airways. When lung tissue becomes inflamed, the calf has less ability to move oxygen into the bloodstream. Fluid, mucus, and damaged tissue can make breathing difficult and reduce the calf’s ability to grow and thrive.

Pneumonia is usually part of a broader disease process known as bovine respiratory disease. A calf may first experience a viral infection or environmental irritation. This damages the protective lining of the respiratory tract, allowing bacteria normally found in the upper airways to move deeper into the lungs and cause more serious disease.  

Calves can develop either acute or chronic pneumonia:

- **Acute pneumonia** develops quickly and may cause fever, depression, coughing, nasal discharge, and difficult breathing.
- **Chronic pneumonia** develops more gradually. The calf may continue eating but show poor growth, a persistent cough, nasal discharge, or an increased breathing rate.
- **Subclinical pneumonia** causes lung damage without obvious outward signs. These calves may look healthy but gain weight poorly and can later develop more severe respiratory disease.

Lung damage may remain even after a calf appears to recover. As a result, pneumonia can affect future growth, feed efficiency, reproductive performance, and milk production.

## What Causes Calf Pneumonia?

Calf pneumonia is a multifactorial disease. This means that several factors usually contribute to the problem at the same time.

### Viral pathogens

Viruses commonly damage the respiratory tract and make secondary bacterial infection more likely. Important viral contributors include:

- Bovine respiratory syncytial virus, or BRSV
- Infectious bovine rhinotracheitis, or IBR
- Parainfluenza-3 virus, or PI3
- Bovine viral diarrhea virus, or BVDV

BRSV may cause fever, depression, reduced feed intake, coughing, nasal and eye discharge, rapid breathing, and severe respiratory distress. Secondary bacterial pneumonia is common after viral infection.  

BVDV can also weaken immune defenses, allowing other respiratory pathogens to cause more severe disease. It is particularly important to identify and manage persistently infected animals because they can maintain infection pressure within a herd.

### Bacterial pathogens

Common bacteria associated with pneumonia in calves include:

- *Mannheimia haemolytica*
- *Pasteurella multocida*
- *Histophilus somni*
- *Mycoplasma bovis*

These organisms may live in the upper respiratory tract without causing disease. Stress, viral infection, poor air quality, or inadequate immunity can allow them to multiply and enter the lower respiratory tract. *Mannheimia haemolytica* is especially associated with severe tissue damage in bovine respiratory disease. 

*Mycoplasma bovis* may cause chronic pneumonia and can also be associated with arthritis, swollen joints, ear disease, and treatment failure. If several calves have recurring or poorly responsive pneumonia, your veterinarian may recommend diagnostic testing for Mycoplasma and other organisms.

### Aspiration pneumonia

Aspiration pneumonia occurs when milk, medication, stomach contents, amniotic fluid, or another substance enters the lungs instead of the esophagus. It can happen when:

- A calf is incorrectly tubed
- Milk is delivered too quickly
- A nipple has a hole that is too large
- A weak calf cannot swallow properly
- A calf inhales fluid during a difficult birth

Aspiration pneumonia may begin suddenly and can be severe. Prevention depends on careful feeding technique and ensuring that weak calves are handled by trained personnel.

## Risk Factors for Pneonia in Calves

The same pathogens may cause mild disease in one group and severe disease in another. The difference is often the calf’s immunity, environment, and level of stress.

Important risk factors include:

- Inadequate or poor-quality colostrum
- Failure of passive transfer
- Poor ventilation
- High humidity
- Ammonia buildup
- Dust and mold
- Wet or dirty bedding
- Overcrowding
- Mixing different age groups
- Commingling calves from multiple farms
- Transportation
- Weaning
- Sudden diet changes
- Castration, dehorning, or disbudding
- Extreme weather
- Poor nutrition
- Exposure to older cattle

Calves are especially vulnerable when maternal antibodies begin to decline, often around two to four months of age.  

Stress does not directly create pneumonia, but it can suppress immune function and increase pathogen exposure. Several stressors occurring together—such as transport, weaning, ration change, and commingling—can greatly increase the risk of disease.

## Signs of Pneumonia in Calves

Early pneumonia signs can be difficult to recognize. A calf may not look seriously ill at first, so observe calves closely during feeding and routine checks.

Early warning signs include:

- Reduced interest in milk or feed
- Slower drinking
- Mild depression or lethargy
- Drooped ears
- A lowered or bowed head
- Increased nasal or eye discharge
- More frequent nose licking
- Occasional cough
- Separation from other calves
- Reduced activity

More advanced signs may include:

- Frequent or spontaneous coughing
- Fever
- Rapid breathing
- Shallow breathing
- Labored breathing
- Open-mouth breathing
- Abdominal effort when breathing
- Thick or yellow nasal discharge
- Severe depression
- Weight loss
- Grunting during expiration
- Blue or gray mucous membranes

A rectal temperature above approximately 103.5°F may indicate bacterial involvement, although temperature alone cannot confirm pneumonia. Some seriously ill calves may have a normal temperature, particularly in chronic disease or late-stage illness.  

A calf with open-mouth breathing, severe respiratory distress, inability to stand, or blue mucous membranes needs urgent veterinary attention.

## How Is Calf Pneumonia Diagnosed?

Your veterinarian will usually combine the calf’s history, clinical signs, temperature, breathing pattern, and lung sounds. The veterinarian may also assess the group’s housing, recent stressors, vaccination status, colostrum records, and treatment history.

Additional diagnostic tools may include:

- Lung auscultation
- Thoracic ultrasound
- Nasal or deep nasopharyngeal swabs
- Tracheal washes
- Blood testing
- PCR testing
- Bacterial culture and sensitivity testing
- Necropsy examination of deceased calves

Thoracic ultrasonography can detect lung consolidation and identify calves with lung damage that do not show obvious clinical signs. It can also help evaluate treatment response and determine whether respiratory disease is affecting more animals than expected.  

Testing is especially valuable when pneumonia is recurring, affecting many calves, causing treatment failures, or resulting in unexpected deaths. Identifying the pathogens can help your veterinarian develop a more effective treatment and vaccination plan.

## Treatment for Pneumonia in Calves

Early treatment gives a calf the best chance of recovery. Delaying treatment allows infection and inflammation to cause more extensive lung damage.

Treatment may include:

- A veterinarian-selected antimicrobial for suspected bacterial pneumonia
- Anti-inflammatory medication
- Fluids when dehydration is present
- Nutritional support
- Easy access to clean water
- Comfortable, dry bedding
- Isolation from healthy calves
- Reduced handling and stress

Antimicrobials should only be used under veterinary direction and according to the product label, prescription requirements, withdrawal periods, and local regulations. Common antimicrobial classes used for bovine respiratory disease include certain macrolides and phenicols, but the correct product depends on the calf, suspected pathogen, production system, and previous treatment history.  

Do not use leftover medication or stop treatment as soon as the calf appears better. Follow the complete veterinary protocol and record the calf’s identification, temperature, signs, product, dose, route, and response.

A calf that does not improve within the expected period may require reassessment. Possible explanations include incorrect diagnosis, advanced lung damage, resistant bacteria, Mycoplasma infection, aspiration, or an ongoing environmental problem.

## What Should You Do When a Calf Becomes Sick?

Use a consistent response plan:

1. **Move the calf to a clean hospital area** away from healthy calves.
2. **Take its temperature** and observe its breathing rate and effort.
3. **Check appetite and hydration.**
4. **Contact your veterinarian** if signs are moderate, severe, recurring, or not improving.
5. **Administer only veterinarian-approved medication.**
6. **Improve comfort** with dry bedding, protection from weather, and easy access to feed and water.
7. **Monitor the calf at least twice daily.**
8. **Review pen mates** for early signs of respiratory disease.
9. **Record the case** so you can identify patterns over time.

Sick calves should generally be handled after healthy calves to reduce the risk of spreading respiratory pathogens. Use separate feeding equipment where possible, and clean and disinfect shared equipment thoroughly. 

## Preventing Pneumonia in Calves

Prevention works best when you address immunity, exposure, and stress together.

### 1. Prioritize colostrum management

Colostrum is the calf’s first major source of immune protection. Provide clean, high-quality colostrum as soon as possible after birth, using a written protocol that covers volume, timing, quality, storage, and delivery.

Your farm should monitor colostrum management rather than assuming every calf received enough. Blood testing calves for passive transfer can help identify whether the program is working.

### 2. Improve ventilation

Good ventilation removes moisture, ammonia, dust, and contaminated air while bringing in fresh air. Ventilation should provide fresh air without creating cold drafts directly at calf level.

Warning signs of poor ventilation include:

- Ammonia odor
- Condensation
- Damp bedding
- Mold
- Rust or moisture stains
- Persistent coughing in housed calves
- Poor air movement
- Heavy humidity

Calves should not share stagnant air with older cattle whenever possible. Well-designed hutches, outdoor pens, or appropriately ventilated calf barns can reduce pathogen exposure.

### 3. Keep housing clean and dry

Clean, dry bedding helps calves stay warm and reduces exposure to contaminated material. Remove wet bedding regularly, repair leaks, and provide adequate drainage.

Clean and disinfect calf pens, feeding equipment, buckets, nipples, and automatic feeders according to a written schedule. Whenever possible, use all-in/all-out management and allow pens to dry between groups. 

### 4. Avoid overcrowding and age mixing

Group calves by similar age and size. Avoid placing young calves in the same airspace as older calves or recently purchased animals.

Overcrowding increases moisture, pathogen concentration, respiratory droplets, and competition for feed and resting areas. It also makes it harder for staff to notice early signs of illness.

### 5. Provide adequate nutrition

Calves need enough energy, protein, minerals, and clean water to develop normally and maintain immune function. Avoid abrupt diet changes, particularly around weaning or transport.

A calf that is underfed has fewer resources available to fight infection. Monitor body weight, growth rate, appetite, and milk or milk-replacer intake.

### 6. Use a veterinarian-designed vaccination program

Vaccines may reduce the frequency and severity of respiratory disease, but they cannot compensate for poor colostrum, inadequate ventilation, overcrowding, or excessive stress.

Your veterinarian can help select vaccines for the pathogens relevant to your farm. Timing matters: complete primary courses and boosters before predictable periods of high risk whenever possible. Store and administer vaccines exactly as directed by the manufacturer.

Vaccinating dams before calving may improve the quality of antibodies passed through colostrum. Calf vaccination may also be appropriate, depending on age, disease risk, vaccine label, and herd health plan.

### 7. Reduce stress

Avoid stacking stressful procedures together. For example, do not combine weaning, transport, housing changes, dietary changes, and disbudding unless necessary.

Reduce risk by:

- Handling calves quietly
- Keeping groups stable
- Minimizing transport time
- Allowing new arrivals to rest
- Introducing feed changes gradually
- Avoiding unnecessary commingling
- Planning preconditioning before sale or movement

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can calves recover from pneumonia?

Many calves recover when pneumonia is identified and treated early. However, severe or prolonged disease can cause permanent lung damage, poor growth, chronic illness, or death. Early treatment and follow-up are important.

### Is calf pneumonia contagious?

Many respiratory viruses and bacteria can spread through respiratory droplets, direct contact, shared airspace, and contaminated equipment. Isolate sick calves, improve ventilation, reduce crowding, and maintain good hygiene to limit transmission.

### What are the first signs of pneumonia in calves?

The first signs may include reduced appetite, mild depression, drooped ears, increased eye or nasal discharge, slower drinking, a slight cough, and increased breathing rate. Early signs are often subtle.

### Can vaccinated calves still get pneumonia?

Yes. Vaccines reduce risk but do not provide complete protection against every pathogen or every cause of pneumonia. Poor ventilation, inadequate colostrum, stress, heavy exposure, and incomplete vaccination can still result in disease.

### When should you call a veterinarian?

Contact your veterinarian for fever, labored breathing, open-mouth breathing, severe depression, thick nasal discharge, persistent coughing, repeated cases, treatment failure, or sudden deaths. A veterinarian should also investigate recurring pneumonia in a group.

### Does poor ventilation cause pneumonia?

Poor ventilation does not act as a single infectious cause, but it increases risk by allowing moisture, ammonia, dust, and airborne pathogens to accumulate. Fresh, clean air is one of the most important parts of calf respiratory health.

### Should you treat every coughing calf with antibiotics?

No. Coughing can have several causes, and not every calf requires antibiotics. Work with your veterinarian to establish a case definition and treatment protocol that supports effective care and responsible antimicrobial use.

## Conclusion

Pneumonia in calves is preventable and manageable, but success depends on acting before the lungs are badly damaged. Watch calves closely, recognize subtle early signs, provide prompt veterinary-directed treatment, and isolate sick animals.

The strongest prevention program combines excellent colostrum management, clean and dry housing, fresh air, appropriate nutrition, vaccination, low-stress handling, careful grouping, and strong biosecurity. If pneumonia keeps returning, do not treat each calf as an isolated problem. Investigate the entire calf-management system with your veterinarian and address the underlying causes.
