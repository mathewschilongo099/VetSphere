## Spotting the Gut Trouble Early

Farmers Weekly reports that coccidiosis is an intestinal disease usually seen in young beef and dairy calves aged between three weeks and six months. That specific age window is critical for watching your young stock closely. The condition is caused by microscopic parasites known as coccidia. These organisms infect a wide range of animals, but they are strictly host specific. This means that coccid.ia coming from a given animal species cannot affect an entirely different species, such as sheep and goats. Understanding this specificity helps you focus your biosecurity efforts directly on the cattle rather than worrying about cross-species transmission from other livestock sharing your land.

## How the Infection Spreads in Pens and Pastures

![Close up of a brown calf with a yellow ear tag](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6339152/pexels-photo-6339152.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


Transmission of the parasite happens entirely through the fecal-oral route. Infected fecal material contaminates feed, water, or soil, and calves ingest it while eating, drinking, or licking contaminated surfaces. If you manage dairy calves, disease outbreaks are frequently associated with overstocking and contaminated accommodation. For beef herds, contaminated watercourses pose a major risk for young spring-born calves while they are out at pasture during the warm season. Animals tend to congregate heavily around feed and water troughs right after turnout, creating high-traffic zones where the parasite easily passes from animal to animal. There is also a central nervous system form of the disease that occurs in Canada and the northern US during or just after severe cold weather in midwinter, though veterinary authorities note this specific form has not been reported anywhere else.

## Keeping the Bugs Away from Feed and Water

![several cows eating feed from a trough in a barn](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1629313471551-ba2052384b9b?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyfHxjYXR0bGUlMjBmZWVkaW5nfGVufDF8MHx8fDE3OTU4Nzc5MDR8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


Because the primary transmission route relies on droppings getting into what your calves consume, your best defense is physical separation of feed and water from waste. You should raise feed and water troughs well off the ground to avoid fecal contamination. When troughs sit flat on the earth or floor, it is far too easy for manure to get kicked in or splashed into the water supply. Keeping these structures elevated cuts down the amount of contaminated material your calves ingest during their daily feeding and drinking routines. Simple changes to how you position containers can dramatically reduce the chances of an intestinal parasite outbreak.

## Managing Housing and Batches

Pen design and stocking rates dictate whether an outbreak gets a foothold in your sheds. Keep your pens clean, well-drained, and bedded with good ventilation to reduce moisture and slow parasite survival. When raising young stock, operate strict all in, all out management systems to break the infection cycle. Pay close attention to the thorough disinfection of buildings between batches of calves so that incoming animals do not walk into an environment seeded by the previous group. If you wait until clinical signs are glaringly obvious, you may already be facing major growth setbacks in your herd. Instead, talk to your veterinarian about treating calves before they display clinical symptoms to properly protect the profitability of your operation. Specific drug dosage regimens are not detailed in the general research, so always consult a local veterinary authority for the correct treatment protocols for your specific animals.

## What to Do This Week

Walk through your calving pens and pastures today to inspect where your troughs are placed. If any feed or water containers are sitting directly on the ground, lift them up immediately using simple blocks or stands. Check the drainage in your main calf pens and plan a thorough cleaning and disinfection schedule for the next empty batch. Speak with your local vet about preventative treatments before symptoms show up in your herd.
