## Catching the early warning signs

When you walk through the calving pens, you want to spot trouble before a calf stops eating completely. Scours is simply the common name for diarrhoea in calves. It is a disease of the digestive system that is characterised by watery faeces and an increased frequency of bowel movements. According to NADIS, calves are most commonly affected at 8 - 14 days old, when there is an acute onset of diarrhoea. This involves the passage of very watery yellow or green faeces, and the infection can spread rapidly among young calves in the group.

The early signs can be easy to miss if you are only looking for a completely sick animal. Typical early signs of scour include a reluctance to stand and suck, mild depression, and salivation. The calf rapidly becomes dehydrated and recumbent if you do not catch these initial changes. Picking out the calf that is struggling before they have completely stopped eating gives you the best chance to turn things around.

## Breathing issues and other hidden signs

![multiple cattle resting and grazing on a green grassy hillside](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8974723/pexels-photo-8974723.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


Many caregivers watch only the gut when dealing with digestive upsets, but scours brings other symptoms with it. Many calves with scours will exhibit respiratory symptoms. They breathe faster than usual with increased effort. Because of this, you must make sure not to misdiagnose scours cases as simple respiratory infections.

A calf that is feeling unwell will often reduce its intake gradually. Not eating can also be a sign of a sick calf, long before it collapses. By paying attention to breathing rates alongside manure consistency and suckling enthusiasm, you catch the full picture of how the animal is failing.

## Why fluid loss and acid build-up kill

![multiple cows and calves grazing in a large green pasture under a cloudy sky](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1665105519765-a0ec8e027100?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwzfHxjYWx2ZXMlMjBpbiUyMGElMjBmaWVsZHxlbnwxfDB8fHwxNzk1NDkxNjM1fDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


The high water content in the faeces results in massive water loss from the body, leading straight to dehydration. Beyond the water, the calf loses vital salts. According to the OSU Extension Service, the loss of electrolytes causes metabolic acidosis, which will kill the calf if not corrected promptly. This imbalance must be corrected promptly in less than 2 days.

When these chemical shifts happen inside the body, oral rehydration becomes an urgent priority. The body cannot buffer the acid build-up on its own once the system tips past a certain point. You have to step in with the right fluids to restore normal blood chemistry.

## Choosing the right electrolytes

When you mix up fluids to fight dehydration, resist the temptation to use home-prepared kitchen recipes. Electrolyte powders prepared by veterinary pharmaceutical manufacturers are strongly recommended over home-prepared recipes. They are carefully balanced to provide the correct proportions of salts relative to water.

Depending on the size of the calf and the severity of the scours, a specific volume of electrolytes may need to be administered each day. You may need to provide 2 - 6 quarts of electrolytes each day to keep pace with the fluid loss. Treatment must be aimed squarely at correcting clinical signs, specifically targeting dehydration and metabolic acidosis.

## Shelter and protecting weak calves

Sick calves lose body heat rapidly and cannot maintain their core temperature. Supportive care includes protecting sick calves from harsh environmental elements. You must protect sick calves from the cold, wind, and rain, keeping them above a minimum of 50°F to give their bodies a fighting chance.

If the disease progresses and the calf gets too weak, oral fluids alone will not be enough. Call a veterinary surgeon for intravenous fluids in dehydrated calves that are unable to stand unaided.

## Your actions for this week

Walk your pens daily and identify struggling calves before they have completely stopped eating. Stock up on balanced electrolyte powders recommended by veterinary pharmaceutical manufacturers instead of relying on home recipes. Prepare to administer 2 - 6 quarts of electrolytes each day depending on calf size and scours severity. Ensure your treatment is aimed at correcting clinical signs, specifically dehydration and metabolic acidosis, and protect sick calves from the cold with a minimum of 50°F, wind, and rain. Finally, call a veterinary surgeon for intravenous fluids if you find dehydrated calves that are unable to stand unaided.
