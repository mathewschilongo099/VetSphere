# Ringworm in Cattle: Causes, Symptoms, Treatment and Prevention

## Introduction

Ringworm in Cattle is one of the most common skin diseases seen in calves and housed adult cows worldwide. It is a contagious fungal infection that affects the outer layers of the skin and hair, causing circular patches of hair loss, crusts and scaling, particularly on the head and neck.  Despite the name, ringworm has nothing to do with worms; it is caused by a group of fungi called dermatophytes.  

This condition is important for farmers and students to understand because it can spread rapidly through a herd, slow growth in young stock, damage hides, and infect people who handle affected animals.  Knowing the causes of Ringworm in Cattle, how to recognise the symptoms, how to treat Ringworm in Cattle, and how to prevent new cases will help protect both animal welfare and farm income. 

---

## What is Ringworm in Cattle?

Ringworm in Cattle (bovine dermatophytosis) is a superficial fungal infection of the skin and hair. The fungi live on keratin – the tough protein that makes up the outer skin layer, hair and horn – and they invade these tissues, causing local damage, inflammation and the characteristic circular lesions.  It is considered a self‑limiting disease, which means many cases will eventually clear on their own as the animal develops immunity, but this can take months.  

The main fungus responsible for Ringworm in Cattle is Trichophyton verrucosum, a slow‑growing dermatophyte specifically adapted to cattle and other ruminants.  It is responsible for around 90–95% of bovine cases worldwide.  Less commonly, other dermatophytes such as Trichophyton mentagrophytes, Nannizzia (Microsporum) gypsea and Microsporum canis may be isolated from cattle lesions.  These fungi produce spores that are highly resistant and can survive for months to years in buildings, on equipment and in hair and scabs shed from the animal.  

Ringworm is also a zoonosis, meaning it can be passed from animals to humans. Farm workers, veterinary staff and children who handle affected cattle can develop itchy, circular skin lesions.  This makes good control and prevention on farm important for public health as well as animal health.

---

## Causes of Ringworm in Cattle

The primary cause of Ringworm in Cattle is infection with the dermatophyte fungus Trichophyton verrucosum. This organism is transmitted mainly by direct contact with infected animals or indirectly via contaminated objects and the environment.  Spores are shed in hair and skin scales and can contaminate walls, feed barriers, grooming tools, halters and bedding. Cattle become infected when spores contact the skin, especially if the skin is damaged by rubbing, minor wounds or external parasites.  

Several management and environmental factors increase the risk of outbreaks. Ringworm is much more common in winter when cattle are housed indoors in close contact, with high stocking densities, poor ventilation and limited sunlight.  These conditions favour survival and spread of spores and reduce the natural disinfecting effect of ultraviolet light. Young animals, particularly calves between 2 and 6 months of age, are much more susceptible, mainly because they have not yet developed immunity from earlier exposure.  Stress, poor nutrition and concurrent diseases can further weaken the skin’s defences and make infection more likely.  

Buildings and equipment contaminated from previous outbreaks are an important source of infection. Dermatophyte spores can remain viable in cracks, wood and dust for many months.  If cleaning and disinfection between groups of cattle are inadequate, new batches of calves are exposed as soon as they enter the building, leading to repeated seasonal problems every winter.

## Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Ringworm in Cattle

The clinical signs and symptoms of Ringworm in Cattle usually start as small, greyish areas of hair breakage or roughness, often around the eyes, muzzle, ears and neck.  These early lesions gradually enlarge into circular or oval patches of hair loss (alopecia) with thick, grey‑white crusts and scaling. The skin underneath may be dry and scaly.  Lesions are often well‑defined, raised and can have a classic “ring” appearance with more active inflammation at the edges.

As the disease progresses, multiple lesions may merge to form larger irregular patches. In some animals the crusts become thick, wart‑like and fissured; in severe cases they may crack and ooze, especially if there is secondary bacterial infection.  Lesions are most common on the head and neck due to rubbing on feed barriers and gates, but in more extensive infections they can spread to the shoulders, back and limbs.  Pruritus (itchiness) is variable: some cattle show little irritation, while others rub and scratch, which spreads spores and worsens skin damage.  

In many cases, overall health and appetite remain normal, but heavy infections can reduce growth rates in calves and lower body condition because of stress and discomfort.  In long‑haired or thick‑coated animals, lesions may be hidden until the coat is parted or clipped. In mild or early cases, farmers may only notice rough hair or slightly dull patches rather than obvious bald spots, which emphasises the need for close inspection in housed groups of young stock during the risk period.

## How to Diagnose Ringworm in Cattle

Diagnosis of Ringworm in Cattle is often based initially on the typical appearance and distribution of lesions in a susceptible group, especially young cattle in winter housing.  The presence of multiple animals with similar circular, crusty patches of hair loss on the head and neck strongly supports a clinical diagnosis. However, several other skin diseases can mimic ringworm, including mange, lice infestation, photosensitisation and some nutritional skin disorders, so confirmation is recommended in uncertain or atypical cases.  

Definitive diagnosis is usually made by microscopic examination and fungal culture. Plucked hairs and skin scrapings are collected from the edge of active lesions, where fungi are most abundant. Direct microscopy after treatment with potassium hydroxide allows identification of fungal spores and hyphae on the hair shafts (ectothrix infection).  Fungal culture on special media, such as Sabouraud dextrose agar with antibiotics and thiamine, enables growth and identification of Trichophyton verrucosum and other dermatophytes, though T. verrucosum is slow growing and may take several weeks to appear.  

Other diagnostic aids include skin biopsy in unusual cases and, in research or reference laboratories, molecular methods such as PCR and RFLP analysis to identify dermatophyte species.  Wood’s lamp (UV light) is commonly used in small animals to detect fluorescence of some Microsporum species, but it is generally not useful for bovine ringworm because T. verrucosum usually does not fluoresce.  Your veterinarian will select the most appropriate tests based on clinical findings, herd history and the need for precise identification (for example, in outbreaks with human cases or repeated herd problems).

## Treatment of Ringworm in Cattle

When considering how to treat Ringworm in Cattle, it is important to remember that the disease is self‑limiting: many animals will eventually recover without therapy as they develop natural immunity.  However, untreated infections can persist for months, spread within the herd, pose a zoonotic risk and cause economic losses from reduced growth and hide damage.  Therefore, treatment is often justified, especially in young stock, pedigree animals, show cattle and herds with human exposure.

Topical antifungal therapy is the mainstay of treatment and is generally the most cost‑effective option. Products containing fungicidal agents such as enilconazole are commonly used as washes or sprays applied to affected areas and sometimes to the whole body in heavy infections.  These treatments act by killing fungal organisms on the skin and in hair shafts, reducing the infectious load and speeding up clinical recovery. Repeated applications are usually required according to label directions, often at intervals of several days to a week. Thorough wetting of lesions and the surrounding skin is critical for success.

In some countries, vaccines containing Trichophyton verrucosum antigens are licensed for both prevention and treatment of Ringworm in Cattle. These dermatophyte vaccines can be used prophylactically to reduce the incidence and severity of disease in at‑risk groups, and therapeutically to shorten the duration of clinical signs in already infected animals.  Published studies show that such vaccines stimulate both humoral and cellular immune responses against dermatophyte antigens and can significantly improve recovery times in affected cattle.  These vaccines are typically licensed for use from around two months of age; exact schedules depend on the product and veterinary advice.  

Systemic antifungal drugs are rarely used in cattle because of cost, withdrawal times and limited evidence of additional benefit over topical therapy and vaccination.  Supportive management remains essential: clipping or carefully brushing crusts (with proper protective clothing) can improve penetration of topical agents, but care must be taken to avoid spreading spores. Environmental disinfection and improved housing (see prevention section) are crucial to prevent re‑infection. Herbal and homeopathic remedies lack scientific evidence for effectiveness against Trichophyton verrucosum and should not replace proven antifungal treatments.

## Prevention and Control of Ringworm in Cattle

Effective prevention of Ringworm in Cattle focuses on breaking the cycle of infection between animals, the environment and new susceptible stock. Because spores can survive for months in buildings, thorough cleaning and disinfection of housing between batches of animals is vital.  All organic material such as manure, bedding and loose hair should be removed, followed by washing and application of an appropriate fungicidal disinfectant to walls, gates, feed barriers and equipment. Particular attention should be paid to rough surfaces and cracks where hair and scabs accumulate.  

Good housing management greatly reduces risk. Avoid overcrowding, improve ventilation and, where possible, allow access to natural sunlight, as ultraviolet light has a natural antifungal effect.  Providing adequate nutrition, minimising stress and controlling external parasites help maintain skin health and support the animal’s immune response. Young calves should be grouped by age and not mixed unnecessarily with older carriers during the high‑risk winter housing period. Isolating or at least separating obviously affected animals from clean groups will slow spread, though it may not be practical to remove every mild case in a large outbreak.  

Vaccination against Trichophyton verrucosum, where available, is a powerful tool for herd‑level control. Used prophylactically, dermatophyte vaccines reduce the incidence and severity of Ringworm in Cattle, particularly in calves entering contaminated housing or intensive rearing systems.  In herds with a history of recurrent winter outbreaks, strategic vaccination of young stock before housing can be highly cost‑effective. Regular monitoring of skin health, early detection of lesions and prompt treatment of clinical cases, combined with strict hygiene for people and equipment, provide the best integrated approach to long‑term control.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Ringworm in Cattle

### Q: Is Ringworm in Cattle caused by worms?

**A:** No. Ringworm in Cattle is caused by fungi, mainly Trichophyton verrucosum, not by worms. These dermatophyte fungi invade the outer skin and hair, feeding on keratin and producing circular, crusty lesions. 

### Q: How does Ringworm in Cattle spread within a herd?

**A:** Ringworm spreads mainly through direct contact between infected and healthy animals and indirectly via contaminated walls, feed barriers, grooming tools, halters and bedding. Fungal spores shed in hair and scabs can survive in buildings for months, so new animals entering contaminated housing are easily infected. Overcrowding and poor ventilation in winter housing greatly increase spread. 

### Q: What are the most common symptoms of Ringworm in Cattle?

**A:** Typical symptoms of Ringworm in Cattle include circular or oval patches of hair loss with grey‑white crusts and scaling, most often on the head, around the eyes, muzzle, ears and neck. Lesions may merge into larger areas, sometimes forming thick, wart‑like crusts. Itchiness varies; some cattle rub and scratch affected areas, which helps spread spores. General health is often normal, but heavy infections can reduce growth rates in calves. 

### Q: Will Ringworm in Cattle go away on its own?

**A:** In many cases, yes. Dermatophytosis is a self‑limiting disease, and affected cattle usually develop immunity and recover over time without treatment. However, natural recovery can take several months, during which infected animals continue to shed spores, infect others and pose a zoonotic risk to people. Treatment and improved management can greatly shorten the course of disease and reduce spread. 

### Q: Can people catch Ringworm from cattle?

**A:** Yes. Ringworm in Cattle is a zoonotic disease, and the fungi can infect humans who have close contact with affected animals or contaminated equipment. People typically develop itchy, circular red lesions on exposed skin. Farm workers, veterinarians and children are at particular risk. Wearing gloves, protective clothing and practising good hand hygiene when handling infected cattle are essential preventive measures. Anyone who develops suspicious skin lesions after contact with affected animals should consult a doctor. 

### Q: Is there a vaccine for Ringworm in Cattle?

**A:** In several countries there are licensed vaccines containing Trichophyton verrucosum antigens. These can be used both to prevent Ringworm in Cattle and to help treat existing cases by shortening recovery time. They are generally given to young stock from about two months of age, often before housing, and can significantly reduce the severity and duration of outbreaks when used alongside good hygiene and management.

## When to Call a Veterinarian

You should contact a veterinarian if you notice circular patches of hair loss, crusting or scaling on the head and neck of calves or adult cattle, especially when several animals in the group are affected. Early veterinary assessment helps confirm that the problem is indeed Ringworm in Cattle and not another skin disease such as mange or lice, which require different treatment strategies.  

Veterinary involvement is particularly important when lesions are severe or widespread, when animals show signs of discomfort or poor growth, or when there is concern about spread to other livestock or to people. A veterinarian can collect appropriate samples for diagnosis, advise on the most effective topical antifungal products, design a vaccination programme where available and recommend practical environmental disinfection and housing improvements tailored to your facilities.  

You should also seek veterinary advice in herds with repeated winter outbreaks of Ringworm in Cattle, in pedigree or show cattle where skin appearance is critical, and whenever human cases of ringworm occur in people who have contact with the herd. In these situations, a coordinated plan including diagnosis, treatment, vaccination and biosecurity is needed to protect both animal and human health.

## Conclusion

Ringworm in Cattle is a common, contagious fungal skin disease that mainly affects young housed animals and is caused predominantly by Trichophyton verrucosum.  Although the condition is often self‑limiting, it can spread rapidly through a herd, slow growth in calves, damage hides and pose a zoonotic risk to farm workers and their families. Recognising the typical symptoms of Ringworm in Cattle – circular, crusty patches of hair loss, especially on the head and neck – and understanding how the disease is transmitted are the foundations of effective control.  

Management of Ringworm in Cattle requires a combination of measures: accurate diagnosis, appropriate topical antifungal treatment, strategic use of dermatophyte vaccines where available, and rigorous environmental cleaning and disinfection of housing and equipment.  Preventive strategies such as avoiding overcrowding, improving ventilation, supporting good nutrition and minimising stress significantly reduce the risk of new outbreaks, particularly during winter housing. With a well‑planned, herd‑level approach guided by veterinary advice, most farms can bring Ringworm in Cattle under control and protect both animal performance and human health.
