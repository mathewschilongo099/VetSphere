Ringworm in cattle is a common, contagious fungal skin infection that can spread quickly through calves, heifers, cows, farm workers, and contaminated equipment. Despite its name, ringworm is not caused by a worm. It is a condition known medically as **dermatophytosis**, caused by fungi that grow in keratin—the protein found in hair, skin, and claws.

The most common cause of cattle ringworm is *Trichophyton verrucosum*. Calves are especially vulnerable, although adult cattle can also develop infection, particularly when they are stressed, poorly nourished, overcrowded, or housed in damp conditions.   

Most healthy cattle eventually recover, but untreated animals can continue contaminating the environment for months. Because cattle ringworm can infect people, early recognition, sensible treatment, and strict hygiene are important parts of herd health.

## What Is Ringworm in Cattle?

Ringworm is a superficial fungal infection of the skin and hair. The fungus usually enters through minor abrasions or irritated skin and begins growing in the outer layers of the skin and around hair shafts.

The infection commonly produces round or irregular patches of:

- Hair loss
- Gray or white crusts
- Scaling
- Thickened skin
- Broken or brittle hairs
- Mild swelling around the edges of lesions

It does not usually cause fever or serious illness. In many cases, cattle remain bright, active, and eating normally. Itching may be absent or mild, although some animals may rub affected areas against posts, gates, or other surfaces.

Ringworm is more than a cosmetic issue. The fungal spores can spread through direct animal contact, shared grooming equipment, bedding, fences, stanchions, halters, trailers, and clothing. The infection can also reduce hide quality and create problems when affected animals are being prepared for sale or exhibition.

## What Causes Ringworm in Cattle?

Several dermatophyte fungi can cause ringworm, but *Trichophyton verrucosum* is the primary cause in cattle. Other fungi, including *Trichophyton mentagrophytes* and some *Microsporum* species, may occasionally be involved.  

### How cattle become infected

Transmission occurs through:

1. **Direct contact** with an infected animal  
2. **Contaminated equipment**, such as brushes, currycombs, clippers, halters, and neck straps  
3. **Contaminated housing**, including gates, walls, posts, bedding, and scratching surfaces  
4. **Contact with infected hair and scabs** that have fallen into the environment  
5. **New cattle introduced to the herd** without adequate quarantine  

The fungal spores are hardy and may remain infectious in buildings and on equipment for extended periods. This is why ringworm can reappear in the same herd year after year, even when the original affected animals are no longer present. 

## Which Cattle Are Most at Risk?

Ringworm can affect cattle of any age, but it is most frequently recognized in calves older than two months through the yearling stage. This often coincides with the time when young cattle are grouped together, increasing contact and allowing infection to spread.  

Risk is higher when cattle are:

- Kept in crowded housing
- Exposed to poor ventilation
- Housed in damp or dirty conditions
- Under nutritional stress
- Recently transported or mixed with unfamiliar animals
- Affected by another illness
- Exposed to contaminated grooming tools
- Kept indoors for long periods
- Experiencing skin damage from rubbing, insects, or rough surfaces

Calves with a weakened immune system may develop more severe or widespread lesions. Adult cattle can also be affected, especially when they are sick, stressed, or introduced into an infected housing group.

Winter outbreaks are common because cattle are housed closely together, ventilation may be reduced, and contaminated hair and scabs accumulate indoors. However, ringworm can occur at any time of year.

## Signs of Ringworm in Cattle

The appearance of ringworm varies depending on the animal’s age, immune status, and the stage of infection.

### Early signs

Early lesions may look like small areas of:

- Raised skin
- Mild swelling
- Scaling
- Rough hair
- Slight crust formation

The affected area may gradually expand as the fungus moves outward through the hair and skin.

### Typical signs

As the infection develops, you may notice:

- Clearly defined circular or oval bald patches
- Gray-white, powdery crusts
- Thick, firmly attached scabs
- Broken hairs around the edge of lesions
- Dry, flaky skin
- Multiple lesions that join together
- Hair regrowth in the center of older lesions

In calves, lesions are often found around the eyes, ears, muzzle, neck, and shoulders. Lesions may also develop on the trunk and back. Adult cows and heifers commonly have ringworm on the chest, limbs, neck, flank, udder, or hindquarters. Bulls may develop lesions around the dewlap and the skin between the jaw branches.   

Ringworm lesions are often not very itchy. If an animal is intensely itchy, has widespread redness, pus, pain, fever, or rapidly worsening skin disease, another condition may be present as well.

## Is Ringworm Dangerous to Cattle?

For most otherwise healthy cattle, ringworm is not life-threatening. The disease is often self-limiting, meaning the animal’s immune system eventually controls the infection without treatment. However, spontaneous recovery may take several weeks or months, and the animal may continue spreading fungal spores during that time.   

Severe or prolonged infection can:

- Spread across large areas of the body
- Damage the appearance and quality of the hide
- Increase the risk of secondary skin infection
- Create animal welfare concerns if lesions become painful
- Delay sale or show eligibility
- Expose farm workers and family members to infection

Treatment is often recommended even when the disease is likely to resolve naturally because it can shorten the infectious period and reduce contamination.

## Can Cattle Ringworm Spread to Humans?

Yes. Ringworm in cattle is a **zoonotic disease**, meaning it can pass from animals to people. Humans may become infected through direct contact with affected cattle or by handling contaminated bedding, hair, scabs, fences, tools, or clothing. *Trichophyton verrucosum* can cause highly inflamed skin lesions in people.   

People at increased risk include:

- Farmers
- Milkers
- Calf caretakers
- Veterinarians
- Livestock exhibitors
- Children who handle calves
- Slaughterhouse and tannery workers

When handling a suspected case:

- Wear disposable gloves and protective clothing.
- Cover cuts and abrasions on your hands.
- Wash your hands thoroughly after handling cattle.
- Do not share clothing, brushes, towels, or equipment.
- Launder contaminated clothing separately.
- Keep children away from affected animals.
- Contact a physician if you develop circular, scaly, itchy, or inflamed skin lesions.

A person with a suspected fungal skin infection should seek medical advice rather than relying on home remedies.

## How Is Ringworm Diagnosed?

An experienced veterinarian may suspect ringworm from the typical appearance of the lesions, but visual diagnosis is not always reliable. Several other cattle skin conditions can cause hair loss, crusting, or scaling.

Conditions that may resemble ringworm include:

- Dermatophilosis
- Lice infestation
- Mange
- Bacterial skin infection
- Photosensitization
- Warts
- Insect irritation
- Nutritional skin disease
- Lumpy skin disease in regions where it occurs

A veterinarian may collect hair and skin scrapings from the active edge of a lesion. Laboratory testing can include:

- Direct microscopic examination
- Fungal culture
- Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR
- Examination of infected hairs for fungal spores

Fungal culture remains an important method for confirming dermatophytosis, although *T. verrucosum* can grow slowly. PCR may provide faster identification in laboratories equipped to perform the test.   

A Wood’s lamp should not be relied on as the sole diagnostic test because many cattle ringworm infections do not fluoresce consistently. A negative lamp examination does not rule out ringworm.

## Treatment for Ringworm in Cattle

Your veterinarian should help you choose a treatment that is legal, safe, practical, and appropriate for food-producing animals. Product availability, labeling, withdrawal periods, and regulations differ between countries.

### 1. Isolate affected cattle

Move visibly affected animals away from healthy cattle when practical. Use separate feed and water equipment, and handle infected animals after healthy animals during daily routines.

Isolation alone will not eliminate the infection because contaminated equipment and housing can continue spreading spores.

### 2. Remove infectious crusts carefully

Thick crusts can prevent topical medication from reaching the affected skin. A veterinarian may recommend gently loosening and removing crusts before treatment.

Do not aggressively scrape, scrub, or clip lesions. Rough handling can injure the skin and spread infected material to other areas. Any brush, disposable scraper, or grooming tool used on an affected animal should be discarded or thoroughly disinfected.

Collected crusts and hair should be disposed of safely so cattle cannot contact them again.

### 3. Use an appropriate topical antifungal

Topical treatment is generally the most practical and cost-effective approach for cattle. Products used in veterinary practice may include lime sulfur preparations or approved enilconazole products, depending on local regulations and availability. Merck Veterinary Manual identifies topical therapy as the preferred treatment approach and advises following product directions carefully.  

Some antifungal creams used in people or companion animals are not approved for cattle, particularly dairy cattle. Do not apply miconazole, clotrimazole, terbinafine, iodine, bleach, or other products without veterinary guidance.

Bleach can irritate skin and create a human health hazard. It should not be used casually on cattle or in poorly ventilated barns.  

Treatment may need to be repeated several times. Stopping as soon as the crust falls away can allow infection to continue at the edge of the lesion.

### 4. Be cautious with oral antifungals

Oral antifungal drugs are generally not cost-effective for cattle and may create concerns involving toxicity, residues, and food-animal regulations. Drugs that are commonly used in dogs, cats, or people should not be given to cattle unless specifically prescribed by a veterinarian.

Never use leftover medication or change the dose without professional advice.

### 5. Treat the environment at the same time

Treating the animal without cleaning the environment often leads to reinfection. Remove organic material first, including hair, scabs, manure, and old bedding. Then clean and disinfect surfaces, gates, stalls, trailers, and equipment according to veterinary and product instructions.

Pay particular attention to:

- Brushes and combs
- Clippers and blades
- Halters and lead ropes
- Calf pens
- Neck straps
- Milking equipment that contacts affected skin
- Trailer partitions
- Scratching posts
- Bedding and feed barriers

Cleaning is essential because disinfectants work poorly when heavy organic material is present.

## Prevention of Ringworm in Cattle

A prevention plan should address cattle, equipment, housing, and new arrivals.

### Improve housing conditions

Reduce overcrowding and improve airflow. Keep bedding dry and replace heavily contaminated material. Better ventilation lowers humidity and reduces the buildup of fungal spores.

Adequate nutrition is also important. Healthy, well-nourished cattle are better able to resist and recover from infection.

### Quarantine new cattle

Keep new arrivals separate from the established herd and inspect their skin regularly. Watch for hair loss, crusts, and lesions around the head, neck, and shoulders before allowing them to share equipment or housing.

New cattle should be evaluated by a veterinarian if lesions are present or if they come from a herd with a history of ringworm.

### Disinfect equipment between animals

Do not use the same brush, currycomb, clipper, or halter on multiple animals without cleaning and disinfecting it. Clippers can spread infection rapidly, particularly when they cause small skin abrasions.

### Avoid unnecessary mixing

Limit contact between your cattle and animals from other farms, markets, shows, and communal grazing areas. Ringworm can spread quickly when animals from multiple sources are grouped together.

### Ask about vaccination

A live attenuated vaccine against *T. verrucosum* is used in some countries. It may help reduce clinical disease and transmission during herd control programs, but availability varies and it is not available in North America according to the Merck Veterinary Manual.  

Discuss vaccination with your veterinarian if ringworm is a recurring herd problem.

## How Long Does Ringworm Take to Clear?

The timeline depends on the number of lesions, the animal’s immune response, treatment consistency, and environmental contamination.

Some cattle improve within several weeks, while untreated infections may persist for four months or longer. Hair regrowth often occurs after the active fungal infection has resolved, so a bald area does not necessarily mean treatment has failed.

Signs that the control plan is working include:

- No new lesions appearing
- Existing lesions becoming smaller
- Less crusting and scaling
- Healthy hair beginning to regrow
- Negative follow-up testing when laboratory confirmation is required

Continue treatment for the period recommended by your veterinarian, even if the animal looks better.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is ringworm in cattle caused by worms?

No. Cattle ringworm is caused by dermatophyte fungi, most commonly *Trichophyton verrucosum*. The name describes the circular appearance of many lesions, not the cause.

### Can ringworm kill cattle?

Ringworm is rarely fatal in otherwise healthy cattle. However, widespread infection, secondary bacterial disease, poor nutrition, or another underlying illness can make the condition more serious.

### Should I separate a calf with ringworm?

Yes, if practical. Isolation reduces direct contact and contamination, but you must also clean shared equipment and housing to control the outbreak.

### Can I use human antifungal cream on cattle?

Do not use human medication on cattle unless your veterinarian specifically recommends it. Food-animal drug regulations, withdrawal periods, skin irritation, and treatment effectiveness must all be considered.

### Will sunlight cure cattle ringworm?

Some infections improve when cattle are turned outdoors, but sunlight alone should not be considered a dependable treatment. Better ventilation, improved nutrition, reduced crowding, and appropriate antifungal therapy may all contribute to recovery.

### Can people catch ringworm from calves?

Yes. Wear gloves and protective clothing when handling affected cattle, wash your hands thoroughly, and seek medical care if you develop suspicious skin lesions.

### Should I vaccinate my herd?

Vaccination may be useful in some countries and in herds with persistent outbreaks. Vaccine availability and recommendations vary, so consult your veterinarian about local products and herd-specific risks.

## Conclusion

Ringworm in cattle is a contagious fungal disease that commonly causes circular hair loss, gray-white crusts, and scaling, especially in calves. Although many healthy cattle recover naturally, the infection can persist for months, spread throughout a herd, contaminate buildings, and infect people.

The best approach combines early recognition, veterinary diagnosis, isolation, suitable topical treatment, thorough cleaning, good ventilation, reduced overcrowding, and careful handling of new cattle. If ringworm continues to return, ask your veterinarian about a complete herd-control program, including vaccination where available.

Prompt action protects your cattle, your farm workers, your family, and the long-term health of your herd.
