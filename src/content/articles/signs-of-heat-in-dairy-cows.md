Recognizing the signs of heat in dairy cows is one of the most important parts of a successful breeding program. When you identify estrus at the right time, you can improve artificial insemination results, reduce open days, shorten calving intervals, and make better use of your herd’s reproductive potential.

The challenge is that heat, also called estrus, is brief. A dairy cow may show obvious signs for only a few hours, and many cows display subtle behavior rather than dramatic mounting activity. Heat detection can also become more difficult during hot weather, when cows tend to move less and show fewer standing events.

The most reliable sign is simple: a cow stands still and allows another cow or bull to mount her. This behavior is called standing heat. Other signs, including restlessness, clear mucus, mounting attempts, and a swollen vulva, can help you identify cows that are approaching or have recently passed standing heat.

## What Does “Heat” Mean in a Dairy Cow?

Heat is the common farm term for estrus, the stage of the reproductive cycle when a cow is sexually receptive and capable of becoming pregnant. During this stage, hormonal changes—especially rising estrogen—affect the cow’s behavior, activity, appetite, and reproductive tract.

A dairy cow’s estrous cycle averages approximately 21 days, although normal cycles may range from about 17 to 24 days. Heat itself is much shorter, commonly lasting around 15 hours, with individual cows ranging from approximately 6 to 24 hours. Ovulation usually occurs about 24 to 32 hours after the onset of standing heat.   

Because ovulation occurs after standing heat begins, breeding too early or too late can reduce the chance of conception. This is why accurately identifying the beginning of standing heat is so important.

## The Most Reliable Sign: Standing to Be Mounted

The clearest sign of heat in a dairy cow is standing to be mounted. A cow in standing heat will remain still while another cow, heifer, or bull mounts her.

This is different from simply mounting another animal. A cow that rides other cows may be coming into heat, already in heat, or leaving heat. The cow being mounted is the one showing the strongest evidence of sexual receptivity.   

When you observe standing heat, record:

- The cow’s identification number
- The date and time
- Whether the cow stood once or multiple times
- Any additional signs, such as mucus or tailhead rubbing
- The planned breeding time

Standing behavior may be difficult to see in crowded pens, slippery areas, or during milking and feeding. A cow may also stand briefly and then move away before you notice her. For this reason, you should use standing heat as the primary sign but support it with regular observation and good records.

## Secondary Signs of Heat in Dairy Cows

Secondary signs do not confirm heat by themselves, but they can help you identify cows that need closer observation. A combination of several secondary signs is more useful than relying on only one.

### 1. Mounting or Attempting to Mount Other Cows

A cow approaching heat may attempt to mount other cows. She may place her head or front legs on another cow, jump partially onto her, or repeatedly follow cows that are already sexually active.

However, mounting behavior alone does not prove that the mounting cow is in standing heat. She may be entering heat or may have recently passed through it. Watch her closely to determine whether she eventually stands to be mounted.

### 2. Increased Restlessness and Activity

Cows in or approaching heat often move more than usual. You may notice a cow:

- Walking around while other cows are lying down
- Pacing along a fence
- Changing positions frequently
- Moving between groups
- Spending less time lying down
- Following other cows
- Appearing nervous or unsettled

Activity monitors and pedometers can help identify increases in movement, particularly when visual observation is difficult. Activity-based systems may also help detect cows with subtle or “silent” heat. 

### 3. More Vocalization

A cow in heat may bellow more often than normal. Her vocalizations may sound louder or more frequent, particularly when she is separated from other cows or trying to interact with them.

Bellowing should not be used alone to make a breeding decision. Cows may vocalize because of hunger, discomfort, separation, illness, or changes in their environment. Still, increased vocalization combined with restlessness and mounting activity is a useful warning that heat may be approaching.

### 4. Increased Interaction With Herd Mates

Cows in heat often become more socially active. They may:

- Sniff another cow’s vulva
- Nudge or butt other cows
- Rest their chin on another cow’s back or rump
- Follow cows closely
- Stand head-to-tail with other cows
- Gather into a small, sexually active group

Chin resting is sometimes a way for one cow to test whether another cow is receptive to mounting. Increased sniffing and close contact can occur before standing heat, so cows displaying these behaviors should be watched carefully.  

### 5. Clear Vaginal Mucus

Clear, stringy mucus is a common physical sign of estrus. You may see mucus:

- Hanging from the vulva
- Stretched between the vulva and the ground
- Smeared on the tail
- Dried on the rump, legs, or flanks
- Visible in the alley or where the cow was lying

The mucus may look clear, glossy, and similar to egg white. A small watery discharge may appear as the cow begins to approach heat, while thicker, more abundant mucus may be observed closer to standing heat.

Mucus is helpful, but it is not conclusive on its own. Vaginal discharge can also occur with reproductive disease, irritation, or other conditions. If the discharge is yellow, green, foul-smelling, thick, or accompanied by fever or poor appetite, contact your veterinarian.

### 6. A Red, Moist, or Swollen Vulva

Estrogen causes changes in the reproductive tract. During heat, the vulva may appear:

- More swollen than usual
- Red or pink
- Moist
- Smooth rather than dry and wrinkled

This sign can be difficult to assess from a distance and may vary between cows. It is most useful when combined with standing behavior, clear mucus, or increased activity.  

### 7. Rough or Missing Tailhead Hair

Repeated mounting can roughen the hair at the tailhead. The hair may stand up, appear brushed backward, or be rubbed away entirely.

You may also see:

- Missing hair over the tailhead
- Abraded skin
- Mud or manure on both flanks
- Dirt marks on the rump or sides
- Saliva on the back from chin resting

These signs suggest that mounting has occurred recently. They do not always prove that the cow is currently in heat, because the behavior may have happened several hours earlier.

### 8. Reduced Feed Intake or Rumination

Some cows in heat spend less time at the feed bunk and may eat less than usual. They may also show changes in rumination or lying behavior.

A small, temporary decrease in feed intake may occur during heat, but a major drop in appetite is not normal estrus behavior. Severe appetite loss, depression, fever, diarrhea, or a sudden milk-production decline may indicate illness and should be investigated.

### 9. Slight Changes in Milk Production

Some producers notice a temporary decline in milk yield when a cow is in heat. This may happen because the cow is more active, spends less time eating, or is distracted by other cows.

Milk production should never be used as the only heat indicator. A drop in milk may also result from mastitis, lameness, poor feed intake, heat stress, or digestive disease.

## Signs Before, During, and After Standing Heat

Heat detection becomes easier when you understand how signs change throughout the cycle.

### Before Standing Heat

A cow may:

- Become restless
- Bellow more frequently
- Sniff other cows
- Attempt to mount
- Rest her chin on other cows
- Show a moist or slightly swollen vulva
- Produce a small amount of clear mucus

At this stage, she may not stand to be mounted yet. Mark her for closer observation over the next several hours.

### During Standing Heat

The key behavior is standing to be mounted. Other possible signs include:

- Repeated mounting activity
- Increased vocalization
- Nervous or excited behavior
- More movement
- Clear, stretchy mucus
- A red, moist, swollen vulva
- Grouping with other sexually active cows

This is the stage that should guide breeding decisions, according to your farm’s reproductive protocol.

### After Standing Heat

A cow that has passed heat may still attempt to mount other cows, but she usually will not stand to be mounted. You may also see:

- Rough tailhead hair
- Dirty flanks
- Reduced activity compared with earlier
- Continued mucus
- A small bloody discharge one to three days later

Bloody mucus after heat generally indicates that the cow was in estrus earlier. It is not a reliable sign that she should be inseminated immediately. Instead, record the observation and monitor her expected return to heat approximately 17 to 24 days later.  

## When Is the Best Time to Observe Dairy Cows for Heat?

Cows do not show heat signs evenly throughout the day. They often express more mounting and standing activity during cooler periods, especially in the evening, overnight, and early morning.

Some guidance indicates that approximately 70% of standing events occur between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m., while fewer events occur during the warmest afternoon hours.  

For better heat detection:

1. Observe cows early in the morning.
2. Check them again in the late afternoon or evening.
3. Add a midday observation when possible.
4. Watch cows for at least 20 to 30 minutes during each dedicated check.
5. Avoid relying only on observations during feeding or milking.
6. Record every suspected heat, even when the cow is not bred.

When cattle are being moved, fed, or crowded into a holding area, mounting may be difficult to interpret. Cows may also show fewer signs on slippery concrete, in overcrowded pens, or when they are uncomfortable.

## How Heat Stress Affects Heat Detection

Heat stress can make heat detection significantly more difficult. During hot conditions, cows may stand in shade, bunch together, drink more, and move less. Research summarized by the University of Wisconsin indicates that standing events may be reduced by half during summer heat stress compared with winter conditions.  

To improve detection during hot weather:

- Provide shade and effective cooling.
- Use fans and sprinklers where appropriate.
- Check cows during cooler hours.
- Inspect tailhead paint or heat-detection patches regularly.
- Use activity monitors if practical.
- Pay closer attention to mucus, grouping, and subtle changes in behavior.
- Keep accurate records of previous heats.

Heat abatement supports both cow comfort and reproductive performance. If cows are not comfortable enough to walk and mount, visual heat detection becomes less reliable.

## Why Some Dairy Cows Show Weak or Silent Heat

Some cows ovulate without displaying obvious behavioral signs. This is commonly called silent heat, although “silent ovulation” may be a more accurate description.

Weak heat expression may be associated with:

- The first ovulation after calving
- Heat stress
- Lameness or sore feet
- Poor footing
- Crowding
- Low energy balance
- Illness
- Reproductive disorders
- Short periods of standing activity
- High milk production
- Heat occurring primarily at night

A lame cow may be physically unable or unwilling to mount and may not stand comfortably when another cow mounts her. Prompt attention to hoof health, flooring, nutrition, and cow comfort can improve heat expression and detection.

If a cow is not showing heat within the expected time after calving, or if she has irregular cycles, consult your veterinarian. Ovarian cysts, uterine disease, inactive ovaries, and other reproductive problems can interfere with normal estrus.

## Heat Detection Tools for Dairy Farms

Visual observation remains important, but detection aids can reduce missed heats.

### Tailhead Chalk or Paint

Apply chalk or paint over the tailhead. If mounting occurs, the marking becomes rubbed, faded, or missing.

This method is inexpensive, but rain, rubbing against gates, and other contact can cause false results. Check and refresh markings routinely.

### Heat Detection Patches

Adhesive patches may change color or reveal a bright surface when rubbed by mounting activity. They can be useful when cows are not observed continuously.

Patches can become loose or activate from contact with equipment, so always interpret them along with cow behavior.

### Activity Monitors

Pedometers, leg bands, collars, and other sensors can identify increases in movement. Some systems also monitor rumination, lying time, or feeding activity.

Technology is especially useful for large herds, nighttime heat expression, and cows with subtle visual signs. However, alerts should be reviewed by trained staff rather than accepted automatically.

### Pregnancy and Hormone Testing

Milk or blood progesterone testing may help confirm reproductive status in selected situations. These tests should be interpreted alongside breeding records and veterinary guidance. They do not replace a complete herd reproductive program.

## Timing Artificial Insemination After Heat

Many dairy farms use the traditional AM-PM guideline:

- If standing heat is observed in the morning, breed that evening.
- If standing heat is observed in the evening, breed the following morning.

This generally places insemination about 12 hours after the beginning of standing heat. Exact timing may vary based on semen type, farm protocol, synchronization program, and veterinary recommendations.

Ovulation typically occurs 24 to 32 hours after standing heat begins, so insemination is performed before ovulation.   

Do not breed a cow solely because she is mounting others, has a rough tailhead, or has clear mucus. Confirm standing heat whenever possible. If standing heat was missed but several strong secondary signs are present, follow your farm’s reproductive protocol or consult your veterinarian or breeding technician.

## A Simple Daily Heat Detection Checklist

Use this checklist during each observation period:

- Is any cow standing to be mounted?
- Is a cow repeatedly attempting to mount others?
- Are several cows grouped together?
- Is one cow more restless or active than her herd mates?
- Is she bellowing more than usual?
- Is she sniffing or nudging other cows?
- Is she resting her chin on another cow?
- Is clear mucus visible?
- Is the vulva red, moist, or swollen?
- Is the tailhead hair rough, rubbed, or missing?
- Are there mud or manure marks on both flanks?
- Is the cow eating or ruminating less?
- Has she shown heat at the expected 17- to 24-day interval?

Record your findings immediately. Good communication between milkers, herdspeople, managers, veterinarians, and breeding technicians prevents missed information.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the strongest sign of heat in a dairy cow?

The strongest and most reliable sign is standing to be mounted. The cow remains still while another cow, heifer, or bull mounts her.

### Is a cow in heat if she mounts another cow?

Not necessarily. Mounting another cow is a secondary sign. The mounting cow may be approaching heat or may have recently passed it. Watch for her to stand to be mounted before making a breeding decision.

### How long does heat last in dairy cows?

Heat averages about 15 hours but may last from approximately 6 to 24 hours. Because the window is short, cows should be observed more than once daily.

### How often does a dairy cow come into heat?

A normal estrous cycle averages about 21 days. A cycle between 17 and 24 days may still be normal, depending on the cow.

### What does clear mucus mean in a dairy cow?

Clear, stretchy mucus commonly occurs when a cow is approaching or experiencing heat. It is a useful secondary sign, but it should be evaluated with behavior and herd records.

### Is bloody mucus a sign that a cow is in heat?

Bloody mucus usually appears after heat, often one to three days later. It generally indicates that the cow was in heat earlier, so it is usually too late to breed based on that sign alone.

### Why is my cow not showing heat after calving?

Possible causes include normal postpartum anestrus, silent ovulation, poor body condition, heat stress, lameness, uterine disease, ovarian problems, or inadequate observation. Ask your veterinarian to evaluate cows with delayed or irregular cycling.

### When should I call a veterinarian?

Contact your veterinarian if a cow has foul-smelling discharge, fever, depression, severe appetite loss, persistent reproductive irregularities, repeated failure to conceive, or no apparent return to cycling after the expected postpartum period.

## Conclusion

Successful heat detection starts with knowing what normal estrus looks like. Standing to be mounted is the most dependable sign, but restlessness, increased activity, mounting attempts, clear mucus, a swollen vulva, vocalization, chin resting, grouping, and rubbed tailhead hair can help you identify cows before or after standing heat.

Observe cows during cooler hours, provide safe footing and good cow comfort, use detection aids when appropriate, and record every heat event. When you combine careful observation with accurate records and timely veterinary support, you give your dairy herd a stronger chance of improved conception, fewer missed breeding opportunities, and better long-term reproductive performance.
