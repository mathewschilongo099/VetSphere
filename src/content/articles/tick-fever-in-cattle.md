Tick fever in cattle can begin with signs that look mild: a cow stands apart from the herd, eats less, or produces less milk. Within a short time, however, the disease may progress to severe anemia, jaundice, weakness, nervous signs, collapse, and death.

The term “tick fever” commonly refers to bovine babesiosis and anaplasmosis, two important tick-borne diseases affecting cattle. In some regions, “tick fever” may also describe other infections carried by ticks, so accurate diagnosis is essential. Bovine babesiosis is caused by *Babesia bovis*, *Babesia bigemina*, and related parasites, while bovine anaplasmosis is usually caused by the blood-cell parasite *Anaplasma marginale*.   

If you suspect tick fever in your herd, contact a veterinarian immediately. Early treatment can make the difference between recovery and death.

## What Is Tick Fever in Cattle?

Tick fever is a group of serious blood-borne infections transmitted mainly through ticks. The organisms invade or damage red blood cells, reducing the blood’s ability to carry oxygen.

Bovine babesiosis is often called:

- Cattle fever
- Texas cattle fever
- Redwater
- Piroplasmosis
- Babesial fever

Anaplasmosis is sometimes included in the broader “tick fever” disease complex because it produces similar signs and may be spread by some of the same tick species.

In tropical and subtropical areas, cattle fever ticks such as *Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus* and *Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) annulatus* are important vectors of bovine babesiosis. These ticks may also transmit *Anaplasma marginale*.   

The disease is especially dangerous when cattle with no previous exposure are introduced to an area where infected ticks are established.

## What Causes Tick Fever?

### Bovine babesiosis

Babesiosis is caused by microscopic parasites of the genus *Babesia*. The parasites enter red blood cells, multiply, and eventually cause those cells to rupture. This process can result in:

- High fever
- Rapid anemia
- Jaundice
- Weakness
- Dark red or brown urine
- Reduced milk production
- Death in severe cases

*Babesia bovis* can also cause infected red blood cells to adhere to small blood vessels, including vessels in the brain. This may produce nervous signs such as incoordination, teeth grinding, abnormal behavior, tremors, or seizures.  

### Bovine anaplasmosis

Anaplasmosis is caused primarily by *Anaplasma marginale*, a bacterial blood parasite. It damages red blood cells and causes the body to remove both infected and some uninfected cells from circulation.

Unlike babesiosis, anaplasmosis usually does not cause red urine from intravascular red-cell rupture. The urine may become dark brown, but true hemoglobinuria is not usually present.  

Recovered cattle can remain carriers of *Anaplasma marginale* for life. These apparently healthy animals may still contribute to disease transmission when ticks or blood-contaminated equipment spread the organism. 

## How Does Tick Fever Spread?

Ticks are the primary biological vectors, but anaplasmosis can also spread mechanically.

Possible routes of transmission include:

- Bites from infected ticks
- Biting flies that move infected blood between animals
- Reusing needles between cattle
- Contaminated dehorning equipment
- Contaminated castration instruments
- Other procedures that transfer blood
- Rarely, transmission from cow to calf before birth

Cattle fever ticks can transmit *Babesia* from one generation of ticks to the next. This makes established tick populations difficult to eliminate once the disease becomes endemic.  

Transmission risk tends to increase when:

- Tick numbers rise
- Cattle are moved into tick-infested pastures
- Naive cattle are introduced into endemic areas
- Acaricide resistance reduces tick-control effectiveness
- Cattle are stressed, poorly nourished, or affected by another illness
- Routine livestock procedures are performed with contaminated equipment

## Tick Fever Symptoms in Cattle

Signs depend on the organism involved, the number of parasites, the animal’s age, breed, immunity, and overall health. Disease may develop rapidly, so check cattle carefully when several animals become unwell around the same time.

Common symptoms include:

- Fever, often higher than 104°F or 40°C
- Depression and lethargy
- Reduced appetite
- Decreased rumination
- Weakness or reluctance to walk
- Separation from the herd
- Weight loss
- Drop in milk production
- Rapid breathing
- Rapid or forceful heartbeat
- Pale, yellow, or orange mucous membranes
- Constipation
- Jaundice
- Abortion
- Dark urine
- Staggering or incoordination
- Tremors or unusual aggression
- Collapse
- Sudden death

A fever may be the first obvious sign, but severe anemia can develop quickly. Affected cattle may breathe harder because their blood cannot deliver enough oxygen to tissues.

### Signs more suggestive of babesiosis

Babesiosis is more likely when you see:

- Red, brown, or coffee-colored urine
- Severe fever
- Sudden weakness
- Jaundice
- Rapid anemia
- Nervous signs
- Collapse after being moved or driven

Red urine is often associated with babesiosis, but its absence does not rule it out. Not every infected animal develops obvious hemoglobinuria.

### Signs more suggestive of anaplasmosis

Anaplasmosis is more likely when you see:

- Progressive weakness
- Pale or yellow mucous membranes
- Falling milk production
- Weight loss
- Constipation
- Difficulty breathing after exercise
- A strong, rapid heartbeat
- Abortion
- Brown urine without obvious red blood cells

Older cattle often develop more severe disease than calves. Cattle younger than one year may become infected with few clinical signs, while adult cattle can become severely ill or die. 

## Is Tick Fever an Emergency?

Yes. Suspected tick fever should be treated as a veterinary emergency, particularly when cattle show fever, pale or yellow gums, dark urine, severe weakness, breathing difficulty, or nervous signs.

Do not force a sick animal to walk long distances. Cattle with severe anemia may collapse or die when stressed, transported, or driven through handling facilities.

Move the animal calmly only when necessary, provide access to water and shade, and contact your veterinarian. Separate visibly sick cattle when practical, but avoid excessive handling.

## How Is Tick Fever Diagnosed?

A veterinarian may begin with the animal’s history and physical examination. Important details include:

- Recent movement between farms or regions
- Exposure to cattle fever ticks
- Tick numbers on the animal
- Previous herd disease
- Vaccination history
- Age and breed of affected cattle
- Recent use of shared needles or equipment
- Seasonal timing of the outbreak
- Changes in milk production or abortions

Laboratory testing is usually needed because babesiosis and anaplasmosis can resemble other diseases, including theileriosis, leptospirosis, severe parasitism, toxic conditions, and other causes of anemia or jaundice.

### Common diagnostic tests

Your veterinarian may recommend:

- Giemsa-stained blood smears
- Complete blood count
- Packed cell volume or hematocrit
- PCR testing
- ELISA or other antibody tests
- Serology
- Blood chemistry testing
- Postmortem tissue examination in dead cattle

Blood smears can help identify *Babesia* or *Anaplasma* inside red blood cells. Capillary blood from the ear or tail tip may improve detection of *B. bovis* in live cattle. PCR can help identify the organism when parasite levels are low or microscopy is inconclusive.   

A negative blood smear does not always rule out infection, particularly in carrier animals or animals tested after treatment. Your veterinarian may recommend repeat testing or a combination of methods.

## Treatment for Tick Fever in Cattle

Treatment depends on the specific organism, the severity of the disease, local regulations, and the medicines approved for food-producing animals.

### Babesiosis treatment

Veterinarians may use babesiacidal medicines such as:

- Imidocarb dipropionate
- Diminazene aceturate

These drugs are not approved or available in every country. Dosage, route of administration, repeat treatment, and withholding periods must follow the product label and veterinary instructions.  

Treatment is most successful when started early. Once an animal has severe anemia, shock, or neurological disease, the prognosis becomes guarded.

### Anaplasmosis treatment

Tetracycline antibiotics and, in some regions, imidocarb may be used to treat anaplasmosis. Early treatment can improve survival, but severely anemic cattle may still require intensive supportive care. 

### Supportive care

Depending on the animal’s condition, supportive treatment may include:

- Fluids
- Anti-inflammatory medication
- Nutritional support
- Reduced handling and stress
- Blood transfusion
- Treatment for secondary complications
- Careful monitoring of body temperature, breathing, and hydration

Blood transfusion may be lifesaving when anemia is severe. However, it requires veterinary equipment, suitable donor blood, and careful management.

Never give leftover antibiotics or livestock medicines without veterinary direction. Incorrect treatment may fail to control the disease, create residues in meat or milk, or make laboratory diagnosis more difficult.

## What Happens After Recovery?

Cattle that survive babesiosis may remain carriers for months or years, depending on the species and circumstances. Recovered animals may look healthy while still harboring the organism.

Cattle that recover from anaplasmosis commonly become lifelong carriers. Carrier cattle can be important sources of infection when ticks or contaminated equipment spread blood between animals.  

After an outbreak, your veterinarian may recommend:

- Testing the remaining herd
- Identifying carrier cattle
- Monitoring packed cell volume
- Delaying stressful procedures
- Reviewing vaccination options
- Improving tick control
- Quarantining incoming cattle
- Testing replacement animals before purchase

## How to Prevent Tick Fever in Cattle

Prevention requires more than simply spraying cattle once. A successful program combines tick control, biosecurity, surveillance, and veterinary planning.

### 1. Control ticks strategically

Use only approved acaricides and follow label directions carefully. Under-dosing, incorrect application, and treating at ineffective intervals can contribute to acaricide resistance.

Inspect cattle regularly, especially around:

- Ears
- Under the tail
- Between the hind legs
- Udder skin folds
- Neck and shoulder areas
- Around the groin

The USDA recommends regular inspection and approved treatments for cattle, vegetation, and equipment where appropriate. 

### 2. Manage tick habitat

Reduce exposure by:

- Cutting back heavy brush and weeds
- Managing overgrown pasture edges
- Avoiding heavily infested grazing areas when possible
- Rotating pastures according to local veterinary advice
- Repairing fences to prevent contact with neighboring herds
- Monitoring wildlife that may carry ticks

Habitat management is not always enough to eliminate ticks, but it can reduce exposure.

### 3. Quarantine new cattle

Keep new arrivals separate from the established herd. During quarantine:

- Inspect them for ticks
- Treat them according to veterinary advice
- Review their health and vaccination records
- Test when appropriate
- Monitor body temperature and appetite
- Avoid sharing needles or equipment

Introducing older, susceptible cattle into an endemic area is a recognized risk factor for serious outbreaks. 

### 4. Prevent blood contamination

Use a new sterile needle for every animal whenever possible. Clean and disinfect instruments between animals, and establish strict hygiene procedures for:

- Vaccination
- Dehorning
- Castration
- Ear tagging
- Blood collection
- Surgery
- Reproductive procedures

This is especially important for controlling anaplasmosis, which can spread through contaminated equipment. 

### 5. Consider vaccination

Vaccines for babesiosis and anaplasmosis are available in some countries but not others. They may be live, attenuated products and require careful handling and veterinary supervision.

Vaccination programs should consider:

- Local disease prevalence
- Cattle age
- Breed
- Pregnancy status
- Regional regulations
- Vaccine availability
- Risk of reactions
- Whether cattle are being moved into an endemic area

Do not assume a vaccine used in one country protects against every strain in another region.

### 6. Monitor the herd

Keep records of:

- Fever cases
- Tick counts
- Milk production
- Abortions
- Treatments
- Deaths
- Cattle movement
- Laboratory results

Early recognition allows your veterinarian to test affected animals before the disease spreads or more cattle become critically ill.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can tick fever kill cattle?

Yes. Babesiosis and anaplasmosis can both be fatal, particularly in older cattle, previously unexposed cattle, and animals with severe anemia. *Babesia bovis* may cause serious neurological disease and death. 

### Is red urine always caused by tick fever?

No. Red or brown urine can occur with babesiosis, but other diseases and conditions may produce similar changes. Anaplasmosis usually causes dark brown urine rather than classic red urine. Laboratory testing is needed for confirmation.

### Can calves get tick fever?

Yes, calves can become infected. However, young cattle often develop milder disease than adults. This does not mean calves are unimportant; early infection may result in carriers, and calves can become seriously ill depending on the organism, immune status, and exposure level.

### Do recovered cattle remain infectious?

Often, yes. Cattle that recover from anaplasmosis can remain lifelong carriers. Cattle that recover from babesiosis may also carry the parasite for extended periods. Testing and herd management are important after recovery.  

### Can humans catch bovine tick fever?

Bovine anaplasmosis caused by *Anaplasma marginale* has not been reported to infect people. Some *Babesia* species can infect humans, although the species that commonly cause disease in cattle are not all important human pathogens. Avoid handling ticks or animal blood unnecessarily, and follow local public-health and veterinary guidance.  

### How quickly should treatment begin?

Treatment should begin as soon as a veterinarian suspects tick fever. Waiting for severe anemia, dark urine, collapse, or neurological signs can significantly worsen the prognosis.

## Conclusion

Tick fever in cattle is a serious, potentially fatal disease complex associated mainly with bovine babesiosis and anaplasmosis. Fever, weakness, pale or yellow mucous membranes, falling milk production, dark urine, breathing difficulty, and nervous signs should never be ignored.

The best protection combines regular tick surveillance, effective acaricide use, pasture and habitat management, quarantine of new cattle, sterile handling equipment, herd testing, and regionally appropriate vaccination. If an animal shows signs of tick fever, contact your veterinarian immediately and keep handling to a minimum. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment give your cattle the best chance of survival while helping protect the rest of your herd.
