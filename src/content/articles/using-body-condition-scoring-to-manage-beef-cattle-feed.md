## Look Past the Scale and Check the Fat

![several black and white cows grazing in a green pasture](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1566310203664-bb0828838943?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxjb3dzJTIwZ3JhemluZyUyMHBhc3R1cmV8ZW58MXwwfHx8MTc5NTI2OTk3N3ww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


Weighing your cattle on a scale doesn't always tell you the whole story about how well they are doing. Live weight can be markedly affected by gut fill and stage of pregnancy, making it easy to misjudge whether an animal is actually gaining or losing condition. A heavy-looking cow might just be carrying a heavy calf or a belly full of forage, while a thinner animal might be hidden behind a thick winter coat. Body condition scoring gives you a clearer picture by focusing on fat reserves rather than total weight. It costs nothing except a bit of time in the crush or paddock.

## Read the Nine-Point Scale

![several brown cattle standing close together with a yellow ear tag visible](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6339152/pexels-photo-6339152.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)


When you assess beef cattle body condition scores, you use a nine-point scale. Scores range from 1, which means the animal is extremely thin or emaciated, all the way up to 9, which describes an obese or very fat animal. Within this range, a body condition score of five represents average flesh. UNL Beef notes that a BCS 5 cow is in average flesh and serves as a logical target for most cow herds. Knowing this baseline helps you figure out which animals need more help and which are doing just fine on the current pasture.

## Check the Key Skeletal Areas

![several black and white and brown cows grazing on a green pasture under a cloudy sky](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1665105519765-a0ec8e027100?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5ODE1NjV8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwzfHxjYWx2ZXMlMjBpbiUyMGElMjBmaWVsZHxlbnwxfDB8fHwxNzk1MjY5OTgzfDA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)


To get an accurate score, you need to look at specific parts of the animal where fat is stored or lost first. Appraisers observe fat deposits over the backbone, ribs, hooks, pin bones, tailhead, and brisket. Don't just rely on a quick glance from across the yard. Combine visual appraisal with palpation of key bone structures for a more precise scoring technique. Put your hands on the animal to feel how much fat covers those sharp bones. A thin cow will have sharp, easily felt hip bones and ribs, while a well-conditioned animal will have smooth layers of fat masking those structures.

## Understand Weight Shifts Between Scores

Condition changes involve real shifts in tissue and mass. Weight differences often range from 70 to 140 pounds depending on the score and production cycle stage, as reported by Mississippi State University Extension. When a cow drops a score, she is losing a significant amount of weight and energy reserves. Recognising this helps you understand how much feed it will take to pull an animal back up to target condition before the next big production demand comes along.

## Sort Your Herd and Adjust the Feed

There is a strong correlation between a cow's body condition and her reproductive performance and productivity. Thin cows take longer to cycle after calving, and over-fat cows waste expensive feed and can run into trouble at birth. Sort cows and feed them according to their specific nutritional needs based on their condition score rather than treating the whole herd the same. Put the ones that need to gain condition into a separate group with better access to forage or supplement. Regulate feeding and management to ensure breeding cattle are in appropriate condition at different stages of the production cycle.

## What to Do This Week

Walk through your herd with the 1 to 9 scale in mind and handle a few animals to check their fat cover over the ribs and tailhead. Identify any cows sitting below your target condition and draft them into a separate group for extra feed adjustments.
