Vesicular Stomatitis Virus: What Livestock Owners Need to Know  

Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) is one of those diseases you hope you never see in your barn or pasture, but you absolutely need to recognize.  
It causes painful blisters in horses, cattle, and other livestock, can shut down animal movement, and looks almost identical to foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), a major foreign animal disease.   

If you own horses, cattle, pigs, camelids, sheep, or goats, this guide will walk you through what vesicular stomatitis virus is, how it spreads, what to watch for, and what you can do to protect your animals and your farm business.  

---

### What Is Vesicular Stomatitis Virus?  

Vesicular stomatitis virus is an enveloped RNA virus in the Rhabdoviridae family, genus Vesiculovirus.   
It causes a disease called vesicular stomatitis (VS), which is a reportable viral disease in large animals.   

Key points:  

- Main species affected:  
  - Horses, donkeys, and mules  
  - Cattle  
  - Pigs (less common)  
  - Camelids (llamas, alpacas), sheep, goats (occasional)  

- Geographic pattern:  
  - Confined to the Americas  
  - Endemic in parts of Mexico, Central America, and northern South America  
  - Causes sporadic outbreaks in the United States every 2–10 years, especially in western states during warm seasons  

- Zoonotic:  
  - Can infect people, usually causing mild flu-like illness   

Because its lesions look just like those caused by foot-and-mouth disease, any suspected VS case triggers regulatory attention, testing, and movement restrictions.  

---

### Why Vesicular Stomatitis Matters to Your Operation  

Even though many animals recover uneventfully, vesicular stomatitis virus creates serious practical problems for owners and producers:  

- **Pain and welfare issues**  
  - Blisters and ulcers in the mouth and on the feet are very painful.  
  - Affected animals often refuse feed and water.  

- **Production and performance losses**  
  - Decreased milk and meat production in cattle. 
  - Weight loss due to reduced feed intake.  
  - Lameness can sideline working and performance horses. 

- **Regulatory and economic impact**  
  - Reportable disease: veterinarians must notify state and federal officials.  
  - Quarantines and movement restrictions on affected premises.  
  - Trade and show cancellations; border closures in some circumstances. 

- **Public health considerations**  
  - Zoonotic transmission to people exposes staff, veterinarians, and family members to infection.   

Managing VS well is not just about getting your horse or cow through the acute illness; it’s about protecting your entire herd or stable and minimizing disruption to your business.

### How Vesicular Stomatitis Virus Spreads  

Understanding transmission is the foundation of prevention. VSV is both **vector-borne** and **contact-transmitted**.  

**1. Insect vectors (main route)**  

Biting insects are considered the primary way VSV moves between animals:  

- Documented or strongly suspected vectors include:  
  - Black flies (Simuliidae)  
  - Sand flies (Psychodidae)  
  - Culicoides biting midges (Ceratopogonidae)  

- Insects pick up virus from:  
  - Vesicular fluid  
  - Saliva  
  - Lesion exudate of infected animals  

- Seasonality:  
  - Outbreaks are strongly associated with warm months when biting fly populations are high.  

**2. Direct contact**  

- Nose-to-nose contact with infected animals  
- Contact with active vesicles, ruptured blisters, or saliva containing virus  

**3. Indirect contact (fomites)**  

Shared contaminated items can move virus around a farm or between groups:  

- Water troughs and automatic waterers  
- Feed bunks, lick tubs, and hay feeders  
- Halters, bits, lead ropes, nose tongs  
- Oral dosing equipment and dental floats  
- Milking equipment and teat cups  

**4. Transmission to people**  

Humans are typically exposed via:  

- Direct handling of infected animals  
- Contact with vesicular fluid or tissue  
- Laboratory exposure   

Person-to-person spread is not considered important, but basic hygiene and PPE are recommended when handling suspect animals.

### What Vesicular Stomatitis Looks Like in Animals  

The incubation period is usually 2–8 days after exposure.   
Most animals start with a short fever, then develop vesicles (blisters) that quickly rupture and ulcerate.  

**Common lesion sites**  

In horses and cattle, you’ll typically see lesions in:  

- Mouth and muzzle  
  - Lips, nostrils  
  - Tongue and gums  
  - Oral mucosa, especially the dental pad and tongue  

- Feet  
  - Coronary bands of the hooves  
  - Leading to coronitis, erosions, and lameness 

- Udder and teats  
  - Crusting, ulcers, pain during milking  
  - Risk of secondary mastitis in dairy cows  

- Other areas  
  - Ears, sheath, ventral abdomen, and sometimes the face  

**Typical clinical signs you might notice**  

- Drooling, slobbering, foamy saliva  
- Reluctance or refusal to eat and drink  
- Dropping feed from the mouth  
- Swelling, blisters, or raw ulcerated areas in and around the mouth  
- Crusts and sores on lips, nose, teats, sheath, or around the coronary band  
- Lameness, reluctance to walk  
- Mild fever early in the course, often gone by the time lesions are obvious    

In many cases, the actual blisters have already burst by the time you examine the animal, so you may only see erosions, ulcers, and scabs.

### How Dangerous Is Vesicular Stomatitis for Humans?  

Vesicular stomatitis is a zoonotic disease, but illness in humans is usually mild compared to animals.  

Reported human signs include:  

- Sudden onset of fever and chills  
- Headache and muscle aches  
- Fatigue, malaise, loss of appetite  
- Occasionally vesicular lesions in the mouth or on the nose   

Most human infections resolve without complications within 7–10 days.  

Those at highest risk:  

- Farmers and ranch workers handling infected animals  
- Veterinarians and technicians  
- Laboratory personnel working with the virus  

Basic precautions:  

- Wear gloves and, if possible, eye protection when handling suspect cases.  
- Wash hands thoroughly after contact.  
- Avoid eating, drinking, or smoking while working with infected animals.  

If you develop flu-like illness after working with a known or suspected VS herd, contact your physician and inform them of the exposure.

### Diagnosing Vesicular Stomatitis: Why You Must Call Your Veterinarian  

Vesicular stomatitis is **clinically indistinguishable** from:  

- Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD)  
- Swine vesicular disease  
- Vesicular exanthema of swine   

Because of the serious trade and regulatory implications of these diseases, **you cannot rely on appearance alone**. Laboratory confirmation is mandatory.  

What typically happens when VS is suspected:  

1. **Call your veterinarian immediately**  
   - Do not move animals on or off the property.  
   - Separate obviously affected animals if safe to do so.  

2. **Veterinarian exam and regulatory notification**  
   - Your vet is legally required to report suspect VS to state and federal animal health officials (e.g., State Veterinarian, USDA-APHIS).   

3. **Sample collection**  
   - Vesicular fluid or swabs from fresh lesions  
   - Tissue from vesicle epithelium  
   - Blood samples (serum)  

4. **Laboratory testing**  
   - PCR to detect viral RNA  
   - Virus isolation  
   - ELISA or other serologic tests   

5. **Quarantine and movement control**  
   - If VS is confirmed, premises are typically quarantined for at least 14 days after the last lesion is observed.  
   - Movement of horses and cattle may be restricted at the farm, county, or state level, depending on the outbreak.   

Prompt reporting is not just a legal duty; it helps shorten outbreaks and protect regional livestock industries.

### Treatment: What You Can Actually Do for Affected Animals  

There is no specific antiviral drug for vesicular stomatitis virus. Treatment is **supportive and symptomatic**, aimed at pain control, preventing secondary infections, and keeping animals eating and drinking.   

Core components of care:  

**1. Pain relief and anti-inflammatories**  

- Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) under veterinary direction:  
  - Reduce pain from mouth and foot lesions  
  - Help improve appetite and mobility 

**2. Supportive feeding strategies**  

- Offer:  
  - Soft pelleted feeds soaked into a mash  
  - Slurry or wet feeds that are easy to swallow  
  - Avoid coarse hay or sharp feeds that irritate oral ulcers 

- Monitor water intake closely:  
  - Provide clean, easily accessible water sources  
  - Check for dehydration, especially in severely affected animals  

**3. Local wound care**  

- Gentle rinsing of the mouth with antiseptic solutions may help reduce secondary bacterial infection. 
- Keep feet clean and dry where coronary band lesions are present.  

**4. Antibiotics (only when needed)**  

- Systemic antibiotics are **not** used to treat the virus itself, but may be prescribed if secondary bacterial infections (e.g., mastitis, foot infections) develop. 

**5. Rest and stress reduction**  

- Reduce handling and work demands.  
- Provide shade and good footing for lame animals.  

Most animals recover completely within a couple of weeks, though severe oral or foot lesions can prolong recovery.

### Practical Biosecurity and Vector Control on Your Farm  

Because vesicular stomatitis virus is spread mainly by biting insects and direct/indirect contact, your prevention efforts should focus on **vector control** and **farm biosecurity**.  

**1. Reduce insect exposure**  

- Stable management:  
  - Bring horses and high-value animals into barns or shelters during peak insect activity (dusk and dawn).  
  - Use fans in stalls to discourage flying insects.  

- Physical barriers:  
  - Fine-mesh screens where feasible.  
  - Fly masks and, when appropriate, fly sheets for horses.  

- Chemical control:  
  - Use veterinarian-approved insect repellents on horses and cattle.  
  - Implement integrated fly control (larvicides, adulticides, manure management). 

- Habitat management:  
  - Reduce standing water where black flies and midges breed.  
  - Manage manure and wet organic matter that supports insect populations. 

**2. Strengthen daily biosecurity**  

- Do not share:  
  - Buckets, troughs, hoses, or feed tubs between groups.  
  - Bits, halters, and grooming equipment at shows or events.  

- Clean and disinfect:  
  - Water and feed equipment regularly.  
  - Oral equipment (drench gun, dental float) between animals. 

- Manage new arrivals and returning animals:  
  - Quarantine newcomers for at least 7–14 days.  
  - Pay special attention to horses returning from events in regions with recent VS activity.  

**3. Isolation of sick animals**  

If you suspect VS in one or more animals:  

- Immediately separate clinically affected animals from unaffected groups if feasible.  
- Assign dedicated equipment and personnel to the sick group.  
- Handle healthy animals first, sick animals last.  

**4. Communication and compliance**  

- Stay informed about regional VS activity through state animal health agencies or equine disease communication networks. 
- Follow all movement restrictions, testing requirements, and quarantine orders during an outbreak.

### Vaccination: Is There a Shot for Vesicular Stomatitis?  

Vaccination options for vesicular stomatitis are limited and vary by country and region.  

- In some parts of Latin America (e.g., Colombia), inactivated bivalent vaccines (covering two VS virus serotypes) are used in cattle to reduce viral spread. 
- Studies have shown that commercial inactivated vaccines can induce protective immunity in cattle, but data are still limited and not universally applicable.  

In the United States, routine VS vaccination is **not** widely practiced in horses or cattle, and any vaccine use is subject to regulatory policy and availability.  

For most owners, the focus should remain on:  

- Surveillance and early recognition  
- Insect control  
- Strict biosecurity and movement management  

If you operate in an area with recurrent VS activity or in a country where vaccines are available, discuss vaccination with your veterinarian in the context of local regulations and risk.  

---

#### Key Takeaways for Managing Vesicular Stomatitis Virus  

- Vesicular stomatitis virus is a **vector-borne, zoonotic** virus that primarily affects horses and cattle but can involve multiple livestock species.  
- It causes painful **blisters and ulcers** on the mouth, teats, and feet, leading to reduced feed intake, lameness, and production losses.  
- VS is **clinically identical** to foot-and-mouth disease, so **laboratory testing and official reporting** are mandatory when vesicular lesions are seen.  
- There is **no specific antiviral treatment**; management centers on pain control, soft feed, prevention of secondary infections, and strict biosecurity.  
- Effective prevention depends on **biting insect control**, careful management of shared equipment and water sources, and rapid isolation of suspect cases.   

By recognizing vesicular stomatitis virus early, working closely with your veterinarian, and tightening your vector control and biosecurity practices, you can protect your animals, safeguard your farm’s operations, and help limit the spread of this important vesicular disease in your region.
