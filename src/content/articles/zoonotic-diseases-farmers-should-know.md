Working with livestock, poultry, horses, pets, wildlife, and animal products is rewarding—but it also comes with health risks. Some infections can pass naturally from animals to people. These illnesses are called **zoonotic diseases**, and they may spread through bites, scratches, manure, urine, birth fluids, contaminated food, water, dust, equipment, or animal housing.

Farmers and farm workers are often exposed repeatedly, which increases the importance of prevention. The good news is that practical steps such as handwashing, vaccination, pest control, personal protective equipment, and early veterinary care can significantly reduce the risk. The World Health Organization recognizes more than 200 zoonoses, including bacterial, viral, parasitic, and fungal diseases.  

This guide explains the most important zoonotic diseases farmers should know, how they spread, warning signs in animals and people, and how to protect your family, employees, herd, and customers.

## What Are Zoonotic Diseases?

A zoonotic disease is an infection that can naturally spread between vertebrate animals and people. Transmission may occur through:

- Direct contact with an animal
- Bites, scratches, or saliva
- Blood, urine, feces, milk, or respiratory secretions
- Birth fluids, placentas, and aborted tissues
- Contaminated feed, water, soil, bedding, clothing, or equipment
- Inhaling contaminated dust or aerosols
- Eating undercooked meat or unpasteurized dairy products
- Ticks, mosquitoes, fleas, and other vectors

You do not always need to handle a visibly sick animal to become infected. Some animals carry zoonotic pathogens without showing obvious signs. Q fever, for example, may be present in cattle, sheep, and goats that appear healthy, while bacteria can be shed in birth products, urine, feces, and milk. 

## Why Farmers Face Higher Risks

Farmers regularly perform tasks that can expose them to infectious organisms, including:

- Assisting with births
- Handling newborn animals
- Treating sick or injured livestock
- Cleaning stalls, barns, and poultry houses
- Managing manure and bedding
- Handling aborted fetuses or stillborn animals
- Butchering or processing animals
- Working around wildlife, rodents, and standing water
- Moving animals between farms, markets, fairs, and shows

Children, pregnant women, older adults, and people with weakened immune systems may develop more serious illness after exposure. Consider assigning higher-risk tasks to trained workers and keeping vulnerable household members away from birthing areas, sick animals, manure, and carcasses.

## Common Zoonotic Diseases Farmers Should Know

### 1. Brucellosis

Brucellosis is caused by *Brucella* bacteria. It affects cattle, bison, goats, sheep, pigs, dogs, and some wildlife species. People may become infected by contact with reproductive tissues and fluids, blood, milk, or animal carcasses. Eating unpasteurized dairy products is another important route of infection.

Farmers face the greatest risk when handling:

- Animals giving birth
- Aborted fetuses and placentas
- Uterine discharges
- Raw milk
- Infected tissues
- Live brucellosis vaccines

Symptoms in people may include fever, sweating, headache, weakness, back pain, joint pain, and fatigue. Illness can be prolonged or return after treatment. Because symptoms may resemble influenza or other common conditions, always tell your healthcare provider about livestock exposure.

To reduce the risk:

- Wear disposable gloves when handling birth products or abortions.
- Use eye protection and protective clothing when splashes are possible.
- Never consume raw milk or unpasteurized dairy products.
- Follow your veterinarian’s vaccination and testing recommendations.
- Use proper restraint when vaccinating animals to prevent needle-stick injuries.
- Contact your veterinarian and public health authorities if brucellosis is suspected.

The CDC identifies animal parturition, abortion cases, specimen collection, and live vaccine exposure as important occupational risks for veterinary and livestock workers. 

### 2. Q Fever

Q fever is caused by *Coxiella burnetii* and is especially associated with cattle, sheep, and goats. The bacteria are concentrated in birth products, including placentas and amniotic fluid, but may also be present in feces, urine, milk, and contaminated dust.

People commonly become infected by inhaling contaminated particles. Dust from barns, bedding, manure, wool, and birthing areas can carry the organism. Infected animals may look healthy, so relying only on visible illness is not enough.

Human symptoms may include:

- Fever
- Chills or sweats
- Severe headache
- Fatigue
- Muscle aches
- Nausea or vomiting
- Cough
- Pneumonia or hepatitis in more serious cases

Some people develop chronic Q fever, which can affect the heart valves or other organs. People with heart valve disease, vascular abnormalities, weakened immune systems, or pregnancy may need special medical advice after exposure.  

Prevent Q fever by:

- Wearing gloves, waterproof boots, eye protection, and coveralls during births.
- Using respiratory protection when dust or aerosols may be generated.
- Isolating animals with abortions or reproductive problems.
- Disposing of placentas, aborted fetuses, and contaminated bedding safely.
- Cleaning birthing areas carefully without creating unnecessary dust.
- Keeping pregnant household members away from animal birthing areas.
- Avoiding raw milk and unpasteurized dairy products.

### 3. Leptospirosis

Leptospirosis is caused by *Leptospira* bacteria, which can be carried by cattle, pigs, sheep, horses, dogs, wildlife, and rodents. Infected animals may shed the bacteria in urine. The organism can survive in wet soil, mud, standing water, and streams.

You may be exposed through cuts, scrapes, mucous membranes, contaminated water, or contact with infected urine and tissues. Flooding, wet pastures, poorly drained yards, and rodent activity can increase the risk.

Human symptoms often begin with:

- Fever
- Headache
- Chills
- Muscle pain
- Vomiting
- Severe tiredness
- Red or irritated eyes

Some infections progress to serious kidney or liver disease. Seek medical care if you develop a fever or flu-like illness after exposure to animal urine, muddy water, or flooded areas.

Farm prevention includes:

- Controlling rats and mice.
- Removing standing water when possible.
- Wearing waterproof gloves and boots.
- Covering cuts before beginning farm work.
- Avoiding untreated water where livestock congregate.
- Disinfecting contaminated equipment.
- Discussing livestock vaccination with your veterinarian.

### 4. Salmonella, E. coli, Campylobacter, and Cryptosporidium

These gastrointestinal infections are common concerns around livestock, poultry, calves, lambs, kids, reptiles, and contaminated farm environments. Young animals may shed organisms even when they appear active and healthy.

Transmission can occur when contaminated manure reaches:

- Hands
- Clothing
- Boots
- Tools
- Feed containers
- Water sources
- Vehicles
- Food preparation areas

Symptoms may include diarrhea, stomach cramps, nausea, vomiting, and fever. Some *E. coli* infections can cause severe complications, particularly in young children and older adults.

Protect your household and visitors by:

- Washing hands with soap and running water after animal contact.
- Keeping food, drinks, and tobacco products out of animal areas.
- Creating separate clean and dirty zones.
- Cleaning boots before entering the house.
- Washing work clothing separately from household laundry.
- Preventing children from sitting or playing in livestock pens.
- Keeping young ruminants away from kitchens and eating areas.
- Cleaning and disinfecting frequently touched surfaces.

Handwashing is considered one of the most important ways to reduce illness associated with animal contact. 

### 5. Listeriosis

Listeriosis is caused by *Listeria monocytogenes*. Cattle, sheep, and goats may be affected, particularly when feed quality is poor or silage is improperly fermented. The infection can spread to people through contaminated food, animal tissues, milk, and the farm environment.

Pregnant people are at particular risk because infection may cause miscarriage, stillbirth, or serious illness in a newborn. Older adults and people with weakened immune systems can also become severely ill.

Use these precautions:

- Do not consume unpasteurized milk or dairy products.
- Store feed properly and discard moldy or spoiled silage.
- Wear gloves when handling sick animals or contaminated material.
- Keep raw animal products away from ready-to-eat foods.
- Cook meat thoroughly.
- Wash hands after handling livestock, feed, or manure.

### 6. Rabies

Rabies is a fatal viral disease that affects mammals. On farms, exposure may involve bats, raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes, stray animals, or livestock bitten by wildlife.

The virus is usually transmitted through saliva, most often from a bite. It may also enter through scratches, open wounds, or mucous membranes. Once clinical signs develop, rabies is almost always fatal, so prevention and rapid medical evaluation are essential.

Protect your farm by:

- Vaccinating dogs, cats, horses, and other animals as recommended.
- Preventing livestock and pets from contacting wildlife.
- Avoiding animals that are acting strangely, unusually tame, aggressive, or disoriented.
- Never handling wildlife bare-handed.
- Reporting suspected rabies exposures promptly.
- Washing any bite or scratch immediately with soap and running water.
- Contacting a healthcare provider after a possible exposure.

Livestock that have been bitten or exposed to potentially rabid wildlife may require veterinary evaluation, observation, testing, or other official measures.

### 7. Avian Influenza

Avian influenza viruses primarily affect birds but may infect mammals, including people. Farm workers can be exposed through infected or dead poultry, dairy cattle, contaminated equipment, feces, milk, respiratory secretions, or other materials.

USDA advises farmers to maintain strong biosecurity because infectious material can move between farms on people, vehicles, equipment, and other items.  

Reduce the risk by:

- Avoiding contact with sick or dead birds and animals.
- Wearing appropriate respiratory and eye protection during high-risk tasks.
- Using dedicated boots and clothing in poultry or dairy areas.
- Disinfecting equipment and vehicles.
- Limiting visitors and unnecessary movement between animal groups.
- Keeping poultry away from wild birds and other livestock.
- Isolating sick animals and contacting your veterinarian.
- Reporting suspected infections to state or federal animal health officials.

If you develop eye redness, fever, cough, sore throat, shortness of breath, or other symptoms after exposure to infected animals, contact a healthcare provider promptly and explain the exposure.

### 8. Ringworm and Other Fungal Infections

Ringworm is a contagious fungal infection that can spread from cattle, horses, dogs, cats, and other animals to people. Despite its name, it is not caused by a worm.

It usually causes itchy, circular, scaly skin lesions. Infection can spread through direct contact or contaminated brushes, halters, bedding, fencing, and clothing.

Prevention includes:

- Wearing gloves when handling affected animals.
- Isolating animals with suspicious skin lesions.
- Disinfecting grooming tools and shared equipment.
- Washing hands after animal contact.
- Seeking veterinary treatment for affected animals.
- Seeing a healthcare provider if skin lesions develop.

### 9. Anthrax

Anthrax is an uncommon but serious bacterial disease associated with soil and infected livestock, particularly in areas where the disease occurs naturally. Animals may die suddenly, sometimes with bleeding from body openings.

Never open, butcher, move, or consume an animal that died suddenly from an unknown cause. Contact your veterinarian or animal health authorities immediately. Handling a carcass can expose you to spores through the skin, inhalation, or ingestion.

Vaccination may be recommended for livestock in areas where anthrax is a known risk.

## Farm Biosecurity: Your First Line of Defense

Biosecurity is a set of practices that prevents diseases from entering or spreading around your farm. A practical farm biosecurity plan should include:

### Control animal movement

- Quarantine new or returning animals.
- Buy livestock from reputable sources.
- Request health records and testing information.
- Avoid mixing animals from different farms unnecessarily.
- Separate sick animals quickly.

### Keep equipment clean

- Clean manure and organic matter before applying disinfectant.
- Use farm-specific boots, coveralls, and tools.
- Disinfect trailers, gates, buckets, needles, and handling equipment.
- Do not share equipment without cleaning it first.

### Manage wildlife and pests

- Secure feed in rodent-proof containers.
- Remove spilled grain.
- Repair holes in barns and poultry houses.
- Keep carcasses and waste away from livestock.
- Limit contact between domestic animals and wild birds or mammals.

### Maintain animal health

- Schedule routine veterinary examinations.
- Follow vaccination programs.
- Provide clean water and appropriate nutrition.
- Reduce overcrowding and stress.
- Record abortions, unexplained deaths, respiratory disease, diarrhea, and neurologic signs.

APHIS recommends keeping species separated, restricting unnecessary visitors, controlling rodents and wild animals, and maintaining clean, healthy livestock environments. 

## Personal Protective Equipment for Farm Work

The right personal protective equipment depends on the task. Useful items include:

- Waterproof gloves
- Heavy-duty work gloves
- Safety glasses or face shields
- Waterproof boots
- Coveralls
- Disposable aprons
- Respirators or masks when recommended
- Protective sleeves for birthing or veterinary procedures

PPE does not replace handwashing or good sanitation. Remove contaminated clothing before entering your home, wash reusable items properly, and dispose of single-use equipment safely.

## When to Call Your Veterinarian

Contact your veterinarian promptly if you notice:

- Several animals becoming sick at once
- Sudden unexplained deaths
- Abortions or stillbirths
- Severe diarrhea in young animals
- Neurologic behavior
- Unusual respiratory disease
- Swollen lymph nodes or unexplained weight loss
- Skin lesions spreading through a group
- Illness after introducing new animals
- Possible exposure to rabies, anthrax, brucellosis, or avian influenza

Do not move, sell, butcher, or give away potentially infected animals until you receive professional guidance. Some diseases are reportable, and early notification can protect neighboring farms and the wider community.

## When Should a Farm Worker See a Doctor?

Seek medical advice after an animal exposure if you develop:

- Fever
- Severe headache
- Muscle or joint pain
- Persistent cough or breathing difficulty
- Vomiting or diarrhea
- Yellowing of the skin or eyes
- Unusual rash or skin lesion
- Red eyes
- Swollen lymph nodes
- Confusion or neurological changes

Always tell your healthcare provider what animals you handle, whether you worked around births or abortions, and whether you had contact with manure, urine, wildlife, raw milk, carcasses, or contaminated water.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most important way to prevent zoonotic diseases on a farm?

Handwashing with soap and water after animal contact is one of the most important prevention measures. Combine it with proper PPE, animal vaccination, clean equipment, pest control, and prompt veterinary care.

### Can healthy-looking animals spread zoonotic diseases?

Yes. Some animals carry and shed pathogens without obvious signs of illness. This is especially important with diseases such as Q fever and certain intestinal infections.

### Should pregnant women help with animal births?

Pregnant women should avoid assisting with livestock births, abortions, and handling placentas or other reproductive tissues when possible. Diseases such as Q fever, brucellosis, and listeriosis can pose serious pregnancy risks.

### Is raw milk safe if it comes from your own healthy animals?

No. Healthy-looking animals can carry pathogens, and contamination can occur during milking, storage, or handling. Choose pasteurized milk and dairy products.

### What should I do after an animal bite?

Wash the wound immediately with soap and running water. Contact a healthcare provider and your veterinarian, especially if the animal is wild, stray, acting abnormally, or not vaccinated.

### Can farm dogs and cats spread zoonotic diseases?

Yes. Dogs and cats can carry diseases through bites, scratches, feces, urine, fleas, ticks, and contact with wildlife. Keep pets vaccinated, dewormed, and under veterinary care.

### Should I report a suspected zoonotic disease?

Contact your veterinarian first. Depending on the disease and location, your veterinarian may coordinate with state animal health officials, public health authorities, or USDA APHIS. Reporting can help prevent larger outbreaks.



## Conclusion

Zoonotic diseases are a real part of farm life, but they do not have to become a routine threat. The most effective approach is to recognize high-risk activities, use appropriate protective equipment, keep animals healthy, control rodents and wildlife, maintain clean facilities, and respond quickly to unusual illness.

Your veterinarian is an essential partner in building a farm-specific zoonotic disease prevention plan. By protecting animal health, worker safety, food hygiene, and the farm environment together, you protect your family, your employees, your customers, and the future of your operation.
